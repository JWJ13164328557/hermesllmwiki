# Dimensionality reduction for visualizing single-cell data using UMAP

---

## 1. Scientific Context

### Existing Consensus (Before This Paper)

By 2018, single-cell technologies — particularly mass cytometry (CyTOF) and single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) — had matured to enable high-dimensional profiling of thousands to millions of individual cells. However, interpreting these massive, high-dimensional datasets posed a fundamental visualization challenge. Key prior consensus:

- **t-SNE was the dominant tool**: [[xr-10.1080/15384101.2015.1128591|t-distributed Stochastic Neighbor Embedding]] (van der Maaten & Hinton, 2008) was the de facto standard for single-cell visualization, used in nearly every scRNA-seq and CyTOF publication
- **PCA was insufficient**: Principal component analysis, while fast and deterministic, captured only linear relationships and often failed to resolve biologically meaningful subpopulations in nonlinear single-cell data
- **Multiple dimensionality-reduction methods existed**: Diffusion maps, Isomap, lle, and ForceAtlas2 were available but had seen limited adoption in single-cell biology due to computational cost, hyperparameter sensitivity, or poor scalability
- **t-SNE had known limitations**: (1) stochastic results with poor reproducibility across runs, (2) loss of global structure — distances between clusters were not meaningful, (3) slow runtime on large datasets (O(N²) or O(N log N) with approximations), (4) tendency to fragment continuous trajectories
- **UMAP existed but was untested in biology**: McInnes, Healy, and Melville had published the UMAP algorithm on arXiv in 2018 as a general-purpose manifold learning technique, but it had not been applied to biological data

### Existing Models

- **t-SNE**: Converts high-dimensional pairwise similarities into a low-dimensional embedding by minimizing KL divergence between probability distributions in high and low dimensions. Strengths: excellent local structure preservation. Weaknesses: no global structure, stochastic, slow
- **PCA**: Linear projection onto principal components maximizing variance. Strengths: fast, deterministic, preserves global distances. Weaknesses: cannot capture nonlinear manifolds
- **Diffusion maps**: Constructs a Markov transition matrix and embeds cells using top eigenvectors. Strengths: preserves diffusion distances, captures developmental trajectories. Weaknesses: computationally intensive, sensitive to noise
- **ForceAtlas2**: Force-directed graph layout algorithm. Strengths: fast for network data. Weaknesses: not designed for high-dimensional point clouds
- **Isomap / lle**: Geodesic-distance-based or local-linear embedding. Strengths: can unfold curved manifolds. Weaknesses: sensitive to parameter choices, poor scalability

### Knowledge Gaps

- No systematic, quantitative comparison of dimensionality-reduction methods on real biological data
- No assessment of whether newer manifold learning approaches (like UMAP) could address t-SNE's reproducibility and speed limitations
- No benchmarking of runtime, reproducibility, and biological meaningfulness in a unified framework
- No evaluation of how well each method preserves rare cell populations — a critical concern in single-cell studies
- No principled guidance for single-cell researchers choosing among the proliferating dimensionality-reduction options

### Why This Paper Matters

This paper introduced UMAP to single-cell biology and provided the first rigorous, multi-method comparison of dimensionality-reduction tools on real biological datasets. By demonstrating that UMAP was simultaneously faster, more reproducible, and more biologically meaningful than t-SNE and four other methods, it triggered a rapid field-wide shift from t-SNE to UMAP. Within two years, UMAP became the standard visualization method in virtually every single-cell publication. The paper also established benchmarking methodology for visualization tools in single-cell genomics, including metrics for runtime, cluster organization, reproducibility, and rare-population preservation.

## 2. Research Questions

### Primary Question

Does UMAP (Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection) outperform existing dimensionality-reduction methods — particularly t-SNE — for visualizing single-cell data in terms of speed, reproducibility, and biological meaningfulness?

### Secondary Questions

1. Does UMAP preserve more meaningful biological structure (cell-type clusters, developmental trajectories, rare subpopulations) than t-SNE, PCA, diffusion maps, Isomap, and ForceAtlas2?
2. Is UMAP faster than t-SNE on large single-cell datasets (mass cytometry and scRNA-seq)?
3. Is UMAP more reproducible across independent runs than stochastic methods like t-SNE?
4. Does UMAP better preserve global relationships between cell clusters, enabling meaningful distance comparisons?
5. Can UMAP effectively visualize both mass cytometry (~30–50 parameters) and scRNA-seq (~thousands of genes) data?
6. Does UMAP accurately recover rare cell populations that might be lost in t-SNE embeddings?

### Explicit Hypotheses

- UMAP's mathematical foundation (Riemannian geometry + fuzzy simplicial sets) will provide a better balance of local and global structure preservation than t-SNE's KL-divergence optimization
- UMAP's stochastic gradient descent with negative sampling will scale more efficiently than t-SNE's Barnes-Hut or FFT-accelerated gradient computations
- UMAP's deterministic initialization options will yield higher reproducibility than t-SNE's random initialization

### Implicit Hypotheses

- The single-cell community's reliance on t-SNE is driven by habit and lack of systematic comparison, not by t-SNE's objective superiority
- The biological signal in single-cell data lies on a low-dimensional manifold that UMAP's topological assumptions can capture
- Speed and reproducibility are practically as important as embedding quality for routine single-cell analysis

- **Bone marrow (CyTOF)**: A larger, more complex dataset with continuous hematopoietic differentiation; tests ability to capture developmental trajectories

- **Diffusion maps**: Manifold learning method designed for continuous trajectories
- **Isomap**: Geodesic-distance-based method for curved manifolds
- **ForceAtlas2**: Network-layout method used occasionally in single-cell analysis

## 4. Method Analysis

### Core Algorithm: UMAP (Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection)

**Problem formulation**: Given a high-dimensional dataset $X = \{x_1, \ldots, x_N\} \subset \mathbb{R}^D$, find a low-dimensional embedding $Y = \{y_1, \ldots, y_N\} \subset \mathbb{R}^d$ (typically $d = 2$ or $3$) that preserves the topological structure of $X$ as faithfully as possible.

**Algorithm overview**:

#### Step 1: Construct Weighted k-Nearest Neighbor Graph

For each data point $x_i$, identify its $k$ nearest neighbors using a chosen distance metric (default: Euclidean). Construct a weighted directed graph where edge weights reflect the fuzzy simplicial set membership strength:

$$w_{ij} = \exp\left(-\frac{d(x_i, x_j) - \rho_i}{\sigma_i}\right)$$

where $\rho_i$ is the distance to the nearest neighbor of $x_i$ (local connectivity constraint) and $\sigma_i$ is a local scaling factor chosen to achieve a target local connectivity.

- **Innovation**: The adaptive $\sigma_i$ normalizes distances locally, handling varying data density. The $\rho_i$ parameter ensures every point is connected to at least one neighbor, preventing isolated points.

#### Step 2: Symmetrization — Fuzzy Simplicial Set

Convert the directed graph to an undirected, weighted adjacency matrix via fuzzy union:

$$\mu_{ij} = w_{ij} + w_{ji} - w_{ij} \cdot w_{ji}$$

This operation has a topological interpretation: the fuzzy union of fuzzy simplicial sets. The resulting graph represents the 1-skeleton of the fuzzy simplicial complex approximating the manifold.

- **Innovation**: This is fundamentally different from t-SNE's symmetrization ($p_{ij} = \frac{p_{i|j} + p_{j|i}}{2N}$). UMAP's fuzzy union preserves set-theoretic semantics from the topological framework.

#### Step 3: Low-Dimensional Embedding Optimization

Initialize the low-dimensional embedding $Y$ (can use spectral embedding for deterministic results or random initialization for stochastic results). Optimize a cross-entropy loss function that attracts connected points and repels disconnected points:

$$\mathcal{L}(X, Y) = \sum_{i \neq j} \left[ \mu_{ij} \log\left(\frac{\mu_{ij}}{\nu_{ij}}\right) + (1 - \mu_{ij}) \log\left(\frac{1 - \mu_{ij}}{1 - \nu_{ij}}\right) \right]$$

where $\nu_{ij} = \left(1 + a \|y_i - y_j\|^{2b}\right)^{-1}$ is a smooth approximation of membership strength in the low-dimensional space (parameters $a$ and $b$ are fitted to a curve).

Optimization uses stochastic gradient descent with **negative sampling**: for each edge, sample $m$ random non-neighbors as negative examples, dramatically reducing the computational cost from $O(N^2)$ to $O(kN)$.

- **Innovation**: Unlike t-SNE which minimizes only KL divergence from high-to-low-dimensional distributions (penalizing failure to separate distant points in the embedding), UMAP uses full **cross-entropy**, which also penalizes failure to bring together points that should be close. This provides a better balance of local and global structure.

#### Step 4: Key Parameters

| Parameter | Description | Typical Value | Effect |
|-----------|-------------|---------------|--------|
| `n_neighbors` | Size of local neighborhood | 15 | Larger values preserve more global structure |
| `min_dist` | Minimum distance between points in embedding | 0.1 | Controls clustering tightness |
| `n_components` | Embedding dimensionality | 2 | 2D for visualization |
| `metric` | Distance metric | Euclidean | Can use cosine, correlation, etc. |

### Key Algorithmic Contributions

| Innovation | Significance |
|---|---|
| **Riemannian geometry framework** | UMAP is grounded in rigorous topological theory — the algorithm approximates the manifold's fuzzy simplicial set representation. Contrasts with t-SNE's more ad hoc probabilistic formulation |
| **Cross-entropy loss function** | Balances attractive and repulsive forces better than t-SNE's asymmetric KL divergence, yielding better global structure preservation |
| **Negative sampling for SGD** | Reduces per-epoch complexity from $O(N^2)$ to $O(kN)$, enabling fast optimization on large datasets |
| **Adaptive local scaling ($\sigma_i$)** | Handles varying density across the manifold — points in dense regions and sparse regions both contribute meaningfully |
| **Deterministic initialization option** | Spectral initialization enables fully reproducible embeddings, addressing a key t-SNE limitation |
| **Fuzzy simplicial set construction** | Provides a principled, category-theoretic approach to building the high-dimensional neighborhood graph |

### Comparison with t-SNE

| Aspect | UMAP | t-SNE |
|--------|------|-------|
| **Theoretical foundation** | Riemannian geometry + fuzzy simplicial sets | Probability distributions + KL divergence |
| **Loss function** | Cross-entropy (symmetric) | KL divergence (asymmetric) |
| **Global structure** | Preserved (cross-entropy penalizes both false positives and false negatives) | Poor (KL asymmetry ignores false negatives) |
| **Runtime scaling** | $O(kN)$ with negative sampling | $O(N \log N)$ with Barnes-Hut; $O(kN)$ with FFT |
| **Reproducibility** | Deterministic with spectral init | Stochastic (different random seeds yield different embeddings) |
| **Large datasets** | Efficient with large $N$ | Degrades or requires heavy approximation |
| **Parameter sensitivity** | Moderate — `n_neighbors` and `min_dist` are interpretable | High — perplexity strongly affects results |

### Limitations of the Algorithm

- **$k$-nearest neighbor graph construction** remains $O(N^2)$ in naive implementations; approximate nearest neighbor methods (Annoy, NNDescent) are required for very large datasets
- **No explicit handling of batch effects**: UMAP treats all input points as if from the same manifold — batch-correction must be performed upstream
- **The fuzzy simplicial set construction** has limited mathematical guarantees about manifold fidelity — the theoretical framework is more aspirational than rigorously proven
- **The embedding is not unique**: even with deterministic initialization, small numerical differences can propagate
- **Density information is normalized away**: by design, UMAP does not reflect the local density of the original data in the embedding, which can mask biologically meaningful differences in cell-state abundance

## 7. Critical Evaluation

### Strengths

- **Rigorous systematic comparison**: Six methods evaluated on three well-characterized real datasets with multiple quantitative metrics — far more comprehensive than typical method introductions
- **Practically impactful metrics**: Runtime and reproducibility are metrics that directly affect daily research workflow — not merely aesthetic concerns
- **Cross-modality validation**: Demonstration on both CyTOF (protein) and scRNA-seq (RNA) data confirms general applicability beyond a single technology
- **Rare population preservation**: Addresses a critical practical need — the ability to detect and visualize rare cell types that can be obscured by dominant populations in t-SNE
- **Clear, actionable guidance**: Provides parameter recommendations and practical advice for adopting UMAP in single-cell workflows
- **Timing and execution**: Published at a moment when the field was acutely aware of t-SNE's limitations and actively seeking alternatives
- **Open-source implementation**: UMAP Python/R packages were already available and well-documented, enabling immediate adoption
- **Triggered field-wide transformation**: The paper catalyzed a near-complete transition from t-SNE to UMAP in single-cell genomics within ~2 years

### Weaknesses

- **No rigorous mathematical validation**: The paper demonstrates empirical superiority but does not provide theoretical proofs that UMAP's topological framework yields provably better embeddings
- **No negative control datasets**: Does not test UMAP on data with known ground-truth manifold structure (e.g., synthetic manifolds) where embedding fidelity could be quantitatively measured
- **Dataset scope is limited**: Three datasets from one lab's collaborations — broader validation across more diverse biological contexts would strengthen claims
- **Hyperparameter exploration is limited**: Only default UMAP parameters are used; no systematic sensitivity analysis across $k$ and `min_dist` values
- **No integration benchmark**: Does not test whether UMAP can visualize data integrated across batches or conditions — an increasingly common use case
- **Rare population claim is qualitative**: No quantitative metric for rare-population preservation; visual inspection of a few known examples
- **Comparison methods may be suboptimally tuned**: t-SNE results are highly sensitive to perplexity and iteration count — not clear if all methods received equal optimization effort
- **Authorship includes UMAP creators**: McInnes and Healy (UMAP algorithm authors) are co-authors, introducing potential confirmation bias in method selection and evaluation framing

### Missing Controls

- No comparison with UMAP variants (e.g., different distance metrics, different $k$ values) to assess robustness
- No assessment of UMAP's sensitivity to preprocessing choices (normalization, feature selection, scaling)
- No benchmark on simulated data with known cluster structure and trajectory topology
- No evaluation of computational resource usage beyond runtime (memory, disk I/O)
- No test of UMAP on integrated or batch-corrected data (CCA-aligned datasets becoming common at the time)

## 8. Research Insight

### What Changed After This Paper

- **UMAP replaced t-SNE as the standard**: Within 1–2 years, UMAP became the default visualization in virtually every single-cell paper (scRNA-seq, scATAC-seq, CyTOF, CITE-seq). t-SNE usage plummeted
- **UMAP became a general-purpose single-cell tool**: Beyond visualization, UMAP was adopted as a preprocessing step for clustering ([[xr-10-1038-nbt-4314|Louvain/Leiden on UMAP graphs]]), trajectory inference, and data integration quality assessment
- **Manifold learning theory gained traction**: The paper's emphasis on Riemannian geometry and topology attracted mathematical biologists to the single-cell field, spurring development of more theoretically grounded methods
- **Reproducibility became a recognized metric**: The paper's focus on embedding reproducibility across runs elevated reproducibility as a criterion for evaluating computational methods in single-cell biology
- **Visualization benchmarking was established**: Subsequent methods (PHATE [[xr-10.1038/s41587-019-0336-3]], PaCMAP, densMAP) were expected to benchmark against UMAP using the framework established here
- **The scanpy ecosystem adopted UMAP**: scanpy ([[xr-10.1186/s13059-017-1382-0]]) integrated UMAP as its default visualization, cementing the scanpy → UMAP pipeline that dominates Python-based single-cell analysis
- **Seurat adopted UMAP**: Seurat ([[xr-10-1038-nbt-4096]]) adopted UMAP for visualization in v3, making it the standard in the R ecosystem as well

### How It Connects to Current Knowledge

- UMAP is now **the standard visualization in single-cell atlases** (Human Cell Atlas, Tabula Sapiens, Fly Cell Atlas) — billions of cells visualized with UMAP
- The paper's comparison methodology has been extended by **formal benchmarking frameworks** like scIB and IBRAP that quantitatively evaluate integration + visualization quality
- **UMAP variants** (densMAP for density preservation, scVI-UMAP for probabilistic embeddings) extend the core algorithm while acknowledging the Becht et al. foundation
- UMAP's topological framework inspired **topological data analysis (TDA)** applications in single-cell biology, including Mapper-based trajectory analysis
- The paper demonstrates that **method comparison papers** — not just method invention papers — can have enormous field-wide impact
- UMAP's adoption exemplifies the **rapid community-driven standardization** characteristic of the single-cell genomics field

## 9. Future Research Opportunities

- **Density-preserving UMAP**: Extensions (like densMAP) that preserve local density information in the embedding, enabling visualization of cell-state abundance alongside cell-state identity
- **Supervised UMAP**: Incorporate known cell-type labels or experimental conditions to guide embedding, producing visualizations that highlight specific biological contrasts
- **Multi-omics UMAP**: Joint embeddings that simultaneously visualize cells from scRNA-seq, scATAC-seq, CITE-seq, and spatial transcriptomics in a shared UMAP space
- **Dynamic UMAP**: Visualizations that smoothly update as new data is added (streaming UMAP), enabling real-time atlas expansion without full recomputation
- **Uncertainty-aware UMAP**: Probabilistic UMAP embeddings that show per-cell or per-region uncertainty, indicating where the embedding may be unreliable
- **Hierarchical UMAP**: Multi-resolution UMAP that reveals structure at multiple scales — from coarse tissue-level organization to fine subpopulation structure
- **3D + interactive UMAP**: Better tools for interactive exploration of 3D UMAP embeddings, particularly for atlases with millions of cells
- **UMAP for non-Euclidean data**: Extend UMAP to handle phylogenetic trees, sequence alignments, or other structured data types common in genomics
- **Automated parameter selection**: Data-driven methods for choosing $k$ and `min_dist` based on dataset characteristics rather than user heuristics
- **UMAP-based differential abundance testing**: Statistical frameworks for identifying cell populations that differ between conditions using UMAP density estimates

# Normalization and variance stabilization of single-cell RNA-seq data using regularized negative binomial regression

---

## 1. Scientific Context

### Existing Consensus (Before This Paper)

By 2019, single-cell RNA-seq (scRNA-seq) with Unique Molecular Identifiers (UMIs) had become the dominant technology for profiling cellular heterogeneity. However, normalization of scRNA-seq data lagged significantly behind the experimental advances. Key prior consensus:

- **UMI counts are fundamentally different from bulk RNA-seq reads**: UMIs produce integer molecular counts without amplification bias within a cell, but with extreme sparsity (typically >90% zeros) and enormous dynamic range across cells
- **Standard normalization was log-transformation**: The dominant approach was to divide UMI counts by total counts per cell, multiply by a scale factor (e.g., 10,000), add a **pseudocount** (typically 1), and then **log-transform** — i.e., $\log(1 + 10,000 \times \text{count}_i / \text{total})$. This was used in Seurat v2 ([[xr-10-1038-nbt-4096|Butler 2018]]) and Scanpy ([[xr-10.1186-s13059-017-1382-0|Wolf 2018]])
- **The pseudocount was arbitrary**: The choice of pseudocount (+1) was a heuristic with no statistical justification. It had a disproportionate effect on lowly expressed genes and could distort biological variation
- **Log-normalization does not variance-stabilize UMI counts**: The variance of log-transformed UMI counts still depends on the mean — genes at different expression levels have different variances after transformation, misleading PCA and clustering
- **Alternative normalizations existed but were niche**: scran pooling-based size factors ([[xr-10-1186-s13059-016-0947-7|Lun 2016]]), SCnorm quantile regression ([[xr-10-1038-nmeth-4263|Bacher 2017]]), and Linnorm (Yip 2017) addressed some shortcomings but had limited adoption
- **Seurat v2 normalization workflow**: Log-normalize → identify highly variable genes (HVGs) using mean-variance relationship → scale/center → PCA → clustering. This pipeline was widely used but the HVG selection was confounded by the mean-variance relationship inherent to log-transformed data
- **Batch correction was done post-normalization**: Methods like CCA (Seurat v2), MNN (Haghverdi 2018), and Harmony ([[xr-10-1038-s41592-019-0619-0|Korsunsky 2019]]) operated on log-normalized data, potentially propagating normalization artifacts

### Existing Models

- **Log-normalization + HVG selection (Seurat v2)**: $\log(1 + \text{TP10K})$ → bin genes by mean → select top dispersion within each bin. This worked but the binning was ad hoc and the log transform did not truly decouple mean and variance
- **scran pooling**: Pool cells with similar library sizes, estimate size factors from summed counts, deconvolve cell-specific factors. Principled but requires careful pooling design and fails for very sparse data
- **SCnorm**: Quantile regression to estimate the mean-count relationship for each expression quantile; selects a quantile where the relationship is flat. Computationally expensive and sensitive to the chosen quantile
- **BASiCS**: Bayesian hierarchical model with spike-in-based normalization — principled but requires spike-ins and MCMC sampling, impractical for large datasets

### Knowledge Gaps

- No normalization method **simultaneously** achieved: (1) removal of the technical effect of sequencing depth, (2) variance stabilization (decoupling mean and variance), and (3) preservation of biological heterogeneity — all without heuristic parameters like pseudocounts
- No systematic framework for determining whether the remaining variation after normalization contains meaningful biological signal or residual technical noise
- No principled connection between normalization and HVG selection — the two steps were treated as independent, but the choice of normalization directly affected which genes were selected as variable
- The negative binomial distribution was known to describe UMI counts well ([[xr-10-1186-s13059-014-0550-8|DESeq2]], edgeR for bulk; [[xr-10-1186-s13059-018-1404-5|Townes 2019]] for single-cell), but had not been used to construct a normalization procedure that outputs continuous, variance-stabilized values
- **Overfitting risk in single-cell NB models**: Fitting a per-gene NB GLM with tens of thousands of cells could produce unstable parameter estimates — no regularization strategy had been proposed

### Why This Paper Matters

This paper introduced **sctransform**, a normalization and variance-stabilization method for UMI-based scRNA-seq data based on **regularized negative binomial regression**. It was the first method to frame normalization as a regression problem — modeling UMI counts as a function of sequencing depth using a NB GLM, and using the **Pearson residuals** as normalized, variance-stabilized values. The key insight was that **pooling information across genes with similar abundances** regularizes the NB parameter estimates, preventing overfitting. sctransform eliminated the need for pseudocounts, log-transformation, and ad hoc HVG selection — replacing them with a single, principled statistical framework. It became the default normalization method for Seurat v3 and v4 ([[xr-10-1016-j-cell-2019-05-031|Stuart 2019]], [[xr-10-1016-j-cell-2021-04-048|Hao 2021]]), used in thousands of scRNA-seq studies.

## 2. Research Questions

### Primary Question

Can regularized negative binomial regression of UMI counts against sequencing depth produce Pearson residuals that: (1) remove technical variation due to sequencing depth, (2) stabilize variance across the expression range, and (3) preserve biological heterogeneity — without heuristic parameters like pseudocounts?

### Secondary Questions

1. Does an unconstrained NB model overfit scRNA-seq data, producing unstable parameter estimates? If so, does pooling information across genes with similar abundances provide effective regularization?
2. Do Pearson residuals from the regularized NB model outperform log-normalized data for highly variable gene (HVG) selection — identifying genes whose variation is driven by biology rather than the mean-variance relationship?
3. Do Pearson residuals improve downstream dimensionality reduction (PCA) compared to log-normalized data — producing embeddings where biological structure is more clearly resolved?
4. Do Pearson residuals improve differential expression analysis compared to standard approaches (Wilcoxon test on log-normalized data, MAST, DESeq2 on pseudobulk)?
5. Does sctransform scale to large datasets (tens to hundreds of thousands of cells) and integrate seamlessly with the Seurat workflow?
6. Can sctransform effectively normalize data from different scRNA-seq technologies and protocols?

### Explicit Hypotheses

- UMI counts for each gene, across cells, follow a negative binomial distribution with mean determined by cellular sequencing depth — that is, the relationship between expected counts and depth is approximately linear on the log scale
- The NB overdispersion parameter varies systematically with gene abundance — lowly expressed genes are more overdispersed (closer to Poisson, where variance ≈ mean) than highly expressed genes
- Fitting an NB GLM with only an intercept and log(UMI total) as a covariate will capture the technical component of variation; the residuals will represent biological variation plus sampling noise
- The Pearson residual $r_{ij} = (k_{ij} - \mu_{ij}) / \sqrt{\mu_{ij} + \alpha_i \mu_{ij}^2}$ will be approximately mean-zero, unit-variance, and independent of sequencing depth — achieving variance stabilization
- Pooling genes into $K$ abundance bins and fitting a regularized NB model per gene (with bin-level prior on overdispersion) will yield more stable parameter estimates than gene-wise MLE

### Implicit Hypotheses

- The negative binomial distribution is an adequate model for UMI counts without zero-inflation — i.e., the excess zeros in scRNA-seq data are explained by low mean expression and overdispersion, not by a separate zero-generating process
- Sequencing depth (total UMI count per cell) is the dominant technical factor driving variation in per-gene UMI counts across cells
- Genes with similar mean abundance share similar overdispersion characteristics — justifying the pooling regularization strategy
- Biological variation is reflected in larger-than-expected residuals (given the NB model), enabling residual-based HVG selection
- Pearson residuals are approximately normally distributed after regularization, making them suitable as input to PCA and other methods that assume normality

### Why Negative Binomial Regression?

- UMI counts are integer-valued, overdispersed relative to Poisson, and have a mean-variance relationship — the NB distribution captures all three properties
- Framing normalization as a **regression problem** is conceptually clean: sequencing depth is a covariate whose effect we want to remove; biological variation is what remains in the residuals
- Unlike transformation-based methods (log, sqrt, asinh), NB regression explicitly models the count-generating process, making the residuals interpretable — a residual of +3 means the observed count is 3 standard deviations above the expected count given the cell's sequencing depth
- The NB GLM automatically handles the **heteroskedasticity** of count data — the variance depends on the mean — via the dispersion parameter

### Why Pearson Residuals?

- **Interpretability**: Pearson residuals are on a standard deviation scale — values >2 indicate unexpectedly high expression given the cell's total UMI count
- **Variance stabilization**: When the model is correctly specified, Pearson residuals have approximately unit variance regardless of the mean — solving the fundamental problem of log-normalization where variance depends on mean
- **No pseudocount needed**: The residual formula $r = (k - \mu) / \sigma$ naturally handles zero counts without arbitrary offsets; $\mu$ and $\sigma$ come from the fitted model
- **No log-transform needed**: Residuals are approximately symmetric and can take negative values, better suiting methods that assume normality (PCA, t-SNE, UMAP)
- **Preserves biological variation**: Cells with genuine biological overexpression of a gene produce positive residuals; those with underexpression produce negative residuals

### Why Pooling-Based Regularization?

- Fitting an NB GLM with two parameters (intercept, slope) per gene for 20,000+ genes is prone to overfitting, especially for low-abundance genes with sparse counts
- **Pooling across genes with similar abundance** borrows statistical strength: the relationship between count and depth is constrained to be similar for genes expressed at similar levels
- This is conceptually similar to the **dispersion shrinkage** in DESeq2 ([[xr-10-1186-s13059-014-0550-8]]), which pools information across genes with similar mean expression to stabilize dispersion estimates
- The regularization uses kernel regression: a Gaussian kernel weights the contribution of each gene to the pooled estimate based on the similarity of their mean expression level

## 4. Method Analysis

### Core Algorithm: sctransform — Regularized Negative Binomial Regression for scRNA-seq Normalization

**Problem formulation**: Given a UMI count matrix $K$ ($n$ genes × $m$ cells), where $k_{ij}$ is the UMI count for gene $i$ in cell $j$, produce a normalized, variance-stabilized matrix $R$ of Pearson residuals that removes the effect of cellular sequencing depth while preserving biological heterogeneity.

**Algorithm overview**:

#### Step 1: Model Specification — Negative Binomial GLM per Gene

For each gene $i$, sctransform models the UMI counts across cells as:

$$k_{ij} \sim \text{NB}(\mu_{ij}, \theta_i)$$

where the expected count $\mu_{ij}$ depends on cellular sequencing depth via a log-linear model:

$$\log(\mu_{ij}) = \beta_{0i} + \beta_{1i} \cdot \log(d_j)$$

Here, $d_j = \sum_i k_{ij}$ is the total UMI count for cell $j$ (sequencing depth), $\beta_{0i}$ is the intercept (baseline expression), and $\beta_{1i}$ measures how expression of gene $i$ scales with sequencing depth.

The NB is parameterized with mean $\mu_{ij}$ and dispersion $\theta_i$, such that:

$$\text{Var}(k_{ij}) = \mu_{ij} + \frac{\mu_{ij}^2}{\theta_i}$$

When $\theta_i \to \infty$, the NB converges to Poisson (no overdispersion); smaller $\theta_i$ indicates stronger overdispersion relative to Poisson.

- **Innovation**: This is the simplest possible NB GLM — just one predictor (log sequencing depth) — intentionally minimal to capture only the technical effect. Biological variation manifests as departures from this simple relationship.

#### Step 2: The Overfitting Problem — Why Regularization Is Necessary

A naive approach would fit $\beta_{0i}$, $\beta_{1i}$, and $\theta_i$ independently for each gene by maximum likelihood. However:

- Many genes have extremely sparse counts (<0.1% of cells with non-zero counts), yielding unstable or non-convergent MLEs
- The dispersion parameter $\theta_i$ is particularly hard to estimate for low-abundance genes — it can take extreme values that overfit the noise
- Overfitting leads to residuals that are artificially small (the model fits the noise), masking genuine biological variation

sctransform demonstrated this by showing that unregularized NB fits produced parameter estimates with implausibly high variance across genes with similar mean abundance.

- **Innovation**: This diagnostic of the overfitting problem was itself a contribution — previous methods had not recognized that per-gene NB GLMs would overfit scRNA-seq data.

#### Step 3: Regularization via Pooling — Kernel Regression on Gene Abundance

To regularize, sctransform pools information across genes with **similar mean expression**. The procedure:

1. **Compute gene-wise mean expression**: $\bar{k}_i = \frac{1}{m} \sum_j k_{ij}$ — the average UMI count per cell for gene $i$
2. **Sort genes by mean expression** and divide into $K$ equal-sized bins (default $K = 256$)
3. Within each bin, **pool the count data** across all genes: treat all observations in the bin as if they came from a single "meta-gene"
4. **Fit the NB GLM** to the pooled data for each bin, obtaining regularized parameter estimates $\tilde{\beta}_{0, \text{bin}}$, $\tilde{\beta}_{1, \text{bin}}$, and $\tilde{\theta}_{\text{bin}}$
5. **Smooth across bins** using kernel regression: for each gene $i$, compute a weighted average of the bin-level parameters, with weights based on a Gaussian kernel centered at the gene's mean expression:

$$\tilde{\beta}_{1i} = \frac{\sum_{b=1}^K w_b(\bar{k}_i) \cdot \tilde{\beta}_{1,b}}{\sum_{b=1}^K w_b(\bar{k}_i)}$$

where $w_b(\bar{k}_i) = \exp\left(-\frac{(\log(\bar{k}_i) - \log(m_b))^2}{2h^2}\right)$, $m_b$ is the median mean expression in bin $b$, and $h$ is the kernel bandwidth (set automatically).

- **Innovation**: This is **non-parametric regularization** — it makes no assumptions about the functional form of how parameters vary with mean expression. The kernel regression adapts to any smooth relationship, automatically capturing structure that parametric curves (e.g., splines) might miss.

#### Step 4: Computing Pearson Residuals

With the regularized parameter estimates, sctransform computes the fitted mean and variance for each gene-cell pair:

$$\hat{\mu}_{ij} = \exp(\tilde{\beta}_{0i} + \tilde{\beta}_{1i} \cdot \log(d_j))$$
$$\hat{\sigma}_{ij}^2 = \hat{\mu}_{ij} + \frac{\hat{\mu}_{ij}^2}{\tilde{\theta}_i}$$

The **Pearson residual** is:

$$r_{ij} = \frac{k_{ij} - \hat{\mu}_{ij}}{\hat{\sigma}_{ij}}$$

- Under correct model specification, $r_{ij} \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)$ asymptotically — mean zero, unit variance
- Positive residuals: the gene is expressed above the expected level given the cell's total UMI count — indicative of biological overexpression
- Negative residuals: the gene is expressed below the expected level — indicative of biological underexpression
- Residuals near zero: the gene's expression is consistent with the technical expectation

- **Innovation**: The residuals are on a **standard deviation scale**, directly interpretable and comparable across genes with vastly different expression levels. This is the core of the variance stabilization property.

#### Step 5: Variance Stabilization Validation

To verify variance stabilization, sctransform examines the relationship between **residual variance** and **gene mean**:

- For log-normalized data: residual variance (after regressing out depth) shows a strong dependence on gene mean — highly expressed genes have lower variance, lowly expressed genes have inflated variance
- For sctransform Pearson residuals: residual variance is approximately constant across the expression range — the hallmark of successful variance stabilization

This flat variance-mean relationship means that downstream analyses (PCA, clustering) are driven by biological rather than technical variation.

#### Step 6: Highly Variable Gene Selection with sctransform

Instead of selecting HVGs based on dispersion within mean bins (Seurat v2), sctransform ranks genes by the **variance of their residuals**:

$$\text{Var}_i = \frac{1}{m-1} \sum_{j=1}^m (r_{ij} - \bar{r}_i)^2$$

Because the residuals are already variance-stabilized, genes with high residual variance are those that genuinely vary across cells beyond what sequencing depth explains — they are true **biological** HVGs. The top $N$ genes (default 3,000) with the highest residual variance are selected as HVGs.

- **Innovation**: HVG selection is now a natural consequence of the normalization procedure, not a separate heuristic step. The residual variance directly quantifies how much a gene varies beyond technical expectation.

#### Step 7: Integration with Seurat Workflow

sctransform was designed to integrate seamlessly with Seurat:

1. Run `SCTransform()` → returns Pearson residuals stored in `SCT` assay
2. HVGs are automatically identified (top 3,000 by residual variance)
3. PCA is run on the Pearson residuals (scaled, but not mean-centered — preserving the residual scale)
4. Clustering, UMAP/t-SNE, and differential expression proceed as usual

The `SCTransform` function also supports **regressing out additional covariates** (e.g., percent mitochondrial reads, cell cycle scores) by adding them as terms in the NB GLM before computing residuals.

- **Innovation**: By embedding normalization in a single function call, sctransform dramatically simplified the scRNA-seq preprocessing pipeline — replacing 4–5 separate steps (log-normalize, find HVGs, scale, regress out, PCA) with one principled operation.

### Mathematical Details: Regularization via Generalized Linear Model with Kernel Smoothing

The key regularization challenge is estimating the gene-specific overdispersion $\theta_i$ and the depth-slope $\beta_{1i}$ without overfitting. sctransform's approach:

1. **Binning**: Divide genes into $K$ bins by $\log_{10}(\text{mean expression})$. Each bin contains genes with similar average expression, which are expected to share similar overdispersion and depth-response characteristics.

2. **Pooled GLM per bin**: Within bin $b$, pool all observations (all gene × cell pairs) and fit a single NB GLM:

   $$\log(\mu) = \beta_{0b} + \beta_{1b} \cdot \log(d)$$

   with a single dispersion parameter $\theta_b$. This fit is stable because it uses $|\text{bin}| \times m$ data points.

3. **Kernel smoothing**: For gene $i$ with mean expression $\bar{k}_i$, the regularized parameters are kernel-weighted averages of bin-level parameters:

   $$\tilde{\beta}_i = \frac{\sum_b K_h(\bar{k}_i, m_b) \cdot \hat{\beta}_b}{\sum_b K_h(\bar{k}_i, m_b)}$$

   where $K_h(x, y) = \exp(-(x-y)^2 / 2h^2)$ is a Gaussian kernel. The bandwidth $h$ controls the smoothness — larger $h$ produces smoother estimates but may miss sharp transitions; sctransform sets $h$ based on the density of genes along the mean-expression axis.

4. **Alternative: Poisson regularization for low-abundance genes**: For genes with extremely low mean expression, the NB model may still be unstable. sctransform can optionally fit a **Poisson GLM** (which has no dispersion parameter) for genes below a mean-expression threshold, where overdispersion is negligible (NB ≈ Poisson when $\theta$ is large).

### Limitations of the Algorithm

- **NB assumption**: Assumes UMI counts follow a negative binomial distribution — may fail for data with extreme zero-inflation beyond what the NB can capture, or for data with complex biological sources of overdispersion not captured by a single $\theta$ per gene
- **Simple depth model**: The model $\log(\mu) = \beta_0 + \beta_1 \log(d)$ assumes a log-linear relationship between expected count and total UMIs. If the true relationship is nonlinear (e.g., for genes with saturation at high depth), residuals may retain depth dependence
- **Kernel bandwidth sensitivity**: The regularization strength (controlled by the kernel bandwidth $h$) affects results. sctransform sets default parameters that work well in practice, but extreme datasets may require tuning
- **Gene independence assumption**: The NB GLM treats genes independently, but true biological variation involves coordinated gene programs — the residual variance HVG selection does not explicitly reward co-variation
- **No explicit batch modeling in base sctransform**: Batch effects must be regressed out as additional covariates in the GLM or handled by separate integration methods (CCA, Harmony). The residuals do not automatically remove batch effects
- **Memory footprint**: The full residual matrix ($n$ genes × $m$ cells) at double precision requires substantial memory for large datasets — Seurat v4 stores residuals as a sparse matrix to partially address this
- **Loss of count semantics**: The output is continuous residuals, not counts — methods that require count input (DESeq2, edgeR, NBDirichlet) cannot be applied directly to sctransform output. This is a deliberate trade-off: the gain is variance stabilization

## 7. Critical Evaluation

### Strengths

- **Principled statistical foundation**: Unlike heuristic transformations, sctransform is grounded in a well-understood probability model (negative binomial) with clear assumptions that can be checked
- **Variance stabilization is genuine**: The flat variance-mean relationship of sctransform residuals is a direct, quantifiable improvement over log-normalization — this is demonstrated clearly and reproducibly
- **Eliminates arbitrary parameters**: The pseudocount (+1), log-transform, and bin-based HVG selection of Seurat v2 are replaced by a single model with data-driven regularization — no tuning required
- **Interpretable output**: Pearson residuals are on a standard deviation scale — a gene with residual +5 in a cell is unambiguously 5 SD above its expected expression. This surpasses the interpretability of arbitrary log-transformed values
- **Unified pipeline**: Normalization, variance stabilization, HVG selection, and covariate regression are accomplished in one `SCTransform()` call — dramatically simplifying scRNA-seq preprocessing
- **Seamless Seurat integration**: As the default normalization method in Seurat v3/v4, sctransform has been battle-tested on tens of thousands of datasets and is supported by extensive documentation
- **Scalable**: The binning strategy (256 bins, independent fits) is computationally efficient and parallelizable
- **Diagnostic tools**: The residual variance vs. gene mean plot provides an immediate quality check — if the line isn't flat, something is wrong
- **Preserves rare cell types**: By not distorting the variance structure, sctransform prevents rare populations from being "normalized away" as can happen with log-normalization
- **Non-parametric regularization**: The kernel smoothing approach makes no assumptions about the functional form of how parameters vary with expression — it adapts to any smooth relationship

### Weaknesses

- **Minimal GLM specification**: The model $\log(\mu) \sim \log(\text{depth})$ assumes that depth is the only technical factor driving systematic variation in UMI counts. Other technical factors (batch, cell quality, protocol-specific biases) must be added manually or handled post-normalization
- **No built-in batch correction**: sctransform normalizes within a batch; batch integration requires separate tools (CCA, Harmony, RPCA). This is a deliberate design choice but means the normalization itself does not produce batch-corrected values
- **Gene-wise independence**: The NB model treats genes independently, which is statistically convenient but biologically unrealistic — co-expressed gene modules share information that could improve normalization
- **Depth-slope regularization may oversmooth**: Genes whose expression genuinely scales differently with depth than their abundance-neighbors (e.g., genes with unusual GC content or transcript length biases) may be incorrectly regularized
- **No uncertainty quantification**: sctransform outputs point estimates (regularized parameters, residuals) without confidence intervals — users cannot assess how reliable the normalized values are for specific gene-cell pairs
- **Loss of count information**: The output is continuous residuals, not counts — methods that require count data (negative binomial tests, Dirichlet-multinomial models, differential variability tests) cannot use sctransform output directly
- **Assumes UMI counts follow NB without zero-inflation**: For certain protocols or very sparse data, a zero-inflated NB might be more appropriate; sctransform does not provide this option
- **Single-cell only**: Designed specifically for UMI-based scRNA-seq; not directly applicable to bulk RNA-seq (where DESeq2/edgeR handle multi-replicate designs better) or non-UMI protocols

### Missing Controls

- No systematic comparison with SCnorm, Linnorm, or scran on standardized benchmark datasets (e.g., mixture control experiments with known spike-in ratios)
- No evaluation of how sctransform performs when the NB assumption is violated — e.g., data simulated from a zero-inflated NB or from a mixture of NB distributions representing distinct cell states
- No assessment of the impact of kernel bandwidth choice on downstream results — the default is used throughout but sensitivity is not explored
- No evaluation of sctransform performance on non-UMI scRNA-seq data (e.g., SMART-seq2 full-length data) where count distributions may differ
- No quantitative benchmark for rare cell type preservation — the claim is supported visually (UMAP) but not with a metric that could be statistically tested

## 8. Research Insight

### What Changed After This Paper

- **sctransform became the default Seurat normalization**: Starting with Seurat v3 ([[xr-10-1016-j-cell-2019-05-031|Stuart 2019]]) and continuing through v4 ([[xr-10-1016-j-cell-2021-04-048|Hao 2021]]) and v5, `SCTransform()` replaced `NormalizeData()` as the recommended workflow — affecting tens of thousands of scRNA-seq analyses
- **Pseudocount + log-transform became obsolete for Seurat users**: The community largely abandoned the $\log(1 + \text{TP10K})$ approach in favor of Pearson residuals — a rare case of a heuristic being replaced by a principled method
- **Normalization-as-regression became an established paradigm**: Other methods adopted the framework of modeling counts as a function of technical covariates and using residuals as normalized values — e.g., scVI's latent variable model, Dino's Poisson PCA-based normalization
- **The HVG debate was reframed**: Instead of "which heuristic gives the best HVGs?", researchers started asking "which normalization best decouples biological and technical variation?" — shifting the question upstream to normalization quality
- **Variance-stabilization diagnostics became standard**: The residual variance vs. mean plot became a common quality control step, similar to the MA plot for bulk RNA-seq
- **Integration with sctransform residuals improved**: The variance-stabilized residuals provided a better foundation for batch correction methods — Harmony ([[xr-10-1038-s41592-019-0619-0]]) and RPCA-based integration work more effectively on sctransform output than on log-normalized data
- **Benchmarking studies validated sctransform**: Independent comparisons (e.g., scIB, Luecken and Theis 2019) consistently ranked sctransform-based workflows among the top performers

### How It Connects to Current Knowledge

- **Seurat v5** builds on sctransform for its "bridge integration" and multi-modal analysis capabilities, using Pearson residuals as the common normalized representation
- **sctransform v2** (Choudhary and Satija 2022, [[xr-10-1186-s13059-021-02584-9]]) extended the method with improved parameter estimation (using glmGamPoi for faster inference), support for multi-modal data, and better handling of large datasets
- The **Pearson residual concept** has been adopted by other methods: Dino (normalization via Poisson PCA), Analytic Pearson residuals (Lause et al. 2021), and the generalized linear model framework in Scanpy's `pp.normalize_total` alternatives
- **Differential expression methods** have been developed or adapted to work with sctransform residuals — e.g., the `FindMarkers` function in Seurat v3+ uses a Wilcoxon test on residuals by default
- The **variance-stabilization property** has become a requirement for any proposed scRNA-seq normalization method — new methods are expected to demonstrate a flat residual variance-mean relationship
- **Multi-omics normalization** (CITE-seq, scATAC-seq) has drawn on sctransform's principles — modeling technical covariates via GLMs and using residuals for downstream analysis
- The **pooling regularization** idea has influenced other single-cell methods (e.g., DCA denoising autoencoder's per-gene variance estimates, scVI's hierarchical priors)

## 9. Future Research Opportunities

- **Zero-inflated NB extension**: Incorporate zero-inflation into the GLM framework for protocols or tissues where the NB alone does not adequately capture the excess zeros (e.g., non-UMI protocols, highly sparse data)
- **Multi-covariate GLMs with interaction terms**: Extend the regression model beyond $\log(\mu) \sim \log(\text{depth})$ to include interactions between depth and other technical factors (e.g., batch × depth, protocol × depth) — this would produce residuals that simultaneously correct for multiple technical effects
- **Gene-gene correlation-aware normalization**: Incorporate co-expression information into the regularization — genes with correlated expression patterns should share regularization strength, potentially improving normalization for co-regulated modules
- **Uncertainty quantification for residuals**: Provide posterior distributions or confidence intervals for Pearson residuals, enabling uncertainty-aware downstream analyses (e.g., weighted PCA, probabilistic clustering)
- **Nonparametric depth-response modeling**: Replace the log-linear depth model with a more flexible alternative (e.g., splines, Gaussian processes) that can capture nonlinear relationships between expected count and sequencing depth
- **Adaptive binning and bandwidth selection**: Develop data-driven methods for choosing the number of bins ($K$) and kernel bandwidth ($h$) based on the dataset's gene abundance distribution and complexity
- **GPU-accelerated sctransform**: Port the NB GLM fitting and kernel regression to GPU for near-instant normalization of million-cell datasets
- **Online/incremental normalization**: Develop streaming versions of sctransform that can normalize new cells as they arrive without refitting the entire model — important for iterative atlas-building
- **Cross-species normalization**: Extend the pooling framework to incorporate orthology information, enabling joint normalization of cross-species scRNA-seq datasets
- **Multi-modal sctransform**: Extend the NB regression framework to simultaneously normalize multiple modalities (RNA + ADT for CITE-seq, RNA + ATAC for multiome) with shared and modality-specific parameters
- **Spatial transcriptomics normalization**: Adapt sctransform to spatial data (Visium, MERFISH, Slide-seq) where the technical covariates include spatial location and tissue-specific biases in addition to sequencing depth
- **Theoretical analysis of residual distribution**: Derive the asymptotic distribution of regularized Pearson residuals and their covariance structure, enabling formal statistical tests on residual-based analyses

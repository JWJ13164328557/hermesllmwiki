# Moderated estimation of fold change and dispersion for RNA-seq data with DESeq2

---

## 1. Scientific Context

### Existing Consensus (Before This Paper)

By 2014, RNA-seq had largely supplanted microarrays as the dominant transcriptomics technology, but differential expression (DE) analysis of count data remained a statistical challenge. Key prior consensus:

- **RNA-seq produces counts, not continuous intensities**: Microarray-era tools (limma, SAM) assumed normally distributed log-intensities and were statistically inappropriate for discrete count data with a large dynamic range
- **Negative binomial (NB) distribution** had emerged as the preferred model for RNA-seq counts because it naturally accounts for **overdispersion** — the variance of counts typically exceeds the mean, violating the Poisson assumption
- **DESeq** ([[xr-10-1186-s13059-014-0550-8|Anders 2010]], the predecessor) and **edgeR** ([[xr-10-1093-bioinformatics-bts635|Robinson 2010]]) had established NB-based DE frameworks and were the most widely used tools
- **Dispersion estimation was the bottleneck**: With small replicate numbers (typically n = 2–4 per condition), per-gene dispersion estimates were highly unstable, leading to poor type I error control and low power
- **Fold change estimates were noisy**: Genes with low counts produced wildly inflated fold change estimates, making biological interpretation and gene ranking unreliable
- **DESeq's original dispersion shrinkage** was based on a simple mean-dispersion relationship fit, which did not fully leverage the information available across genes

### Existing Models

- **DESeq (2010)**: Negative binomial test with local regression-based dispersion shrinkage; Wald test for DE. Limited to two-group comparisons.
- **edgeR**: Exact test based on quantile-adjusted conditional maximum likelihood (qCML) for dispersion; later added GLM framework for multi-factor designs
- **baySeq**: Full Bayesian hierarchical model with posterior probabilities of DE — principled but computationally expensive
- **limma-voom**: Transformed RNA-seq counts to log-CPM with precision weights, enabling standard limma linear modeling — a pragmatic hybrid approach
- **Cuffdiff/Cuffdiff2**: Transcript-level DE with beta negative binomial model; complex but slower

### Knowledge Gaps

- No method combined **moderated dispersion estimation** (for stable variance estimates) with **moderated fold change estimation** (for biologically meaningful effect sizes) in a unified empirical Bayes framework
- No principled way to rank genes by **effect size confidence** rather than mere statistical significance — existing tools produced p-values but fold changes were unmoderated
- **Multi-factor designs** with interactions were only partially supported; edgeR had a GLM framework but without dispersion shrinkage of fold changes
- **Likelihood ratio tests (LRT)** for multi-factor models were not available with moderated fold changes
- The **outlier problem**: individual genes with extreme dispersion or single-outlier counts could dominate DE results without robust handling

### Why This Paper Matters

This paper introduced **DESeq2**, a comprehensive framework for RNA-seq differential analysis that combined **empirical Bayes shrinkage of dispersion** AND **fold change estimates** in a unified negative binomial GLM. It transformed DE analysis from a purely hypothesis-testing exercise (p-values) into a **quantitative analysis of effect sizes** — enabling researchers to focus on the *strength* of differential expression, not just its *presence*. With over 60,000 citations, DESeq2 became the single most-cited tool for RNA-seq DE analysis, co-dominant with edgeR. Its innovations — dispersion shrinkage, fold change shrinkage, outlier detection, LRT for multi-factor designs, and interaction terms — set the standard for count-based differential analysis in genomics.

## 2. Research Questions

### Primary Question

Can empirical Bayes shrinkage of both dispersion and fold change estimates, within a negative binomial GLM framework, improve the stability, interpretability, and statistical power of RNA-seq differential expression analysis?

### Secondary Questions

1. Does dispersion shrinkage — using a mean-dispersion trend fitted across all genes — produce more stable and accurate per-gene dispersion estimates than maximum likelihood estimation alone?
2. Can fold change shrinkage (using a zero-centered Normal prior on LFCs) reduce the variance of log fold change estimates from low-count genes while preserving true large effects?
3. Does the combination of dispersion and fold change shrinkage enable meaningful ranking of genes by effect size rather than p-value?
4. Can the GLM framework support arbitrary multi-factor designs, including interaction terms and nested comparisons, with Wald tests and likelihood ratio tests?
5. Does the outlier detection-and-replacement procedure (Cook's distance-based flagging) improve robustness against individual outlier counts?
6. Can DESeq2 handle designs with continuous covariates (e.g., dose-response, time course) via the GLM framework?

### Explicit Hypotheses

- Shrinking dispersion estimates toward a fitted mean-dispersion curve will improve precision without introducing substantial bias, because genes with similar average expression share similar dispersion characteristics
- Shrinking fold changes toward zero using a Normal prior will disproportionately reduce variance for genes with low counts or high dispersion — where fold change estimates are most unreliable — while barely affecting genes with strong signal
- The moderated fold changes will produce better gene rankings (by s-value or shrunken LFC) than rankings based on unmoderated fold changes or p-values alone
- The LRT will be more powerful than Wald tests for multi-factor models where the null hypothesis involves multiple coefficients simultaneously

### Implicit Hypotheses

- The relationship between mean expression and dispersion is smooth and monotonic (higher expression → lower dispersion) across the dynamic range of RNA-seq data
- The empirical Bayes prior for fold changes (fitted from the observed distribution of MLE LFCs) captures the true distribution of effect sizes across all genes — i.e., most genes are not differentially expressed, so the prior should be centered near zero
- Outlier counts that drive extreme dispersion or fold change estimates are rare and can be detected by leave-one-out diagnostics without excessive computational cost
- The negative binomial GLM is sufficiently flexible to model most experimental designs encountered in practice

### Why Negative Binomial GLM?

- RNA-seq counts are discrete, overdispersed, and have a mean-variance relationship — the NB distribution captures all three properties naturally
- The **GLM framework** enables arbitrary experimental designs (multi-factor, interactions, continuous covariates, blocking factors) — the same mathematical infrastructure as classical ANOVA/linear regression but adapted for count data
- The NB dispersion parameter $\alpha$ controls the mean-variance relationship: $\text{Var}(K_{ij}) = \mu_{ij} + \alpha_i \mu_{ij}^2$, where $\mu_{ij}$ is the expected count for gene $i$ in sample $j$
- Link function is $\log(\mu_{ij}) = \sum_r x_{jr} \beta_{ir}$ — log-linear model, interpretable as log fold changes

### Why Empirical Bayes Shrinkage?

- With $n = 2$–$4$ replicates per condition (typical for RNA-seq), per-gene dispersion MLEs are extremely noisy — some genes appear spuriously over- or under-dispersed
- **Dispersion shrinkage** borrows strength across genes by shrinking per-gene estimates toward a fitted mean-dispersion trend, analogous to the variance moderation in limma ([[xr-10-1093-nar-gkv007|Smyth 2004]])
- **Fold change shrinkage** addresses a different problem: even with good dispersion estimates, genes with low counts produce huge but unreliable fold change estimates. A zero-centered Normal prior (with scale fitted from the data) shrinks these toward zero, stabilizing the estimates without losing true large effects
- Both shrinkage steps use **empirical Bayes**: the prior distribution is estimated from the data itself (the collection of all genes), rather than specified arbitrarily

## 4. Method Analysis

### Core Algorithm: DESeq2 — Negative Binomial GLM with Empirical Bayes Shrinkage

**Problem formulation**: Given an RNA-seq count matrix $K_{ij}$ ($n$ genes × $m$ samples) and a design matrix $X$ ($m$ samples × $p$ coefficients), estimate per-gene log fold changes $\beta_{ir}$ with moderated posterior estimates, test hypotheses about linear combinations of coefficients, and rank genes by effect size confidence.

**Algorithm overview**:

#### Step 1: Size Factor Estimation — Median-of-Ratios Normalization

DESeq2 estimates sample-specific **size factors** $s_j$ to account for differences in sequencing depth and RNA composition:

$$s_j = \text{median}_i \frac{K_{ij}}{\left(\prod_{v=1}^m K_{iv}\right)^{1/m}}$$

Each column is divided by the geometric mean across all samples; the median of these ratios (per sample) is the size factor. This is robust to a minority of DE genes because the median is insensitive to outliers.

- **Innovation**: Unlike total-count or TMM normalization, the median-of-ratios method is robust to highly differentially expressed genes that could distort global scaling. The geometric mean reference is computed per gene across all samples, providing a stable pseudo-reference.

#### Step 2: Gene-Wise Dispersion Estimation — MLE

For each gene $i$, DESeq2 fits a negative binomial GLM:

$$\log(\mu_{ij}) = \sum_r x_{jr} \beta_{ir} + \log(s_j)$$

where $\log(s_j)$ is an offset (not a coefficient to be estimated). The dispersion parameter $\alpha_i$ (controlling $\text{Var} = \mu + \alpha_i \mu^2$) is initially estimated by maximum likelihood, given the fitted $\mu_{ij}$.

#### Step 3: Dispersion Shrinkage — Empirical Bayes

The raw gene-wise dispersion MLEs are noisy when replicate numbers are small. DESeq2 fits a **mean-dispersion trend**:

1. Regress the gene-wise dispersion estimates against the mean of normalized counts (using a parametric curve — a gamma-family GLM with natural spline basis)
2. Use this fitted curve as the **prior location** for each gene's dispersion
3. Shrink each gene's dispersion estimate toward the curve, using an **empirical Bayes** approach: the per-gene estimate is a weighted average of the MLE and the fitted curve value, with weights determined by the gene's degrees of freedom and the dispersion of the prior

This borrows strength across genes — genes with low counts contribute less to their own estimate and are pulled more strongly toward the trend.

- **Innovation**: The prior is data-driven — fitted from the entire gene population — so it automatically adapts to the dataset's specific mean-dispersion structure. This is analogous to limma's empirical Bayes variance moderation but adapted for the NB dispersion parameter.

#### Step 4: GLM Coefficient Estimation — MLE

With shrunken dispersions, DESeq2 fits the full NB GLM for each gene by iteratively reweighted least squares (IRLS), producing MLEs $\hat{\beta}_{ir}$ for each coefficient and their standard errors.

The design matrix $X$ can encode arbitrary contrasts: two-group comparisons, multi-factor designs, interactions, continuous covariates, and blocking factors.

#### Step 5: Fold Change Shrinkage — Empirical Bayes (apeglm / ashr Style)

The MLE fold changes $\hat{\beta}_{ir}$ are noisy, especially for genes with low counts or high dispersion. DESeq2 applies a **zero-centered Normal prior**:

$$\beta_{ir} \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma_r^2)$$

The prior scale $\sigma_r^2$ is estimated from the data: DESeq2 fits the observed distribution of MLE fold changes across all genes and estimates the scale of the prior that best explains the data. This is an empirical Bayes approach — genes with weak evidence (large standard errors) are shrunk strongly toward zero, while genes with strong evidence retain their MLE values.

Starting with DESeq2 v1.18 (2017), the package incorporated the **apeglm** algorithm (Zhu, Ibrahim, and Love 2019, [[xr-10-1093-bioinformatics-bty895]]) for more sophisticated shrinkage using a heavy-tailed Cauchy prior, and later the **ashr** package (Stephens 2017) for adaptive shrinkage with unimodal priors. The core principle remains: use the global distribution of effect sizes to inform per-gene estimates.

- **Innovation**: Fold change shrinkage is the key conceptual advance of DESeq2 over DESeq and edgeR. It directly addresses the "low-count volcano plot" phenomenon — genes with few counts produce large but meaningless fold changes. After shrinkage, the MA plot shows a characteristic "funnel" shape narrowed at low expression values, reflecting appropriately increased uncertainty.

#### Step 6: Hypothesis Testing — Wald Test and LRT

**Wald test**: For a single coefficient or contrast $C\beta_i = 0$, the test statistic is:

$$W = \frac{C\hat{\beta}_i}{\text{SE}(C\hat{\beta}_i)} \sim \mathcal{N}(0,1) \text{ (asymptotically)}$$

The shrunken dispersion estimates enter the standard error calculation, producing more stable test statistics.

**Likelihood Ratio Test (LRT)**: For testing whether a set of coefficients (e.g., all coefficients for a factor in a multi-level design) are zero:

$$\text{LR} = -2(\ell_{\text{reduced}} - \ell_{\text{full}}) \sim \chi^2_{\text{df}}$$

where $\ell$ is the log-likelihood of the NB GLM. The LRT is more powerful than Wald tests for multi-coefficient hypotheses because it jointly tests all coefficients rather than testing them individually and correcting for multiplicity.

- **Innovation**: The LRT in DESeq2 is particularly useful for time-course experiments (testing whether any time point differs), multi-factor interactions, and ANOVA-like designs where the question is "does this factor matter at all?" rather than "what is the effect size for this specific contrast?"

#### Step 7: Multiple Testing Correction and Independent Filtering

DESeq2 applies the **Benjamini-Hochberg** procedure to control the false discovery rate (FDR). Before correction, it performs **independent filtering**: genes with very low mean normalized counts are removed because they have negligible power to be detected as DE (the multiple testing burden from these genes is minimal, but removing them increases power for the remaining genes).

- **Innovation**: Independent filtering is a principled way to increase detection power at a given FDR threshold. The filtering threshold (mean count) is chosen automatically by optimizing the number of rejections.

#### Step 8: Outlier Detection and Replacement

DESeq2 detects outlier counts using **Cook's distance** — a leave-one-out diagnostic that measures how much each observation influences the fitted coefficients. For genes flagged as having outliers, the offending counts are replaced with a trimmed mean and the model is refit, producing more robust p-values and fold changes.

- **Innovation**: Outlier handling prevents single aberrant counts (e.g., from PCR duplicates, mapping artifacts, or biological outliers) from driving false DE calls. The replacement approach is conservative — it reduces false positives without substantially reducing power for genes without outliers.

### Mathematical Details: Dispersion Prior

DESeq2 assumes that the dispersion parameter $\alpha_i$ follows a log-Normal prior distribution around the fitted mean-dispersion trend $\alpha_{\text{tr}}(\bar{\mu}_i)$:

$$\log(\alpha_i) \sim \mathcal{N}(\log(\alpha_{\text{tr}}(\bar{\mu}_i)), \sigma^2_d)$$

The prior variance $\sigma^2_d$ is estimated from the data by comparing the observed variability of gene-wise MLEs to the variability expected from sampling error. The posterior (shrunken) estimate is the maximum of:

$$\ell_{\text{post}}(\alpha_i) = \ell_{\text{NB}}(\alpha_i; K_i) + \ell_{\text{prior}}(\alpha_i; \bar{\mu}_i)$$

where the prior acts as a penalty pulling $\alpha_i$ toward $\alpha_{\text{tr}}(\bar{\mu}_i)$.

### Mathematical Details: Fold Change Prior

For fold change shrinkage, DESeq2 uses a zero-centered Normal prior on the coefficient of interest $\beta_{ir}$:

$$\beta_{ir} \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \tau^2)$$

The prior scale $\tau^2$ is estimated from the observed distribution of MLE fold changes across all genes that pass independent filtering. The posterior is:

$$\beta_{ir} \mid \hat{\beta}_{ir}^{\text{MLE}}, \text{SE}_i \sim \mathcal{N}\left(\frac{\hat{\beta}_{ir}^{\text{MLE}}}{1 + \text{SE}_i^2 / \tau^2}, \frac{\text{SE}_i^2}{1 + \text{SE}_i^2 / \tau^2}\right)$$

Note: $\text{SE}_i^2 / \tau^2$ is the noise-to-signal ratio — when $\text{SE}_i \ll \tau$ (strong evidence), the posterior mean ≈ MLE; when $\text{SE}_i \gg \tau$ (weak evidence), the posterior mean is shrunk toward zero.

### Limitations of the Algorithm

- **NB model assumption**: Assumes counts follow a negative binomial distribution — may be violated for heavily zero-inflated data (e.g., single-cell RNA-seq without UMIs) or data with complex dependence structures
- **Size factor normalization assumes most genes are not DE**: The median-of-ratios method can be biased if >50% of genes are differentially expressed in the same direction. This is rare in practice but possible (e.g., global transcriptional amplification in cancer)
- **Fold change shrinkage prior**: The zero-centered Normal prior assumes that most genes are not DE, which is appropriate for most experiments. However, for experiments with global shifts (e.g., between species, between very different tissue types), the prior may be misspecified and may over-shrink true effects
- **Computational cost**: Fitting NB GLMs for 20,000+ genes with IRLS is computationally intensive. DESeq2 uses parallelization (BiocParallel) to mitigate this, but genome-wide LRT analysis remains slower than edgeR-QLF
- **Dispersion prior**: The assumption that genes with similar mean expression have similar dispersion may fail for genes with unusual variance structures (e.g., imprinted genes, genes in amplified/deleted regions)
- **Independent filtering threshold**: The automatic threshold selection optimizes the number of rejections, which may be anti-conservative in edge cases

## 7. Critical Evaluation

### Strengths

- **Unified framework**: Combines dispersion estimation, fold change estimation, hypothesis testing, and outlier handling in a single coherent statistical model — not a patchwork of ad hoc steps
- **Fold change shrinkage is transformative**: This is DESeq2's signature contribution. By providing moderated effect sizes, it fundamentally changed how researchers interpret DE results — enabling gene ranking by biological significance rather than statistical significance alone
- **Empirical Bayes throughout**: Both dispersion and fold change shrinkage use data-driven priors — no arbitrary tuning parameters needed. The method adapts to each dataset's characteristics
- **Full GLM flexibility**: The design-matrix framework supports any experimental design expressible as a linear model — two-group, multi-factor, interactions, continuous covariates, paired designs, time courses, and blocking factors
- **LRT for model comparison**: More principled and powerful than Wald tests for multi-level factors and interactions — analogous to ANOVA F-tests in linear models
- **Outlier robustness**: Cook's distance-based detection and replacement protects against single-outlier false positives, a practical concern in real data
- **Excellent documentation and ecosystem**: The DESeq2 vignette is a model of clarity; tight integration with Bioconductor infrastructure (SummarizedExperiment, annotation packages) lowers the barrier to correct usage
- **Massive validation**: Over 60,000 citations attest to real-world performance across thousands of experimental contexts
- **Active maintenance**: Continuous improvement (apeglm integration, ashr support, performance optimization) since 2014

### Weaknesses

- **NB assumption**: Does not model zero-inflation (common in single-cell data). While DESeq2 can be applied to pseudobulk scRNA-seq, it is not designed for single-cell resolution without aggregation
- **Fold change prior misspecification risk**: The zero-centered prior assumes most genes are null — a reasonable default but inappropriate for experiments with global shifts (the `lfcShrink` function offers alternative priors to partially address this)
- **Size factor assumption**: Median-of-ratios normalization assumes the majority of genes are not DE. In extreme cases (massive transcriptional reprogramming), this may fail — though DESeq2's robustness in practice suggests this is rare
- **No built-in batch correction**: Unlike limma (which can model batch effects via `removeBatchEffect`), DESeq2 requires batch to be included as a factor in the design — which consumes degrees of freedom and may not work when batch is confounded with condition
- **Computational cost for LRT**: LRT fits two models (full and reduced) per gene, roughly doubling runtime for genome-wide analysis
- **P-value calibration at extreme tail**: Some simulation studies have noted that DESeq2 p-values can be slightly conservative or anti-conservative at extreme tails for very small sample sizes (n = 2 per group)
- **Heavy R dependency**: Requires the R/Bioconductor ecosystem — no standalone command-line version; Python users must use rpy2 or switch to PyDESeq2 (a reimplementation)

### Missing Controls

- No comparison with baySeq (full Bayesian hierarchical model) on benchmark datasets — the Bayesian alternative was noted but not benchmarked
- No systematic evaluation of fold change shrinkage when a large proportion of genes are truly DE (prior misspecification)
- No assessment of the median-of-ratios normalization when >50% of genes are DE in the same direction (e.g., global upregulation)
- No systematic comparison of LRT vs. Wald test power across a range of multi-factor designs — the choice is left to user judgment
- No explicit benchmarking of outlier detection sensitivity/specificity on data with known outlier rates

## 8. Research Insight

### What Changed After This Paper

- **Effect size thinking became standard**: DESeq2 made it natural to think about "how much does this gene change?" rather than just "does this gene change?" The MA plot with shrunken fold changes became the canonical visualization for RNA-seq DE results
- **DESeq2 joined edgeR as the co-dominant DE tool**: The two packages together account for the vast majority of RNA-seq DE analyses. The availability of two high-quality, actively maintained tools benefited the field through competition and cross-validation
- **Empirical Bayes shrinkage became the expected standard**: DESeq2 demonstrated that shrinkage at multiple levels (dispersion, fold change) is both practical and beneficial — subsequent methods (dream, Swish, DEqual) adopted similar principles
- **The GLM design matrix approach became the default**: The flexibility of specifying arbitrary contrasts through design matrices replaced ad hoc pairwise comparisons as the standard workflow
- **LRT enabled ANOVA-like thinking for genomics**: Researchers could now ask "does this factor matter?" in a principled way, analogous to ANOVA in classical statistics — particularly impactful for time-course and multi-condition experiments
- **Outlier handling awareness increased**: DESeq2's Cook's distance diagnostics raised awareness that individual outlier counts can drive false DE calls, leading to better QC practices
- **The s-value concept emerged**: Shrunken fold changes naturally led to the concept of "s-values" (Stephens 2017) — posterior probabilities that the true effect size exceeds a threshold — as a more meaningful alternative to p-values

### How It Connects to Current Knowledge

- **DESeq2 remains the gold standard** for bulk RNA-seq DE analysis as of 2026, alongside edgeR. Most RNA-seq workflows (nf-core/rnaseq, bioinformatics best-practices guides) default to DESeq2
- The **negative binomial GLM framework** has been extended to other count-based omics: ATAC-seq (DESeq2 with size factor adjustment), ChIP-seq (DiffBind uses DESeq2 internally), metagenomics (DESeq2 for taxon abundance), and CRISPR screens (MAGeCK uses a similar NB model)
- **Single-cell DE tools** (MAST, pseudobulk approaches with DESeq2/edgeR, mixed models) explicitly engage with the NB framework DESeq2 established
- The **empirical Bayes shrinkage** philosophy has spread to other domains: variance stabilization in proteomics, Bayesian modeling in spatial transcriptomics, and effect size estimation in GWAS
- **Fold change shrinkage** is now expected in any DE tool: edgeR added `glmTreat` for threshold-based testing; limma-voom already had moderation; new tools (Swish, DEqual) incorporate shrinkage
- DESeq2's **design matrix approach** has been adopted by transcript-level tools (swish, DEXSeq for exon usage) and single-cell pseudobulk methods
- The codebase and statistical framework have been reimplemented in Python as **PyDESeq2** (Muzellec et al., 2023), extending the method's reach to Python-centric workflows

## 9. Future Research Opportunities

- **Zero-inflated NB extensions**: Extend the DESeq2 framework with zero-inflated NB or hurdle models to better handle single-cell RNA-seq data at the individual-cell level, while retaining the shrinkage philosophy
- **Hierarchical/multi-level designs**: Extend the GLM framework to include random effects (GLMM) for designs with correlated observations — e.g., multiple samples per patient, litter effects in animal studies, or spatial replicates
- **Bayesian nonparametric fold change priors**: Replace the parametric Normal/Cauchy prior with nonparametric alternatives (e.g., Dirichlet process mixtures) that can capture multi-modal effect size distributions (mixtures of null, moderate, and large effects)
- **Batch correction via latent factors**: Integrate surrogate variable analysis (SVA) or RUVseq-style latent factor estimation into the NB GLM, enabling batch correction when batch is confounded with the condition of interest
- **Transcript-level DE with shrinkage**: Extend fold change shrinkage to transcript-level analysis (DEXSeq/Swish-style), where the prior could share information across transcripts of the same gene
- **Power analysis and experimental design tools**: Build on the NB GLM to provide formal power analysis for RNA-seq experiments with complex designs — enabling researchers to choose sample sizes for multi-factor experiments before data collection
- **Uncertainty-aware gene set enrichment**: Propagate the posterior uncertainty from fold change shrinkage into gene set enrichment analyses (GSEA, fgsea), so that enrichment scores reflect the confidence in individual gene-level estimates
- **Multi-omics integration**: Extend the NB GLM framework to jointly model RNA-seq and ATAC-seq counts with shared dispersion and shrinkage priors
- **GPU-accelerated DESeq2**: Implement the IRLS fitting and shrinkage estimation on GPUs for genome-wide analysis of very large datasets (thousands of samples, e.g., GTEx-scale)
- **Interactive effect size exploration**: Build interactive visualization tools that let researchers explore the posterior distribution of shrunken fold changes — sliding thresholds to see how gene rankings change with different effect size cutoffs
- **Causal inference for RNA-seq**: Integrate the DESeq2 framework with causal mediation analysis to distinguish direct from indirect effects in multi-factor experiments (e.g., genotype → expression → phenotype)

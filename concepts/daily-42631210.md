Detecting clear tissue‐ and organ‐specific patterns of gene expression is key to understanding the genetic mechanisms that control plant development. In situ hybridization (ISH) of mRNA is one of the most precise, yet most challenging approaches to gene expression assays. Detection of histone H4 expression in reproductive tissues ofMimulus lewisii, a model angiosperm, was optimized using the RNAscope ISH assay. The optimized protocol was used to detect histone H4 expression in reproductive tissues of two gymnosperm species,Taxodium distichumandJuniperus virginiana, without further need for species‐specific optimization. Additionally, the optimized protocol was used to detect expression ofCYCLOIDEAtranscription factors inM. lewisiireproductive tissues without further optimization and with results similar to those previously reported. The RNAscope assay can quickly and sensitively generate high‐quality ISH results in reproductive tissues across a breadth of plant species. Keywords:branched DNA probes,CYCLOIDEA, histone H4, inflorescence, in situ hybridization, reproductive cone, RNAscope, Z‐probe A fundamental component of plant developmental studies is understanding gene regulation and, in turn, patterns of gene expression. Methods aimed at determining patterns of spatial gene expression often focus on assaying RNA quantities to elucidate areas of active or vigorous transcription, for example, reverse transcription PCR (RT‐PCR) or RNA sequencing (RNA‐Seq). However, these methods are limited in their ability to reveal fine‐scale, possibly tissue‐ or cell‐specific, spatial patterns of gene expression. In situ hybridization (ISH) assays offer a method to visually observe fine‐scale expression patterns in plant tissues initiating and undergoing differentiation (Kramer,2005). Therefore, ISH and its optimization across a diversity of plant species are essential for broad developmental genetic studies. Standard plant ISH has been based on optimization of the original protocol by Jackson (1992) (Boutilier et al.,2002; Xu et al.,2016; Jia et al.,2020). This classical ISH method has been adapted to a broad range of plant species but requires a significant number of treatments and steps, including probe preparation, pretreatment, post‐hybridization washing, and overnight color detection steps (or multi‐week darkroom processing if utilizing radioactive probes; Tandre et al.,1998; Englund et al.,2011; Groth et al.,2011; Carlsbecker et al.,2013)

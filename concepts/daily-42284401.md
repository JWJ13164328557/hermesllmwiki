Type 1 diabetes (T1D) is a chronic autoimmune disease characterized by destruction of insulin–producing β cells in pancreatic islets (1). Loss of β cells results in insulin deficiency, leading to chronic hyperglycemia, requiring daily supplementation with exogenous insulin to maintain normal glucose levels. While insulin administration is a life-saving intervention, it does not fully prevent the development of long-term complications, including retinopathy, nephropathy, and cardiovascular disease (2,3). Furthermore, despite continuous glucose monitoring and insulin therapy, a substantial proportion of individuals with T1D experience life-threatening severe dysglycemia, highlighting the need for better means of replacing lost islet function, especially in individuals with impaired hypoglycemia awareness (4). Cell transplantation represents a proven approach for restoring endogenous insulin production in T1D (5,6). Current clinical methods for transplanting human islets involve implementation of the Edmonton Protocol—where islets from multiple donors are processed in vitro and then transplanted in the hepatic portal vein of the recipient (7). ## 摘要
The generation of insulin-producing, stem cell-derived β cells (SC-β cells) from human embryonic or induced pluripotent stem cells holds promise for treating type 1 diabetes. We coaggregated autologous SC-macrophages that were either unpolarized (SC-MUnp) or polarized to inflammatory (SC-MInf) or regenerative (SC-MReg) states during stage 7 of SC-β cell differentiation.

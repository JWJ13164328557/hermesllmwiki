Accurate species identification is crucial for ecological restoration and can be especially challenging for understudied non‐model species.Quercus garryanais the only native oak species in the Pacific Northwest and is an important component of the endangered oak savanna ecosystem.Quercus roburis an imported ornamental species from Europe and has been found to be mistakenly planted asQ. garryanain habitat restoration projects. We measured leaf morphological traits sampled from herbarium collections in their native ranges using the digital morphometric tools MorphoLeaf and Tomato Analyzer. We then used Lasso logistic analysis to generate a predictive model and tested it on leaves from Portland, Oregon. To streamline this species detection process, we developed Garryanalyzer, an ImageJ plug‐in that automatically measures leaf traits and outputs species predictions. Garryanalyzer demonstrated 95% accuracy in predicting the species identity of herbarium specimens of oaks. Garryanalyzer correctly identified allQ. roburindividuals sampled in Portland but showed lower accuracy forQ. garryana. Many existing morphometric software are not open source, which makes them unable to be customized to specific study systems. Garryanalyzer is built upon the widely used open‐source ImageJ platform. This study also demonstrates a viable workflow for developing similar tools for other ecologically important non‐model plant species. Keywords:applied ecology, conservation biology, digital morphometrics, non‐native species, oak morphology, species identification The detection and control of non‐native species have been a longstanding topic of interest in ecological restoration (Berger,1993; Graham et al.,2024). Many non‐native species are introduced to new areas as a result of human activities, with landscaping being the most frequent reason in the case of woody plant taxa (Reichard and Hamilton,1997; Reichard and White,2001). Non‐native species have the potential to disrupt the local ecosystem by outcompeting or predating upon native species, by introducing pathogens, or by altering ecosystem functioning (Simberloff,2015; Pyšek et al.,2020). A further concern arises when non‐native species are introduced into habitats with closely related native species where hybridization and introgression might occur (Vanden Broeck et al.,2005; Metcalf et al.,2008; Thompson et al.,2010; Millar et al.,2012; Johnson et al.,2016; Fitzek et al.,2018).

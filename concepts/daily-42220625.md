The interaction between oxygen released from rice roots and reduced species in the surrounding soil matrix establishes the rice rhizosphere as a hotspot for the production of reactive oxygen species (ROS), thereby driving various critical biogeochemical processes. In this study, we observed that the concentration of dissolved Fe2+in rhizosphere porewater exhibited pronounced temporal and spatial variations during the rice growth period, consistent with the dynamic patterns of ROS production. Iron plaque on the root surface facilitated the oxidation of dissolved Fe2+, leading to the production of hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) and hydroxyl radical (•OH) under acidic rhizosphere conditions (pH 5.5). Accordingly, rice roots with a higher content of iron plaque showed a stronger capacity to promote Fe2+oxidation and ROS production. Rhizosphere refers to the micro-ecological niches comprising plant roots, surrounding soils or sediments, microorganisms, and their metabolites. Redox conditions in the rhizosphere are regulated by multiple factors, including climate, plant species, soil physicochemical properties, and microbial activity [1]. Seasonal or anthropogenic waterlogging in wetland ecosystems can result in a deficiency of oxygen (O2) and accumulation of reduced species [e.g., Fe(II) and H2S] in the rhizosphere, which are toxic to roots at high concentrations [2]. To survive under such extreme anaerobic conditions, wetland plants develop aerenchyma structures that actively transports O2from leaves to roots [3]. The subsequent radial O2loss (ROL) from the aerenchyma to adjacent reduced bulk soil drives redox reactions with reduced compounds, creating pronounced redox heterogeneity in rhizosphere soils [[4],[5],[6],[7]]. Notably, this redox heterogeneity provides favorable conditions for the production of reactive oxygen species (ROS).

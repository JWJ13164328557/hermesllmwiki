With recent advances in biomaterials, there has been growing interest in using pulpotomy to treat irreversible pulpitis instead of root canal therapy. In this report, we describe a case of pulpotomy with 9‐month follow‐up in an immature permanent tooth exhibiting signs and symptoms of symptomatic irreversible pulpitis. A 9‐year‐old female presented with a chief complaint of a severe toothache in the lower left molar. Radiologic examination revealed a deep carious lesion and Nolla Stage 9. Based on the findings, a symptomatic irreversible pulpitis was diagnosed. Pulpotomy was preferred over root canal therapy in order to preserve the pulp and allow the roots to continue developing. Cold ceramic was used as a biomaterial barrier during pulpotomy. Evidence of dentin bridge formation, root development, and normal responses to cold and electric pulp tests were observed during the follow‐up sessions at 3, 6, and 9 months. Pulpotomy can be a good treatment option for irreversible pulpitis in immature permanent teeth. Irreversible pulpitis in these teeth does not necessarily mean that the entire pulp has been affected. By removing only the affected part of the pulp with pulpotomy, the healthy pulp could still be preserved. Keywords:biomaterial, case report, immature permanent tooth, irreversible pulpitis, pulpotomy When tooth decay is left untreated, it can lead to a serious condition called irreversible pulpitis. This happens when the dental pulp becomes inflamed due to caries or injury. Untreated irreversible pulpitis in young permanent teeth can cause problems like thinning of the root canal walls, an imbalance between the crown and root size, and arrested tooth development [1]. However, there are no clear guidelines for treating irreversible pulpitis in immature permanent teeth [2,3]. The American Association of Endodontists (AAE) suggests a total pulpectomy and root canal filling for irreversible pulpitis [4]. Another method is apexification, which is used for immature teeth [5]. However, both these methods involve removing the entire radicular pulp, which could lead to a higher risk of secondary root fracture. For reversible pulpitis in permanent teeth, like in cases of asymptomatic carious or traumatic pulp exposure, a procedure called pulpotomy can be a good alternative [6]. Young permanent teeth have a larger apical foramen and pulp tissue at the apex that moves with the periapical tissues. These teeth also have good vascularization, making their pulp more resistant to inflammation [7]. Background:
        
      
      With recent advances in biomaterials, there has been growing interest in using pulpotomy to treat irreversible pulpitis instead of root canal therapy. In this report, we describe a case of pulpotomy with 9-month follow-up in an immature permanent tooth exhibiting signs and symptoms of symptomatic irreversible pulpitis.
    


          Case description:
        
      
      A 9-year-old female presented with a chief complaint of a severe toothache in the lower left molar. Radiologic examination revealed a deep carious lesion and Nolla Stage 9. Cold ceramic was used as a biomaterial barrier during pulpotomy. Evidence of dentin bridge formation, root development, and normal responses to cold and electric pulp tests were observed during the follow-up sessions at 3, 6, and 9 months.
    


          Conclusion:
        
      
      Pulpotomy can be a good treatment option for irreversible pulpitis in immature permanent teeth. By removing only the affected part of the pulp with pulpotomy, the healthy pulp could still be preserved.

Keywords:Wheat bran insoluble dietary fiber, Maize starch, Amylose content, Interaction, Digestion Maize starch possesses extensive applications in the food, pharmaceutical, and material science fields due to its cost-effectiveness and facile processability (Yu & Moon, 2021). However, native starches exhibit inherent functional limitations, such as susceptibility to a rapid viscosity decrease under elevated temperatures or mechanical shear (Mohamed et al., 2024), and proneness to syneresis following freeze-thaw cycles, thereby compromising product quality (Wang, Qin, et al., 2022). Crucially, the majority of native starches are rapidly digestible (RDS); their swift hydrolysis in the small intestine often results in a sharp postprandial glucose surge. Chronic excessive consumption of RDS is a significant contributor to metabolic syndromes, including obesity and type II diabetes (Guo et al., 2021). Conversely, slowly digestible starch (SDS) gradually releases glucose, thereby facilitating the maintenance of stable blood glucose levels (Lehmann & Robin, 2007). Resistant starch (RS), undigested in the small intestine, ferments in the large intestine to produce short-chain fatty acids, which in turn attenuate postprandial glucose elevation, modulate gut microbiota, and enhance intestinal barrier function (Niu et al., 2025a).

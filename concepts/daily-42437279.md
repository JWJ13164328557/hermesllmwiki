California has reduced overall smoking prevalence but people with low income still experience disparities in tobacco use. We examined the associations between recent smoking-induced deprivation (SID; spending on cigarettes instead of other needs), daily smoking, cigarette prices, and coupon use. Weighted data from two waves of the Online California Adult Tobacco Survey Supplement collected between November 2022 and November 2023 were combined then limited to current smoking and other variables (n= 2288; weightedn= 1,718,238) for bivariate analysis and multivariable stepwise logistic regression with an interaction term to produce estimates of recent SID. Nearly one-third of California adults (33.04%; CI: 29.36, 36.94%) reported recent SID. We found a significant interaction (p< 0.01) for coupon use at last purchase and non-daily smoking (aOR: 6.85; CI: 4.00, 11.73). Cigarette pack price was not significantly associated with recent SID. Other factors associated with SID were inability to meet basic expenses, higher educational attainment, female gender, and younger age. Policymakers should consider increasing cessation outreach to populations more likely to report recent SID paired with coupon and minimum pack price policies. Interventionists should also consider new programs to address those who do not smoke daily or have high educational attainment. Keywords:Tobacco, Tobacco-induced deprivation, Coupons, Cigarette smoking, Cigarette prices, Tobacco pricing policy While the California adult smoking prevalence was 6.1% in 2024, approximately 1.2 million California adults <185% of the federal poverty line still used tobacco products (California Tobacco PreventionProgram, 2024). Cessation programs and public policies like cigarette tax increases (Farrelly et al., 2012), coupon bans (American Nonsmokers' Rights Foundation, 2024), and minimum pack prices (White et al., 2026) have contributed to lower prevalence. However, increased cigarette costs may be indirectly related to smoking-induced deprivation (SID), or when a person spends money on cigarettes rather than household necessities, such as food (Siahpush et al., 2012). Internationally, SID prevalence ranged 10.8% in the United States to 22.8% in Australia in 2022 (Cho et al., 2025). SID can predict financial stress (Siahpush et al., 2009), is associated with socioeconomic disparities (Parnia & Siddiqi, 2020), and increased spending on cigarettes (Siahpush et al., 2013). The tobacco industry distributes coupons to people who are more likely to face financial hardship (Osman et al., 2019;Zarei et al., 2024); coupon use is associated with higher odds of smoking behaviors (Choi et al., 2018) and reduced odds of cessation (Choi et al., 2018;Wang et al., 2023). Objective:
        
      
      California has reduced overall smoking prevalence but people with low income still experience disparities in tobacco use.           Methods:
        
      
      Weighted data from two waves of the Online California Adult Tobacco Survey Supplement collected between November 2022 and November 2023 were combined then limited to current smoking and other variables (n = 2288; weighted n = 1,718,238) for bivariate analysis and multivariable stepwise logistic regression with an interaction term to produce estimates of recent SID.
    


          Results:
        
      
      Nearly one-third of California adults (33.04%; CI: 29.36, 36.94%) reported recent SID. Other factors associated with SID were inability to meet basic expenses, higher educational attainment, female gender, and younger age.
    


          Conclusions:
        
      
      Policymakers should consider increasing cessation outreach to populations more likely to report recent SID paired with coupon and minimum pack price policies. Interventionists should also consider new programs to address those who do not smoke daily or have high educational attainment.

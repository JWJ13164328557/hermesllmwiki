When an anonymous box of internal tobacco industry documents from Brown & Williamson landed on the doorstep of Professor Stanton Glantz at the University of California, San Francisco, our understanding of what the tobacco industry knew about the harms of its products began to come to light. Despite increasing evidence to the contrary, tobacco executives had denied that their products were addictive and causally linked to cancer and heart disease. Glantz’s documents, now housed in the online University of California, San Francisco, Industry Documents Library, revealed that, as early as the 1960s, tobacco companies understood that cigarettes were addictive and viewed themselves as “in the business of selling nicotine, an addictive drug” and knew “at the best, the probabilities are that some combination of constituents of smoke will be found conducive to the onset of cancer or to create an environment in which cancer is more likely to occur” (https://bit.ly/4cpTKx3). For decades, the tobacco industry continued to deny this publicly and Americans continued consuming these products, assuming they were safe (https://bit.ly/4cEVLEt). Now, research using these same internal industry documents has revealed the strategies tobacco companies have used to shape our diets and propensity for diet-related diseases. The evidence on the health harms of ultraprocessed food (UPF) is unequivocal, and there is extensive evidence that processed junk food has been reformulated to promote overconsumption and patterns of intake similar to those observed in nicotine dependence. The corporations that make these products are now, therefore, one of the leading vectors of disease in the United States (https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMms2507028). We cannot continue to repeat the same mistakes with regulating UPF as we made with tobacco. Things are about to change again. In the early 1960s, the largest US tobacco companies, RJ Reynolds and Philip Morris, bought up major processed food and beverage companies. They entered the food industry with the goal of applying their proprietary research and technology for making cigarettes (e.g., artificial colors and flavors, processing and packaging technologies) to design food products that would be irresistible to consumers. By the mid-1980s, these acquisitions meant that the two largest tobacco companies owned and operated some of the largest food corporations, including Kraft General Foods, Del Monte, and Nabisco. These companies produced many of the most popular global snack food brands, including Lunchables, Oreo, Kentucky Fried Chicken, and Kraft Macaroni & Cheese. They reformulated UPFs to make them more appealing and hyperpalatable (using artificially high amounts of sodium, sugars, and fats), leading to uncontrollable cravings and overconsumption of these energy-dense products.

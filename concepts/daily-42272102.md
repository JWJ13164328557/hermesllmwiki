Rice domestication stands as one of the most significant technological shifts in human history, transforming a wild, shattering grass into a primary caloric foundation for nearly half the global population. A key change during rice domestication was the transition from a perennial to an annual life history. This transition was driven by our ancestors’ obvious preference then for high-yielding rice, as annual varieties channel a vast proportion of their assimilated photosynthetic energy into seed production during the growing season, whereas wild perennial species need to allocate resources between sexual reproduction and perennating structure development. Despite the significant contribution of this annualization process to human civilization, such a singular pursuit of high yield is increasingly being questioned through the lens of sustainable agriculture. It has been increasingly recognized that annual cropping practices resulting from this transition create several long-term environmental and economic challenges, including soil erosion, land degradation, and high resource requirements. Consequently, crop perennialization, the process of converting annual crops into perennial forms, has been put forward as a strategy to address these sustainability issues (Glover et al., 2010). A better genetic understanding of perenniality is essential for effective crop perennialization; however, much of our existing knowledge has been gained from studies of model perennial species in the Brassicaceae family (Li et al., 2022;Zhao and Wang, 2024). In these plants, floral transition is controlled by the integration of cues derived from both age and vernalization. As juvenile plants age, a gradual decline in microRNA156 (miR156) levels confers developmental competence required for flowering (Bergonzi and Albani, 2011). In adult plants, the restriction of flowering, which underlies their polycarpic perennial flowering behavior, is largely achieved through a “reactivation” mechanism of floral repressor genes after vernalization (Wang et al., 2009;Zhai et al., 2024). Although these findings have clarified the evolution of plant life-history strategies and have even enabled the conversion of annual Brassicaceae plants into perennial ones, they offer limited utility for perennial rice breeding. This is because, unlike many cruciferous species, wild perennial rice does not require vernalization for flowering and, more critically, its perenniality is attained to a large extent through a distinct characteristic: clonality.

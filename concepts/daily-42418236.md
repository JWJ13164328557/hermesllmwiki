Wheat allergy is one of the most common forms of food allergy in the pediatric population.1,2 Oral immunotherapy (OIT) is the main disease‐modifying approach under investigation for food allergy,3but adverse reactions, especially during the build‐up phase, remain a key barrier to adherence.4,5Patient heterogeneity and variation in OIT protocols (e.g., food forms and escalation schedules) contribute to inconsistent outcomes; therefore, patient‐tailored desensitization strategies are clinically relevant. We hypothesized that a constant increment dosing schedule based on the BACH (Building‐An‐Augmentation‐Curve‐Harmoniously) principle could improve safety and efficacy.6 This pilot/feasibility study evaluated three constant‐increment wheat OIT protocols with individualized starting doses based on baseline OFC thresholds; outcomes were desensitization and immunologic changes (not powered for between‐protocol comparisons). Nineteen children were screened; one was excluded at screening due to a predefined exclusion criterion (pre‐existing eosinophilic esophagitis). Eighteen were enrolled; three (two OIT and one control) did not complete follow‐up, thus fifteen children completed the study: 10 (9 boys; mean age 7.7 years) underwent a 1‐year home OIT regimen (OIT‐group) and 5 (4 boys; mean age 7.2 years), whose parents declined OIT served as untreated controls. All controls had documented recent (<1 month) reactions to wheat (two anaphylaxis, three urticaria) and were followed for one‐year. Inclusion criteria were age 2–16 years; IgE‐mediated wheat allergy with a physician documented reaction within 1 year; positive wheat SPT (>3 mm), and/or wheat specific IgE f4 >0.35 kUa/L; positive baseline OFC, unless exempted in participants with a physician‐documented IgE‐mediated reaction to wheat within 3 months prior to enrolment [no OIT participant met this OFC‐exemption criterion (n= 0), therefore all enrolled OIT participants underwent baseline OFC]; and written informed consent from a parent or legal guardian.

## 摘要
Crop phenology is a critical determinant for yield prediction and germplasm evaluation. However, precise phenological monitoring in large-scale rice breeding trials faces significant challenges due to the inherent phenological asynchrony among hundreds of cultivars and the trade-off between spatial resolution and temporal continuity in unmanned aerial vehicle (UAV) remote sensing. To address these issues, this study proposes a multi-scale temporal deep learning framework that integrates high-frequency medium-resolution (MR) images as temporal anchor with sparse high-resolution (HR) images as spatial enhancement. We introduce a Missing Aware Gated Fusion (MAGF) mechanism to dynamically integrate multi-resolution features on non-aligned timelines, enabling robust modeling under irregular sampling conditions. Validated on a massive dataset covering approximately 500 rice cultivars and over 100,000 images across 2023 and 2024 growing seasons, the proposed method significantly outperformed single-temporal-scale baselines despite multiple growth stages coexisting within the same dates. The integration of multi-spatial-scale fusion with LSTM temporal modeling yielded superior performance considering efficiency, achieving an Overall Accuracy (OA) and F1-score of 0.873, with a Kappa coefficient of 0.84. A hybrid sampling strategy (daily MR image combined with weekly HR image) demonstrates that weekly flight time can be reduced from 28 h to approximately 6 h while maintaining high accuracy. Notably, even when HR acquisition was reduced to a once every 14 days frequency, the fusion performance remained significantly superior to that of daily MR monitoring alone. The model exhibited strong generalization capabilities. When directly applying the model trained on 2024 data to the 2023 dataset, it maintained an OA of 0.774 and an F1-score of 0.738 under a 3-day error tolerance, with recall for the maturity stage consistently exceeding 0.96. This framework offers a flexible, scalable, and cost-effective solution for high-throughput phenotyping in precision breeding.

### 1. Introduction
Crop phenology is a fundamental biological attribute of plants, representing the integrated result of physiological development throughout the growth cycle and reflecting the combined effects of genetic background, environmental conditions, and management practices [ 1 , 2 ]. It is not only a key component of crop growth models for yield prediction [ 3 , 4 ] but also an essential parameter for crop classification and mapping [ 5 ]. In major food crops such as rice ( Oryza sativa L.), key phenological stages, including tillering, jointing, heading, flowering, and maturity, not only influence yield formation and define optimal management windows but also serve as important phenotypic traits for germplasm evaluation and variety improvement [ 6 , 7 ]. Accurate identification of these phenological stages provides decision support for agricultural management practices such as fertilization, irrigation, and pest control [ 8 ], making it a fundamental issue for precise rice management and regional breeding.
Particularly in rice germplasm trials, hundreds of cultivars with diverse genetic backgrounds frequently exhibit significant phenological asynchrony [ 9 ], rendering traditional single-temporal observations insufficient to comprehensively characterize phenological diversity at the population level. In large-scale field germplasm trials, phenological surveys have long relied on manual field observations. Although intuitive, manual surveys are labor-intensive, prone to high cumulative subjective errors, and often lack the temporal continuity required to capture rapid phenological transitions when dealing with numerous cultivars [ [10] , [11] , [12] ]. On the other hand, crop growth models—such as ORYZA2000, CERES-Rice, APSIM, and WOFOST—simulate developmental processes primarily based on meteorological drivers [ [13] , [14] , [15] , [16] , [17] ]. ### 2.1. Study site and experimental design
The experiments were conducted in 2023 and 2024 at the Baima Teaching and Research Base of Nanjing Agricultural University ( Fig. S1 ), located in Lishui District, Nanjing, Jiangsu Province, China (31°37′25″N, 119°9′15″E). A total of 520 rice germplasm resources, including 340 japonica and 180 indica cultivars from China, India, Thailand, the Philippines, Sri Lanka, and Japan, were cultivated under the same environmental conditions.

### 2.2. Data description and preprocessing
Time series UAV images were acquired using a DJI Mavic 3M (DJI-Innovations, Shenzhen, China). The UAV was equipped with an RTK module for centimeter-level accuracy and a 20 MP RGB camera (5280 × 3956 pixels). In this study, only RGB images were used for rice phenology identification. UAV surveys were conducted during the 2023 and 2024 growing seasons using a multi-altitude flight strategy. In both years, flights at 20 m altitude were performed for orthophoto acquisition, yielding a ground sampling distance (GSD) of 0.93 cm/pixel, with a flight speed of 1 m/s, 80% forward overlap, and 70% side overlap. In terms of operational efficiency, completing a full-field UAV survey at 20 m altitude took approximately 20 min, whereas a survey at 4 m altitude required about 4 h when approximately 1000 plots were imaged individually. In 2023, the field layout comprised 1560 plots in total. Under this configuration, acquiring high resolution imagery at 4 m would have markedly prolonged the total survey duration and increased the time gap between the first and last plots captured within the same survey. Such a prolonged acquisition window could introduce additional variability from changing illumination conditions and other short term environmental fluctuations. Therefore, low altitude images in 2023 were acquired at 6 m, allowing a single image to simultaneously cover four adjacent plots. This strategy improved acquisition efficiency, reduced within survey inconsistency, and yielded a ground sampling distance (GSD) of 0.26 cm/pixel. In 2024, the field layout was reduced to 1040 plots in total, as replication was two, which made individual plot imaging at 4 m operationally feasible, and provided a finer GSD of 0.17 cm/pixel. Regarding temporal sampling, images at 20 m altitude were collected 2–3 times per week before August 22 and almost daily thereafter, excluding rainy days. ### 2.3. Multi-scale time-series deep learning framework
This study proposes a novel crop phenology classification framework that integrates multi-resolution UAV images (MR images and HR images) with time-series deep learning ( Fig. 2 ). The overall workflow includes data alignment, multi-scale feature extraction, temporal modeling, and phenological stage prediction. The multi-scale time-series framework consists of four key modules:(1) Time-series construction: MR images are used as the temporal anchor to build synchronized multi-resolution sequences. A missing-data mask is introduced to explicitly indicate the absence of HR images while maintaining temporal alignment. (2) Feature extraction: A lightweight CNN backbone [ 42 ] is employed to extract spatial features independently from the MR and HR images. The MR features emphasize temporal continuity, while the HR features capture fine-grained texture and structural information. (3) Feature fusion and Missing-Aware Gated Fusion (MAGF): The extracted features are linearly projected into a shared embedding space and combined using a Missing-Aware gating mechanism. This ensures adaptive fusion across modalities and mitigates the impact of missing HR data. (4) Temporal modeling: LSTM networks are applied to capture temporal dependencies across consecutive time steps, modeling the phenological dynamics. A classification head outputs the final crop phenology stage.

### 3.1. MobileNetV3 achieves the best balance between accuracy and efficiency
To select the optimal backbone network for phenological classification, we evaluated MobileNetV3, VGG16, GoogLeNet, and ResNet50 using 2024 UAV images at MR and HR. All models were trained independently under identical experimental settings. The comparison focused not only on classification accuracy but also on the trade-off between model complexity and performance across different spatial scales, thereby identifying the most suitable backbone for subsequent model development. The results showed ( Table 2 ) that phenology classification performance is jointly influenced by spatial resolution and network architecture. Models trained on HR images consistently outperform their counterparts trained on MR images, indicating that higher spatial resolution provides more discriminative visual information for distinguishing phenological stages. At the same time, substantial differences were observed among backbone networks in terms of classification accuracy and computational efficiency. Among the evaluated architectures, MobileNetV3 achieved the most favorable balance between accuracy and model complexity. When trained on HR images, MobileNetV3 attains the highest overall accuracy (OA = 0.856), F1-score (0.854), and Kappa coefficient (0.816), while requiring significantly fewer parameters and lower computational cost than VGG16 and ResNet50. These results indicate that MobileNetV3 maintained strong classification performance while substantially reducing model complexity.

### 3.2. Temporal robustness and biological consistency of the learned phenology representation
To evaluate the framework's robustness, we simulated temporal misalignment between MR and HR images collected in 2024 using offsets (delta) from 1 to 14 days. Overall, increasing temporal offsets resulted in a gradual degradation of classification performance across all evaluation metrics ( Fig. S6 ). When the temporal offset was small (delta1–delta3), the model achieved consistently high performance, with OA and F1-score remaining above approximately 0.85. As the offset increased beyond delta4, a moderate decline in OA, precision, recall, and F1-score were observed. For larger offsets (delta7–delta14), performance metrics stabilized at a lower level, with OA and F1 converging to approximately 0.83–0.84. In contrast, the Kappa coefficient exhibited a more pronounced decline with increasing temporal offset, decreasing from approximately 0.84 at delta1 to below 0.80 at larger offsets. The model using only MR data with LSTM achieves an OA of 0.828, which is lower than the performance obtained when HR images are incorporated with Δ = 14 ( Fig. S6 ). This indicates that, even in the presence of a 14 day temporal lag, the high-resolution spatial details provided by the HR data can still significantly enhance model performance, thereby making the fusion approach superior to the MR-only method. Stage specific error patterns also changed with temporal offset. Across all tested Δ values, most predictions remained concentrated along the diagonal, indicating generally stable recognition of the 5 phenological stages ( Fig. 4 ). However, misclassification between adjacent stages increased as Δ became larger, especially between HFS and MkS and between MkS and DS. By contrast, JBS and MS remained relatively stable across temporal offsets, suggesting that stages with more distinct visual characteristics were less sensitive to temporal misalignment. ### 3.3. Feature visualization and representation analysis
To assess the effectiveness of different components in the proposed model, feature visualization and representation analyses were performed using t-SNE and Grad-CAM. Fig. 5 a shows Grad-CAM visualizations derived from the output features of the last lightweight temporal block. Across the five phenological stages, the activation maps consistently highlight regions corresponding to phenologically relevant structures, such as canopy texture patterns and panicle-related areas. While attention remains spatially focused for stages with prominent visual characteristics, more dispersed activation patterns can be observed in transition stages, reflecting increased visual variability. Fig. 5 b illustrates the t-SNE embeddings of features extracted at different stages of the framework. Features derived from MR images alone form partially overlapping clusters, with pronounced overlap between JBS and HFS. Using HR images, the corresponding feature clusters become more compact and better separated, whereas residual overlap remains for adjacent phenological stages. After multi-scale feature fusion, inter-class separability is further improved, and the overlap between neighboring stages is reduced. The clearest separation is achieved when temporal modeling is incorporated, where fused features processed by the LSTM form well-defined and compact clusters for all phenological stages. Overall, the visualization results demonstrate a progressive enhancement of representation separability from single-scale spatial features to multi-scale fused features and, subsequently, to temporally modeled representations.

### 3.4. Ablation analysis of input modalities and temporal modeling
To disentangle the contributions of individual components within the proposed multi-scale spatial-temporal framework and to verify the necessity of each design choice, a series of controlled ablation experiments were performed. Under identical training settings and data availability, the temporal modeling module and spatial inputs at different resolutions were systematically removed or substituted ( Table 3 ). When only MR images and spatial feature extraction are used (without LSTM), the model achieves an OA of 0.819 and an F1-score of 0.810, indicating a limited capacity to distinguish phenological stages based solely on medium-resolution spatial information. Introducing LSTM-based temporal modeling under the MR setting leads to consistent performance improvement, increasing the OA to 0.828 and the F1-score to 0.822, demonstrating the benefit of temporal information even without high-resolution inputs. When HR images are used without temporal modeling, performance improves substantially, with the OA reaching 0.856 and the F1-score increasing to 0.857, highlighting the importance of fine-grained spatial details for phenology classification. When dense HR images are combined with LSTM-based temporal modeling, the model achieves the highest numerical performance (OA = 0.887, F1-score = 0.888, Kappa = 0.857), confirming the strong discriminative value of high-resolution spatial details. However, this setting assumes dense HR acquisition at every time step and therefore serves as a high-cost benchmark rather than the practical form of the proposed framework. In contrast, the proposed asynchronous MR + HR + LSTM model achieves an OA of 0.873, an F1-score of 0.873, and a Kappa coefficient of 0.840 while requiring only sparse HR acquisition, demonstrating a more favorable trade-off between phenological recognition performance and operational efficiency.

### 3.5. Cross-year independent validation under multiple confounding factors
Cross-year evaluation ( Fig. S7 , Fig. 6 ) was performed by directly applying the model trained on the 2024 dataset to the independent 2023 dataset. At Δ = 3, the model achieved an OA of 0.774 and an F1-score of 0.738 in 2023. Notably, the two datasets differed not only by year, but also by spatial resolution and field design. Specifically, HR images were acquired at 6 m in 2023, corresponding to a GSD of 0.26 cm/pixel, whereas HR images in 2024 it was acquired at 4 m, corresponding to a GSD of 0.17 cm/pixel.

### 4.1. Synergistic mechanism of temporal modeling and multi-scale spatial information
Crop phenology is inherently a dynamic process that evolves continuously over time, with individual phenological stages connected through gradual changes in morphological, physiological, and structural traits rather than existing as isolated states. Consequently, phenological stage classification based solely on single-date images often struggles to accurately delineate stage boundaries, particularly during transitional phases such as JBS and HFS, where visual differences are subtle and temporally progressive [ 46 , 47 ]. At the scale of rice breeding plots, pronounced genetic diversity and phenological asynchrony among cultivars further exacerbate the uncertainty associated with single-temporal discrimination [ 48 ]. To address these challenges, this study integrates explicit temporal modeling with multi-scale spatial information, aiming to jointly exploit temporal continuity and spatial complementarity to improve the robustness of phenological recognition under complex field conditions.

### 4.2. Advantages and design trade-offs of lightweight CNNs for UAV-based temporal phenological modeling
In UAV-based temporal phenological modeling, the choice of the spatial feature extraction network influences not only single date classification accuracy but also directly constrains the computational efficiency, stability, and scalability of the multi-temporal modeling framework. A systematic comparison between classical deep convolutional neural networks, including VGG16, ResNet50, and GoogLeNet, and lightweight architectures ( Table 2 ) indicates that deeper networks do not exhibit performance gains commensurate with their substantially higher computational complexity in phenological recognition tasks. In contrast, MobileNetV3 [ 42 , 50 ] achieved stable and competitive classification performance under both HR and MR, and attained leading overall accuracy, agreement metrics, and F1-scores under high-resolution conditions. These results suggest that phenological stage discrimination primarily relies on mid and low level visual features such as canopy texture, panicle structure, and color variation, rather than highly abstract deep semantic representations, which is consistent with existing understanding of the hierarchical nature of phenological phenotypic features [ 51 ]. Within a temporal modeling framework, spatial feature extraction must be repeatedly executed across multiple time steps, causing network parameter size and computational complexity to be further amplified along the temporal dimension ( Fig. 2 ). Under such conditions, heavyweight networks tend to introduce substantial computational redundancy and increase the risk of overfitting in multi-temporal settings, particularly in breeding scenarios characterized by limited sample sizes and pronounced interannual variability [ 52 ]. ### 4.3. Asynchronous multi-scale fusion strategy: trade-off between monitoring frequency and operational efficiency
Given the long acquisition time of HR UAV images, which makes high frequency phenological monitoring impractical, this study proposes an asynchronous multi-scale fusion scheme within the MAGF framework. Unlike conventional multi-modal fusion that assumes strict temporal alignment across modalities [ 53 ], the proposed approach allows HR images to be collected at discrete intervals (Δ = 1–14) and introduces a missing-aware gating module at the feature level to adaptively allocate contributions from MR and HR streams. When HR images were unavailable, the model infers informative cues by integrating macro-scale temporal trends from MR observations with the historical memory of the LSTM; when HR images were available, they serve as periodic calibration at critical stages. Mechanistically, this design parallels agronomic practice: frequent MR observations maintain continuous situational awareness, while sparse HR observations provide stage-wise refinement [ 54 , 55 ].

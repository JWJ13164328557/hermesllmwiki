### Abstract
Transitory starch is an important carbon source in leaves, and its biosynthesis and metabolism are closely related to grain quality and yield. The molecular mechanisms controlling leaf transitory starch biosynthesis and degradation and their effects on rice ( Oryza sativa ) quality and yield remain unclear. Here, we show that OsLESV and OsESV1, the rice orthologs of AtLESV and AtESV1, are associated with transitory starch biosynthesis in rice. The total starch and amylose contents in leaves and endosperms are significantly reduced, and the final grain quality and yield are compromised in oslesv and osesv1 single and oslesv esv1 double mutants. Furthermore, we found that OsLESV and OsESV1 bind to starch, and this binding depends on a highly conserved C-terminal tryptophan-rich region that acts as a starch-binding domain. Importantly, OsLESV and OsESV1 also interact with the key enzymes of starch biosynthesis, granule-bound starch synthase I (GBSSI), GBSSII, and pyruvate orthophosphote dikiase (PPDKB), to maintain their protein stability and activity. OsLESV and OsESV1 also facilitate the targeting of GBSSI and GBSSII from plastid stroma to starch granules. Overexpression of GBSSI , GBSSII , and PPDKB can partly rescue the phenotypic defects of the oslesv and osesv1 mutants. Thus, we demonstrate that OsLESV and OsESV1 play a key role in regulating the biosynthesis of both leaf transitory starch and endosperm storage starch in rice. These findings deepen our understanding of the molecular mechanisms underlying transitory starch biosynthesis in rice leaves and reveal how the transitory starch metabolism affects rice grain quality and yield, providing useful information for the genetic improvement of rice grain quality and yield.
Key words: transitory starch, starch-binding domain, starch biosynthesis, leaf starch deficiency, rice, Oryza sativa

### Introduction
Starch accounts for more than 80% of the total storage compounds in rice ( Oryza sativa ) grains and is directly related to rice grain quality and yield. Starch biosynthesis involves a series of complex and intricately regulated enzymatic reactions ( Jeon et al., 2010 ). Photosynthates produced in leaves are transported to grains in the form of sucrose, which is then converted to ADP-glucose (ADPG) by sequential enzymatic reactions. As a substrate, ADPG is used to synthesize amylose by granule-bound starch synthase (GBSS) and to form amylopectin via the starch synthase, starch-branching enzyme, and starch-debranching enzyme ( Umemoto et al., 2002 ; Satoh et al., 2003 ; Bahaji et al., 2014 ). In addition to the above key enzymes, many proteins involved in carbon metabolism, the respiratory chain, and plastid development, such as Floury endosperm (FLO) proteins, participate in endosperm starch biosynthesis ( Kang et al., 2005 ; Peng et al., 2014 ; Teng et al., 2019 ; Yu et al., 2021 ; Yang et al., 2023 ). In addition, many transcription factors, such as RSR1, bZIP58, MADS29, NF-YB1, and Opaque3, assist in endosperm starch biosynthesis by regulating the transcription of genes encoding starch biosynthesis enzymes ( Nayar et al., 2013 ; Wang et al., 2013 , 2020a ; Bello et al., 2019 ; Cao et al., 2022 ).
In addition to serving as the primary storage compound in the endosperm, starch also occurs in the leaves and stem sheaths of cereal crops. During the day, excess photosynthetic products are stored as starch in chloroplasts; however, this leaf starch is degraded at night to provide energy for plant growth. Therefore, leaf starch is referred to as “transitory starch.” During the grain-filling stage, carbohydrates in leaves and stem sheaths are transported to endosperm cells to synthesize storage starch. Therefore, transitory starch biosynthesis and degradation also affect endosperm starch biosynthesis, ultimately affecting grain quality and yield ( Scofield et al., 2009 )

### OsLESV is involved in transitory and storage starch biosynthesis in rice
To gain a better understanding of the biological functions and molecular mechanisms underlying the biosynthesis of transitory starch in rice leaves, we used iodine staining to detect starch in the leaves and identified a rice mutant, leaf starch deficiency 1 ( lsd1 ), with decreased starch content in leaves, from a mutant library generated by ethyl methanesulfonate mutagenesis using indica cv. YK17. Compared with wild-type YK17, the staining of lsd1 leaves was obviously lighter, and the number of SGs in chloroplasts of mesophyll cells was significantly reduced ( Figure 1 A and 1B). We measured the total starch and amylose contents in lsd1 and wild-type leaves at the end of the day and found that lsd1 showed a significantly reduced total starch content and no detectable amylose in leaves compared with the wild type ( Figure 1 C and 1D). The endosperm of the lsd1 mutant was opaque and loosely packed with round and irregularly arranged SGs, whereas SGs in wild-type endosperm were polyhedral, similar in size, and densely packed ( Figure 1 E and 1F). The 1000-grain weight and grain yield per plant were also significantly lower in lsd1 than in YK17 ( Figure 1 G and 1H). Furthermore, the plant height, seed setting rate, grain length, grain width, and grain thickness of lsd1 were significantly lower, whereas there were no significant differences in the tiller number, panicle length, chlorophyll content, and photosynthetic rate of leaves between the mutant and wild type ( Supplemental Figure 1 A–1J).

### OsLESV and its homolog OsESV1 function in transitory starch biosynthesis in rice leaves
The predicted OsLESV protein is composed of 618 amino acids, containing a Trp-rich region (between amino acids 351 and 606) at the C terminus ( Supplemental Figure 2B ). We found that the expressed protein OsESV1 (LOC_Os03g04100), an Arabidopsis ESV1 homolog, has the highest sequence similarity to OsLESV in the rice genome ( Supplemental Figure 2A ; Feike et al., 2016 ; Song et al., 2021 ; Liu et al., 2023 ). OsESV1 (LSD2) is composed of 431 amino acids and shares a C-terminal Trp-rich region with OsLESV (LSD1). OsLESV and OsESV1 homologs are present in other plant species, such as sorghum ( Sorghum bicolor ), maize ( Zea mays ), Setaria italica , and Triticum urartu , and share a highly conserved C-terminal Trp-rich domain ( Supplemental Figure 2A–2C ). To investigate the biological functions of OsLESV and OsESV1, we obtained their single mutants oslesv-1 , oslesv-2 , osesv1-1 , and osesv1-2 and double mutant oslesv esv1 in the Nip genetic background by CRISPR–Cas9 gene editing ( Figure 2 A and 2B). ### The C-terminal Trp-rich regions of OsLESV and OsESV1 are essential for their binding to starch
We examined the temporal and spatial expression profiles of OsLESV and OsESV1 in the wild type and oslesv-1 or osesv1-1 . OsLESV and OsESV1 were constitutively expressed in all examined tissues; OsLESV was expressed at a higher level in leaves and young endosperms, whereas OsESV1 was expressed at a higher level in leaves. Moreover, the transcript levels of OsLESV and OsESV1 were higher in the wild type than in oslesv-1 or osesv1-1 in all examined tissues ( Supplemental Figure 3A and 3B ). As a product of photosynthesis, starch accumulates in plant leaves during the day and gradually converts to sucrose during the night ( Smith and Zeeman, 2020 ). Consistent with this notion, we found that OsLESV and OsESV1 were induced by carbon starvation. Specifically, the expression levels of OsLESV , OsESV1 , and two carbon starvation-induced marker genes ( GBSSII and SnRK1a ), gradually decreased during the day with the accumulation of starch and gradually increased at night with the degradation of starch in both wild-type and oslesv-1 or osesv1-1 plants ( Figure 3 A and 3B; Supplemental Figure 3C ).

### OsLESV and OsESV1 modulate endosperm starch biosynthesis and affect rice grain quality and yield
The biosynthesis and degradation of transitory starch in plant leaves affect plant growth and development ( Abt and Zeeman, 2020 ). Therefore, we investigated whether rice grain characteristics are influenced by OsLESV and OsESV1. The mature endosperm of oslesv was opaque; that of osesv1 showed severe white belly, and that of the double mutant oslesv esv1 appeared floury and shrunken ( Figure 4 A and 4E; Supplemental Figure 4B–4D ). Scanning electron microscopy of the transverse sections of mature endosperms showed that SGs were polyhedral and tightly packed in the wild type but irregularly rounded and loosely packed in the single and double mutants ( Figure 4 B; Supplemental Figure 4B–4D ). Semi-thin sections and transmission electron microscopy showed that the central regions of wild-type developing endosperm cells were filled with well-developed, compound SGs, by contrast, endosperm cells of the single and double mutants had many single and dispersed SGs and many immature amyloplasts that were stained only weakly by iodine ( Figure 4 C and 4D; Supplemental Figure 4B–4D ).

### OsLESV and OsESV1 interact with and maintain the stability of GBSSI, GBSSII, and PPDKB
We performed an RNA sequencing-based transcriptome analysis of flag leaves of the mutants and the wild type to investigate the molecular mechanisms by which OsLESV and OsESV1 modulate starch biosynthesis and degradation and found that many differentially expressed genes are involved in starch and sucrose metabolism and carbon metabolism ( Supplemental Figure 7 ). Reverse transcription quantitative PCR (RT–qPCR) revealed that the transcription levels of many key starch biosynthesis- and degradation-related genes were significantly downregulated or upregulated in the leaves and developing endosperm of oslesv , osesv1 , and oslesv esv1 mutants ( Supplemental Figure 8 ). We then used a yeast two-hybrid (Y2H) assay to screen starch biosynthesis- and degradation-related proteins that directly interact with OsLESV and OsESV1 and identified three key starch biosynthesis enzymes, GBSSI, GBSSII, and PPDKB ( Figure 5 A; Supplemental Figure 9A and 9B ). GBSSI and GBSSII are responsible for the biosynthesis of amylose in rice endosperm and leaves, respectively ( Yamakawa et al., 2007 ; Huang et al., 2020 ), whereas PPDKB encodes a key enzyme responsible for the interconversion of pyruvate and hexose and is expressed in developing endosperm and leaves ( Kang et al., 2005 ). The results of bimolecular fluorescence complementation (BiFC) and GST pull-down analysis further confirmed that OsLESV and OsESV1 physically interact with GBSSI, GBSSII, and PPDKB ( Figure 5 B and 5C; Supplemental Figure 9C ).

### OsLESV and OsESV1 facilitate the targeting of GBSSs to SGs
GBSSs are major SG-bound proteins and key enzymes responsible for amylose biosynthesis. Given that OsLESV and OsESV1 co-localize and physically interact with GBSSs ( Figures 3 C, 3D, and 5 A–5C), we investigated whether the abundance of GBSSs in SGs was altered in the oslesv , osesv1 , and oslesv esv1 mutants. We extracted SGs with granule-bound proteins from the leaves and grains of the wild type and mutants and visualized them on silver-stained sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS–PAGE) gels and immunoblots probed with GBSSI- and GBSSII-specific antibodies. As expected, GBSSI and GBSSII were detected in SGs extracted from endosperms and leaves, respectively. Furthermore, the levels of SG-bound GBSSI in developing endosperms and GBSSII in leaves were significantly reduced in oslesv , osesv1 , and oslesv esv1 compared with the wild type, suggesting weakened binding ability of GBSSI and GBSSII to starch in the single and double mutants ( Figure 5 N).

### Overexpression of GBSSI, GBSSII, and PPDKB partially rescues the oslesv and osesv1 phenotypes
Given that OsLESV and OsESV1 interact with GBSSI, GBSSII, and PPDKB to maintain their protein stability and enzyme activity, and that the contents of transitory and storage starch were compromised in oslesv , osesv1 , and oslesv esv1 , we overexpressed GBSSI , GBSSII , and PPDKB in the oslesv and osesv1 mutants under the control of maize Ubiquitin1 promoter ( Figure 6 ; Supplemental Figure 10 ). The leaves of all GBSSI , GBSSII , and PPDKB overexpression (OE) lines stained significantly darker with iodine and had significantly higher total starch contents compared with the leaves of oslesv-1 and osesv1-1 , although they did not reach the levels observed in wild-type leaves. ( Figure 6 A–6C; Supplemental Figure 10D, 10F , and 10L). The grain appearance qualities of GBSSI , GBSSII , and PPDKB OE lines were considerably improved: the chalkiness rate and chalkiness degree of all OE lines were significantly lower than those of the oslesv and osesv1 mutants ( Figure 6 D–6F, 6J, and 6K; Supplemental Figure 10 G, 10H, 10M, and 10N). Meanwhile, the total starch and amylose contents in the endosperm were also significantly elevated in these OE lines, either being restored to or partially restored to wild-type levels ( Figure 6 G, 6H, 6L, and 6M; Supplemental Figure 10I, 10J, 10O, and 10P ). Moreover, the 1000-grain weight of several OE lines was also significantly elevated, especially in the oslesv background ( Figure 6 I and 6N; Supplemental Figure 10K and 10Q ). These results show that overexpression of GBSSI , GBSSII , and PPDKB partially rescued the defective phenotype of oslesv and osesv1 , further supporting the crucial role of OsLESV and OsESV1 in rice transitory and storage starch biosynthesis by maintaining the stability of GBSSI, GBSSII, and PPDKB, as well as facilitating the binding of GBSSs to developing SGs.

### OsLESV and OsESV1 bind to starch via a Trp-rich region and mediate the targeting of GBSSs to SGs
Subcellular localization and starch-binding experiments revealed that OsLESV and OsESV1 can bind to starch, amylose, and amylopectin ( Figure 3 C–3F; Supplemental Figure 3D–3F ). Starch-binding proteins typically possess surface-binding sites or SBDs ( Wilkens et al., 2018 ). CBM48 represents a relatively common type of SBD ( Guillén et al., 2010 ; Wilkens et al., 2018 ), found in many enzymes and non-enzymatic proteins associated with starch biosynthesis in rice. The non-enzymatic OsGBP1 and FLO6 proteins contain a C-terminal CBM48 domain that is crucial for their binding to starch ( Wang et al., 2020b ). In this study, we found that OsLESV, OsESV1, as well as their Arabidopsis and maize homologs, all possess a highly conserved C-terminal Trp-rich region and could bind to starch ( Figure 3 F; Supplemental Figures 3 E–3G and 2 ; Feike et al., 2016 ). Further protein truncation experiments showed that this region was essential for their binding to starch ( Figure 3 G; Supplemental Figure 3H and 3I ), which aligns with recent research ( Liu et al., 2023 ; Yan et al., 2024 ). CBM domains typically contain conserved aromatic amino acid residues that align proteins with the non-polar facets of glucose residues in starch ( Christiansen et al., 2009 ). Amino acid sequence analysis revealed that the C-terminal Trp-rich regions of OsLESV, OsESV1, and their homologs also contain several conserved aromatic amino acids ( Supplemental Figure 2C ), which may be crucial for their binding to starch. Therefore, our results suggest that the highly conserved C-terminal Trp-rich region in OsLESV, OsESV1, and their homologs in other plants, serves as an SBD that is essential for the involvement of these proteins in starch biosynthesis and degradation.

### OsLESV and OsESV1 maintain the stability and enzyme activity of GBSSI, GBSSII, and PPDKB
The biosynthesis and degradation of starch are facilitated by complex pathways involving many enzymes, non-enzymatic proteins, and transcription factors. GBSSI and GBSSII are responsible for amylose biosynthesis in endosperms and leaves, respectively ( Martin and Smith, 1995 ; Vrinten and Nakamura, 2000 ). In rice, GBSSI and GBSSII loss-of-function mutants have drastically reduced amylose contents in endosperms and leaves, respectively. OsPPDKB, a cytosolic pyruvate orthophosphate dikinase, plays an important role in interconverting pyruvate and hexose in the developing endosperms and leaves of rice, and starch biosynthesis during rice grain filling is affected in osppdkb mutants ( Kang et al., 2005 ). Many transcription factors and non-enzymatic proteins regulate starch biosynthesis by upstream regulation of genes encoding GBSS and PPDKB or through interactions with the proteins ( Wang et al., 2013 ; Zhang et al., 2015b ; Bello et al., 2019 ). In this study, we found that OsLESV and OsESV1 directly interacted with GBSSI, GBSSII, and PPDKB; and the transcript levels, protein abundance, and enzyme activities of GBSSI, GBSSII, and PPDKB in leaves and/or developing endosperms were significantly reduced in OsLESV and OsESV1 single and double loss-of-function mutants ( Figure 5 A–5F and 5J–5M; Supplemental Figures 8 and 9 ). We further demonstrated that OsLESV and OsESV1 help maintain GBSSI, GBSSII, and PPDKB protein stability ( Figure 5 G–5I). The overexpression of GBSSI, GBSSII, and PPDKB in the oslesv and osesv1 mutants significantly increased starch content in both leaves and endosperms and improved the grain weight and appearance qualities, although these phenotypic characteristics of the OE lines did not completely revert to wild-type levels ( Figure 6 ; Supplemental Figure 10 ). 5. We report that the C-terminal Trp-rich regions function as SBDs, and are essential for the binding of OsLESV and OsESV1 to starch.
6. These results suggest that OsLESV and OsESV1 interact with key starch biosynthesis enzymes GBSSI, GBSSII, and PPDKB to maintain their protein stability and enzyme activity.
7. Therefore, our results suggest that the highly conserved C-terminal Trp-rich region in OsLESV, OsESV1, and their homologs in other plants, serves as an SBD that is essential for the involvement of these proteins in starch biosynthesis and degradation.
8. Importantly, OsLESV and OsESV1 also interact with the key enzymes of starch biosynthesis, granule-bound starch synthase I (GBSSI), GBSSII, and pyruvate orthophosphote dikiase (PPDKB), to maintain their protein stability and activity.

### Abstract
Electronic devices have become an indispensable commodity
modern world, greatly contributing to ease and comfort in daily life.
However, this excessive dependence has given rise to ever-increasing
piles of electronic waste (e-waste) generated at a rate of 2 million
metric tons per year, which is disassembled and disposed of carelessly,
due to unregulated management, negligence, inadequate infrastructure,
and lack of proper policies and legislation. E-waste contains hazardous
elements, such as heavy metals and organic pollutants, which infiltrate
the soil, changing its physicochemical characteristics and the microbiome
composition. They hinder plant growth and pose serious health risks
to animals and humans when they enter the food chain. Although different
physical and chemical techniques exist for recycling disposed materials,
alternative methods based on the use of biological means are gaining
attention because of their environmental friendliness. This review
is an attempt to underline the efficacy of biological remediation
in alleviating heavy metal contamination in an environmentally sustainable
manner. The composition of e-waste and its deteriorating impact on
soil health and plant growth are discussed, with a focus on biological
remediation of e-waste-induced pollution, including plant (phytoremediation)-
and microbe-mediated (bioremediation) remediation of heavy metals
and organic pollutants. The biological mechanisms underlying these
remediation processes are describe

equipment that are no longer in use. A
trajectory derived following the recent trends in e-scrap generation
worldwide projects its accumulation to reach a staggering 82 million
metric tons by 2030, a further increase of 33% from the figure of

), with annual production increasing to a
rate of 2.6 million metric tons.

### Abstract
Electronic devices have become an indispensable commodity
modern world, greatly contributing to ease and comfort in daily life.
However, this excessive dependence has given rise to ever-increasing
piles of electronic waste (e-waste) generated at a rate of 2 million
metric tons per year, which is disassembled and disposed of carelessly,
due to unregulated management, negligence, inadequate infrastructure,
and lack of proper policies and legislation. E-waste contains hazardous
elements, such as heavy metals and organic pollutants, which infiltrate
the soil, changing its physicochemical characteristics and the microbiome
composition. They hinder plant growth and pose serious health risks
to animals and humans when they enter the food chain. Although different
physical and chemical techniques exist for recycling disposed materials,
alternative methods based on the use of biological means are gaining
attention because of their environmental friendliness. This review
is an attempt to underline the efficacy of biological remediation
in alleviating heavy metal contamination in an environmentally sustainable
manner. The composition of e-waste and its deteriorating impact on
soil health and plant growth are discussed, with a focus on biological
remediation of e-waste-induced pollution, including plant (phytoremediation)-
and microbe-mediated (bioremediation) remediation of heavy metals
and organic pollutants. The biological mechanisms underlying these
remediation processes are described, and advances in the field, facilitated
by modern biotechnological tools such as nanotechnology, genetic engineering,
and gene editing, are highlighted to provide a comprehensive overview
of the role of these remediation approaches in mitigating the environmental

Major sources of heavy metals and
organic pollutants can be broadly classified as natural and anthropogenic
sources. These sources can, in turn, be point or diffuse sources. The weathering of parent rock materials through
the action of geochemical processes can result in the natural occurrence
of heavy metals in soils. However, anthropogenic
interventions have led to an increase in heavy metals in both rural
and urban areas, exceeding the specified background levels, thereby
posing a significant health risk and environmental hazard. Among the diverse human-driven contributors,
major sources of heavy metals include the use of inorganic fertilizers,
pesticides, manures, sewage sludge application, wastewater irrigation,
metal mining and smelting, industrial wastes, and atmospheric sources.
A variety of organic pollutants (OPs) also enter the soil systems
through these aforementioned routes, either directly or indirectly. For instance, pharmaceuticals and personal care
products can reach the soil when wastewater is used for irrigation
or through the application of sewage sludge, thereby representing
an indirect route of organic pollutant released into soil. Veterinary
medicines can be released directly into the soil or indirectly when
soil amendments from livestock facilities, such as manure and compost,
are applied. Pesticide application in agricultural fields and atmospheric

### Abstract
Electronic devices have become an indispensable commodity
modern world, greatly contributing to ease and comfort in daily life.
However, this excessive dependence has given rise to ever-increasing
piles of electronic waste (e-waste) generated at a rate of 2 million
metric tons per year, which is disassembled and disposed of carelessly,
due to unregulated management, negligence, inadequate infrastructure,
and lack of proper policies and legislation. E-waste contains hazardous
elements, such as heavy metals and organic pollutants, which infiltrate
the soil, changing its physicochemical characteristics and the microbiome
composition. They hinder plant growth and pose serious health risks
to animals and humans when they enter the food chain. Although different
physical and chemical techniques exist for recycling disposed materials,
alternative methods based on the use of biological means are gaining
attention because of their environmental friendliness. This review
is an attempt to underline the efficacy of biological remediation
in alleviating heavy metal contamination in an environmentally sustainable
manner. The composition of e-waste and its deteriorating impact on
soil health and plant growth are discussed, with a focus on biological

and microbe-mediated (bioremediation) remediation of heavy metals
and organic pollutants. The biological mechanisms underlying these
remediation processes are described, and advances in the field, facilitated

Major sources of heavy metals and

sources. These sources can, in turn, be point or diffuse sources. The weathering of parent rock materials through

Major sources of heavy metals and
organic pollutants can be broadly classified as natural and anthropogenic
sources. These sources can, in turn, be point or diffuse sources. The weathering of parent rock materials through
the action of geochemical processes can result in the natural occurrence
of heavy metals in soils. However, anthropogenic
interventions have led to an increase in heavy metals in both rural
and urban areas, exceeding the specified background levels, thereby
posing a significant health risk and environmental hazard. Among the diverse human-driven contributors,
major sources of heavy metals include the use of inorganic fertilizers,

### Abstract
Electronic devices have become an indispensable commodity
modern world, greatly contributing to ease and comfort in daily life.
However, this excessive dependence has given rise to ever-increasing
piles of electronic waste (e-waste) generated at a rate of 2 million
metric tons per year, which is disassembled and disposed of carelessly,
due to unregulated management, negligence, inadequate infrastructure,
and lack of proper policies and legislation. E-waste contains hazardous
elements, such as heavy metals and organic pollutants, which infiltrate
the soil, changing its physicochemical characteristics and the microbiome

Major sources of heavy metals and
organic pollutants can be broadly classified as natural and anthropogenic
sources. These sources can, in turn, be point or diffuse sources. The weathering of parent rock materials through
the action of geochemical processes can result in the natural occurrence
of heavy metals in soils. However, anthropogenic

### Abstract
Electronic devices have become an indispensable commodity
modern world, greatly contributing to ease and comfort in daily life.
However, this excessive dependence has given rise to ever-increasing
piles of electronic waste (e-waste) generated at a rate of 2 million
metric tons per year, which is disassembled and disposed of carelessly,
due to unregulated management, negligence, inadequate infrastructure,
and lack of proper policies and legislation. E-waste contains hazardous
elements, such as heavy metals and organic pollutants, which infiltrate
the soil, changing its physicochemical characteristics and the microbiome
composition. They hinder plant growth and pose serious health risks
to animals and humans when they enter the food chain. Although different
physical and chemical techniques exist for recycling disposed materials,
alternative methods based on the use of biological means are gaining
attention because of their environmental friendliness. This review
is an attempt to underline the efficacy of biological remediation
in alleviating heavy metal contamination in an environmentally sustainable
manner. The composition of e-waste and its deteriorating impact on
soil health and plant growth are discussed, with a focus on biological
remediation of e-waste-induced pollution, including plant (phytoremediation)-
and microbe-mediated (bioremediation) remediation of heavy metals
and organic pollutants. The biological mechanisms underlying these
remediation processes are described, and advances in the field, facilitated
by modern biotechnological tools such as nanotechnology, genetic engineering,
and gene editing, are highlighted to provide a comprehensive overview
of the role of these remediation approaches in mitigating the environmental
impacts of e-waste-induced pollution and to outline directions for

### Composition of E-Waste
Electronic waste mainly consists
materials like minerals, glass, composite materials, etc. According
to a recent report by UNITAR, of the 62 billion kg of e-waste generated
in 2022, metals constituted 31 billion kg (50%), plastics 17 billion
kg (27%), and 14 billion kg other materials (23%) ( Figure
). WEEE comprises waste generated from EEE items, encompassing a wide
array of electrical appliances that can be categorized into 6 categories
in alignment with their waste management characteristics. These categories are (1) temperature exchange
equipment; (2) screens and monitors; (3) lamps; (4) large equipment;
(5) small equipment; and (6) small IT and telecommunication equipment
such as toys, video cameras, microwave ovens, vacuum cleaners, and
e-cigarettes, represents the largest category of e-waste, accounting
for almost one-third of the world’s total e-waste, with around
20.4 billion kg generated in 2022. The second largest category is
formed by large equipment, contributing 15.7 billion kg of e-waste,
including electric stoves, dishwashers, washing machines, clothes
dryers, large printers, copying equipment, and photovoltaic panels.
The third category, accounting for 13.3 billion kg of e-waste, is
the temperature exchange equipment, which refers to the cooling and
freezing equipment, like refrigerators, freezers, air conditioners,
and heat pumps. Screens and monitors constitute the fourth largest
fraction (5.9 billion kg) of e-waste, followed by small IT and telecommunication
equipment as the fifth largest (1.9 billion kg), while lamps represent
the smallest category (1.9 billion kg).

Major sources of heavy metals and
organic pollutants can be broadly classified as natural and anthropogenic
sources. These sources can, in turn, be point or diffuse sources. The weathering of parent rock materials through
the action of geochemical processes can result in the natural occurrence
of heavy metals in soils. However, anthropogenic
interventions have led to an increase in heavy metals in both rural
and urban areas, exceeding the specified background levels, thereby
posing a significant health risk and environmental hazard. Among the diverse human-driven contributors,
major sources of heavy metals include the use of inorganic fertilizers,
pesticides, manures, sewage sludge application, wastewater irrigation,
metal mining and smelting, industrial wastes, and atmospheric sources.
A variety of organic pollutants (OPs) also enter the soil systems
through these aforementioned routes, either directly or indirectly. For instance, pharmaceuticals and personal care
products can reach the soil when wastewater is used for irrigation
or through the application of sewage sludge, thereby representing
an indirect route of organic pollutant released into soil. Veterinary
medicines can be released directly into the soil or indirectly when
soil amendments from livestock facilities, such as manure and compost,
are applied. Pesticide application in agricultural fields and atmospheric
deposition of OPs from vehicular emissions, industrial emissions,
and incineration processes also introduce OPs into soil. E-waste also
contributes to this soil OP contamination, particularly in recycling
plants, because of improper handling. Hence, e-waste acts as a more
localized source of heavy metals and OPs, dominating in e-waste dumping

### Remediation of E-Waste-Induced Pollution
of sites contaminated with EOL electronics released pollutants forms
an indispensable part of ensuring environmental safety and the well-being
of the community. Knowledge about electronic waste composition can
assist with the selection and adoption of an appropriate method for
remediation. As electronic waste contains both valuable and hazardous
metals, proper handling and recycling methods need to be in place
to enable the recovery of valuable metals and inhibit the escape
of hazardous metals and other pollutants to prevent environmental
contamination. Various metallurgical procedures are followed for valuable
metal recovery, while several remediation methods based on physical,
chemical, and biological means exist to reduce e-scrap-associated
contamination. The choice and suitability of remediation techniques
are dependent on a varied set of factors, including expense, efficacy,
duration, general acceptability, suitability for high heavy metal
levels and/or mixed metal and organic wastes, toxicity, mobility,
and volume reduction. The ensuing sections
will focus on the remediation methods with biological means as an
effective solution for removing toxic substances released from WEEE.
Other methods will be discussed only briefly, since the primary objective
of this review is to outline the significance of biological methods
as an environmentally more viable option.

### Integrated Remediation Approach with Microbes and Plants
In addition to the utilization of microbes and plants in isolated
remediation attempts, several studies have also investigated the efficacy
of an integrated remediation approach using selected microbes in combination
with suitable plant species. Microbes not only contribute to improving
soil health by facilitating aggregate formation, organic matter,
macromolecule breakdown, and nutrient cycling, but also harbor unique
metabolic pathways for pollutant removal and degradation. In addition,
microbial inoculation at the contaminated sites can also positively
influence plant growth, thereby improving the success of phytoremediation.
Particularly, the plant symbiotic microorganisms, like plant growth-promoting
rhizobacteria (PGPR), mycorrhizal fungi (e.g., arbuscular mycorrhizal
fungi or AMF), and Actinomycetes, considerably boost plant growth
and aid in revegetation. These plant-microbe interactions can accelerate
the overall rate of pollutant removal.

years, and the unbridled production of electronic waste and its improper
management pose a great challenge in achieving this goal. In this
context, biological remediation, viz., bioremediation and phytoremediation,
presents a promising approach for remediating WEEE-contaminated soils.
However, as with any other method, even bioremediation and phytoremediation
are not completely free of challenges and come with several difficulties

applicability and efficacy. The foremost challenge is the complex
site conditions, such as varying soil characteristics, like soil type,
texture, pH, temperature, water content, and organic matter content.

become obsolete more quickly, the rate of trash generation increases.
Inadequate infrastructure and improper handling of e-waste can cause
the release of heavy metals and other toxic components into the environment.
Toxic substances and elements leach out from electronic waste, such
as persistent organic pollutants and heavy metals, and these elements
are concentrated in soil over time. This pollution negatively impacts
soil quality, potentially harming plants and ecosystems. While some
heavy metals may be used for plant growth in trace amounts, others
have no role, and elevated levels of both kinds can lead to toxicity,
affecting growth and development. Above a certain threshold, heavy
metals in plants can trigger the formation of ROS that can damage
the cellular structures and biomolecules. PCBs are also part of many
electronic equipment, and these organic substances are toxic to plants
and difficult to remediate from the soil.

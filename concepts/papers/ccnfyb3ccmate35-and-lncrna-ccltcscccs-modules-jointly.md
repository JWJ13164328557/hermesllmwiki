### Introduction
Al is a substance of the highest content following oxygen and silicon in the Earth's crust (Feng et al ., 2019 ). It mostly exists in the form of non‐toxic insoluble aluminosilicate or alumina in soil, but becomes toxic in the form of Al 3+ ions when soil pH is lower than 5 (Li and Johnson, 2016 ). Therefore, Al toxicity is often a major threat to crop productivity in acid soils (Kochian et al ., 2004 ; Ma, 2007 ). Due to industrial pollution or inappropriate agricultural practices, the proportion of acidic farmland has reached 40–50% worldwide and there is therefore growing concern about the toxicity of Al (Gupta et al ., 2013 ). Thus, it is critical to have a better understanding of the molecular processes in crop plants to assist in enhancing crop Al tolerance or Al toxicity resistance.
Pigeon pea is an important crop worldwide and a major source of protein for over 1 billion people in developing countries (Varshney et al ., 2011 ). In addition, it also provides great medicinal value (Gomes et al ., 2021 ) and the extracts or active ingredients of this crop are reported to have therapeutic effects on many diseases, including diabetes, hepatitis, measles, and dysentery (Das et al ., 2018 ; Liu et al ., 2010 ). Pigeon peas are mainly distributed in tropical and subtropical regions where soil often has low pH values and thereby frequently experiences Al stress (Daspute et al ., 2018 ). To cope with such Al stress, pigeon peas have evolved a high tolerance. Therefore, the analysis of its Al tolerance mechanism and the search for key Al tolerance genes will lay the molecular foundation for the selection and breeding of superior Al‐tolerant plant varieties.
Al toxicity can inhibit plant growth. It leads to rapid root growth inhibition, and subsequently, the leaves turn yellow and necrotic easily (Zhang et al ., 2019 ). ### Identification of CcMATE25 and CcMATE35 highly expressed in pigeon pea in response to aluminium stress
To explore the molecular basis of Al tolerance, we performed a genome‐wide transcriptome investigation on roots exposed to Al using Illumina high‐throughput mRNA sequencing (RNA‐seq) technology. Based on RNA‐seq data, we analysed the changes in expression of all CcMATEs under Al stress. The results revealed that the expression levels of 21 CcMATE genes were significantly increased in roots treated with Al stress for 12 h. Among them, the expression of CcMATE25 and CcMATE35 increased by approximately 2.5 times compared with the level detected before Al treatment. Furthermore, we quantified the expression levels of CcMATE25 and CcMATE35 under individual aluminium stress treatments and observed that they exhibited upregulation in response to aluminium stress (Figure S2 ).
Utilizing Clustal W and MEGA 6.0, we built a phylogenetic tree using the full‐length proteins of the CcMATE35, CcMATE25, and 12 previously described MATE proteins from 10 other species to define the evolutionary connections among pigeon pea MATE proteins. In addition, structural domain predictions for CcMATE25 and CcMATE35 proteins were performed at the PFAM and TMHMM websites. The CcMATE25 and CcMATE35 proteins were found to be structurally similar to the MATE proteins reported in other plants, both having a conserved MATE family domain and 12 transmembrane structural domains (Figure 1a,b ). According to evolutionary connections and comparative analyses, CcMATE25 and CcMATE35 belonged to the MATE family. MATE has been found in several plants to be a plasma membrane‐localized protein (Morita et al ., 2009 ; Omote et al ., 2006 ; Yokosho et al ., 2011 ). We conducted subcellular localization studies for CcMATE35 and CcMATE25 using tobacco transient transfection. We observed overlapping fluorescence patterns with FM4‐64 dye and AtCBL1 ‐mCherry, indicating their membrane localization. ### Compared with CcMATE25, CcMATE35 has a greater effect on enhancing the tolerance of pigeon pea to aluminium stress
To further investigate the molecular mechanism of high expression of CcMATE35 and CcMATE25 in response to Al stress in pigeon pea, we used the pigeon pea Hairy‐Root transgenic system established in previous studies to generate CcMATE35 overexpressed plants and CcMATE25 overexpressed plants, respectively ( CcMATE35 ‐OEs and CcMATE25 ‐OEs; Meng et al ., 2021 , 2019 ; Figure 2a ). We chose plants for Al stress treatment that had a greater than 2‐fold increase in target gene expression after transformation (Figure S3 ). Given our earlier screening of aluminium stress concentrations, we noted that 1 m m aluminium treatment significantly inhibited root elongation. Therefore, we opted to utilize a 1 m m aluminium concentration for our stress treatment (Figure S1 ). We conducted Evan's Blue staining to assess cell death in root tip cells. As illustrated in Figure 2b,c , aluminium treatment led to increased cell staining. Notably, under aluminium stress, root tip staining was partially alleviated in CcMATE35 ‐OEs and CcMATE25 ‐OEs. Furthermore, root growth inhibition is a typical indicator of aluminium plant toxicity. As depicted in Figure 2d , aluminium significantly suppressed root elongation, with this inhibition partially alleviated in CcMATE25 ‐OEs and CcMATE35 ‐OEs. Similarly, under aluminium stress, there was an increased accumulation of callose content in the root tips of CcMATE25 ‐OEs and CcMATE35 ‐OEs (Figure 2e ). As a result, we hypothesized that CcMATE35 and CcMATE25 may have an enhancement impact on Al stress resistance in pigeon pea. To further verify the citrate transport role of CcMATE25 and CcMATE35, we evaluated whether citrate release was changed in CcMATE25 ‐OEs and CcMATE35 ‐OEs. The findings indicated that the CcMATE35 ‐OEs and CcMATE25 ‐OEs secreted much more citrate than the control under both –Al and +Al settings; however, the CcMATE25 ‐OEs released significantly less citrate than the CcMATE35 ‐OEs under +Al conditions (Figure 2e ).

### Screening for transcription factors that may regulate the expression of CcMATE35 under Al stress
Transcription factors are a group of proteins that bind to specific sequences upstream of the 5′ end of a gene, thus ensuring that the target gene is expressed at a specific intensity, time and space. Transcription factors play an important role in the regulation of plant growth and response to the external environment, and they are now a hot topic in biology research (Strader et al ., 2022 ). Our research above shows that CcMATE35 enhanced Al tolerance in pigeon pea through increased expression and thus enhanced release of citrate. In most cases, the increased expression of functional genes like CcMATE35 might regulated by certain transcription factors. Therefore, in order to further explore the regulatory mechanism of CcMATE35 in Al tolerance in pigeon pea, we proposed to screen for transcription factors that were expressed in response to Al stress and have the same expression pattern as CcMATE35 as candidate regulatory genes. We screened all transcription factors that were differentially expressed in response to Al stress using transcriptome data (Table S2 ). Then, the Mev software was used to group these transcription factors according to their dynamic regulation patterns. The distance measure used is Euclidean distance, and the clustering method is K‐means clustering. Using the FPKM (Fragments Per Kilobase of exon model per Million mapped reads) of each gene at three time points, we clustered these transcription factors into eight clusters (Figure S5 ). We focused on transcription factors in cluster 1, since their expression patterns were comparable with those of CcMATE35 during Al stress, based on the clustering data (Figure 3a ). After 12 h of Al stress, the expression of CcICE1 , CcE2FE3 , CcNAC47 , CcNFYB3 , and CcMADS23 rose more than fourfold in Cluster 1 (Figure 3b ). ### CcLTCS increased CcCS expression to promote citrate synthesis and thus enhance Al tolerance in pigeon pea
Plants can enhance their Al tolerance by increasing their efflux citrate content. Thus, in addition to enhancing the expression of citric acid transporters, does increasing citric acid synthesis also play an essential role? To study the aforementioned, we analysed the transcriptome data of TCA cycle enzyme genes under Al stress. We discovered that as the duration of aluminium stress increased, the number of differentially significantly expressed genes in the TCA cycle pathway increased as well (Figure 7a ). Among these genes, Only CcCS expression was proportional to the duration of Al stress (Figure 7b ). To investigate the function of CcCS in Al stress, we generated CcCS ‐overexpressed and ‐repressed plants, CcCS ‐OEs and CcCS ‐RNAis (Figure 8a,b ). We discovered that overexpression of the CcCS in pigeon pea improved the plant's resistance to Al stress. Under Al stress, CcCS ‐OEs plants outperformed Controls, with significant increases in chlorophyll content in leaves, proline content, citrate content, and citrate release content in roots. Meanwhile, CcCS ‐RNAis exhibited the opposite trends in chlorophyll content, proline content, and citrate release content (Figure 8c ; Figure S10 ).

### Synergistic effect of citric acid synthesis and efflux in Al tolerance of pigeon pea

To explore the relationship between citrate synthesis and efflux under Al stress, we transformed two vectors into pigeon peas concurrently to further investigate the regulatory role of CcLTCS and CcNFYB3 . ### Discussion
Al toxicity affects plant root growth as well as the photosynthesis of leaves. Numerous studies have shown that Al stress can damage the chloroplast tephragm and reduce the chlorophyll content of plants, which ultimately affects photosynthesis severely (Cao et al ., 2011 ; Dawood et al ., 2012 ; Jiang et al ., 2008 ). The inhibition of root elongation is positively correlated with the accumulation of Al in the root system, and the root tip is the most sensitive part of the plant, being the first to come into direct contact with Al ions in acidic media. In addition to inhibiting root growth, the Al stress can lead to increased root proline content in the plant to scavenge oxygen radicals and enhance the stability of cell membranes (Eticha et al ., 2005 ; Giannakoula et al ., 2010 ). Secretion of organic acid from plant root tips is a well‐accepted mechanism for plant tolerance to Al toxicity because these organic acids can chelate the metal in the rhizosphere to prevent its absorption by roots (Kochian et al ., 2004 ). Organic acids are metabolically active solutes and play crucial roles in multiple cellular functions, such as osmotic adjustment and the balance of cation concentration (Nakagawa et al ., 2008 ). In plants, efflux of organic acid anions from root tips is essential in plant resistance against Al stress (Ikka et al ., 2013 ). Citric acid is an important organic acid anions efflux produced from the rhizosphere of a considerable number of plant species (Lawrence et al ., 2007 ; Zajaczkowska et al ., 2020 ). Our study reveals that Al stress induces leaf chlorosis and inhibits root elongation in pigeon peas, while it does not exert a significant impact on shoot height (Figures S9 , S15 and S16 ). And our research identified a key regulatory gene, CcMATE35 through regulating efflux of citrate from pigeon pea to cope with Al stress. ### Measurement of citrate concentration
The root tips of pigeon pea seedlings were rinsed with deionized water, and the root tips (0–2 cm) were cut, weighed, and immediately grounded. The supernatant obtained by ultrasonication and centrifugation was used for citrate analysis (Chen et al ., 2012 ; Delhaize et al ., 1993 ).

### Subcellular localization of CcMATE proteins
The pROK2 vector plasmid carrying the CcMATEs was transformed into GV3101 Agrobacterium tumefaciens strain (Yang et al ., 2020 ). Centrifuged the Agrobacterium solution to remove the supernatant, then resuspended the pellet in the buffer (including 0.2 m m acetosyringone, 10 m m MgCl 2 , and 10 m m MES).

### Abstract
In industrial production, the yield of desired targets derived from carbon sources is frequently diminished by the competitive influence of cellular metabolism within microbial cell factories. The bioproduction of l -valine exemplifies a classic process that is confronted with such a dilemma, substantially hindering its economic industrial-scale production. In this study, we aim to engineer a cell factory capable of efficiently synthesizing l -valine with high yield by minimizing the consumption of its precursor pyruvate through the TCA cycle under oxygen-limited conditions. Metabolic engineering-based adaptive laboratory evolution (ALE) under oxygen-limited conditions resulted in the development of an evolved strain ALE2-40 with better cell growth and enhanced l -valine yield. Through comparative omics analysis and validation experiments, it was uncovered that during the ALE process, both pyruvate dehydrogenase activity and NADH availability were significantly improved. Moreover, beneficial targets have the potential to contribute to the NADH and ATP pools, thereby further promoting l -valine synthesis. Based on these results, reverse engineering of the evolved strain ALE2-40 was further conducted. Ultimately, the final strain VAL19 demonstrated remarkable performance, achieving an impressive l -valine titer of 93.7 g/L within 28 h in a 5-L bioreactor under oxygen-limited conditions, with a remarkable yield of 60.4% from glucose—equivalent to 92.9% of the theoretical yield—a
### 1. Introduction
In nature, microorganisms exist in environments that may or may not contain oxygen. Consequently, they usually rely on either the respiratory chain or the substrate-level phosphorylation to generate the energy necessary for sustaining viability. In comparison, the efficiency of ATP production via the respiratory chain usually significantly surpasses that achieved through substrate-level phosphorylation [ 1 ]. Nevertheless, the operation of the respiratory chain necessitates the availability of oxygen, and meanwhile, the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle becomes active, which poses a significant competing pathway for the synthesis of chemicals that utilize pyruvate as a precursor [ 2 ]. Thus, for the production of pyruvate or derivatives using facultative anaerobic bacteria such as Escherichia coli and Corynebacterium glutamicum , efforts have been made to shift the fermentation process from aerobic conditions to oxygen-limited or anaerobic conditions, aiming to minimize the consumption of pyruvate via the TCA cycle, and to increase the yield of target products from carbon sources [ [3] , [4] , [5] ]. ### 4. Discussion
In the construction of microbial cell factories, the quest for achieving high titer, yield, and productivity stands as the most prevalent objective within laboratory settings. However, in the actual industrial application, high titer does not invariably represent the optimal target. For instance, certain chemicals exhibit low solubility, and an excessively high titer can result in crystal precipitation. Such precipitation poses a significant obstacle to downstream purification processes. In such scenarios, a combination of higher yield and productivity, along with an appropriate titer, proves to be more applicable and economically efficient. The production of l -valine is a typical process that is confronted with such issue, as an elevated titer is often accompanied by the issue of l -valine crystallization. Under such circumstances, the increase of yield derived from glucose serves as a critical bottleneck and a primary concern for reducing production costs.
Due to the function of TCA cycle under aerobic conditions, the divert of pyruvate to TCA cycle has been a main factor limiting the yield of l -valine. In this study, the adaptive evolution of an engineered l -valine producing strain in sealed bottle filled with fermentation medium improved the cell growth under oxygen-limited conditions and significantly benefited the yield of l -valine from glucose. It demonstrated that the “live or die” stress constitutes a direct and highly effective driving force for evolution. After

### Abstract
In industrial production, the yield of desired targets derived from carbon sources is frequently diminished by the competitive influence of cellular metabolism within microbial cell factories. The bioproduction of l -valine exemplifies a classic process that is confronted with such a dilemma, substantially hindering its economic industrial-scale production. In this study, we aim to engineer a cell factory capable of efficiently synthesizing l -valine with high yield by minimizing the consumption of its precursor pyruvate through the TCA cycle under oxygen-limited conditions. Metabolic engineering-based adaptive laboratory evolution (ALE) under oxygen-limited conditions resulted in the development of an evolved strain ALE2-40 with better cell growth and enhanced l -valine yield. Through comparative omics analysis and validation experiments, it was uncovered that during the ALE process, both pyruvate dehydrogenase activity and NADH availability were significantly improved. Moreover, beneficial targets have the potential to contribute to the NADH and ATP pools, thereby further promoting l -valine synthesis. Based on these results, reverse engineering of the evolved strain ALE2-40 was further conducted. Ultimately, the final strain VAL19 demonstrated remarkable performance, achieving an impressive l -valine titer of 93.7 g/L within 28 h in a 5-L bioreactor under oxygen-limited conditions, with a remarkable yield of 60.4% from glucose—equivalent to 92.9% of the theoretical yield—and a productivity of 3.35 g/L/h. These results set a new benchmark for the fermentative production of l -valine, with the highest yield and productivity reported so far.

### 2.4. Adaptive laboratory evolution under oxygen-limited conditions
To enhance the strain's tolerance to oxygen-limited conditions and promote high-yield l -valine production, we employed ALE to optimize its growth performance when subjected to the stress of oxygen-limited condition. A tightly sealed reagent bottle filled with fermentation medium was employed to establish an oxygen-deprived environment. The culture medium used in ALE contained 20 g/L glucose, 1 g/L yeast extract, 4 g/L citrate, 6 g/L K 2 HPO 4 , 5 g/L (NH 4 ) 2 SO 4 , 1 g/L MgSO 4 ·7H 2 O, 60 mg/L FeSO 4 ·7H 2 O, 5 mg/L MnSO 4 ·H 2 O, 0.5 mg/L V H , 20 g/L MOPS to sustain pH stability, and 8 mg/L phenol red. After the ALE experiment, an initial fermentation screening of the evolved strains was conducted in 96-deep-well plates. The seed culture was carried out at a temperature of 37 °C and a shaking rate of 220 rpm for 12 h. ### 3.1. Metabolic engineering of the starting strain to build a suitable chassis for adaptive evolution under oxygen-limited conditions
In our previous study, an l -valine-hyperproducing strain designated as VAL0 was constructed, which demonstrated the capacity for efficient l -valine production through a two-stage fermentation process, with similar performance as our previously reported VHY18 strain [ 2 ]. Specifically, during the first-stage fermentation, aerobic conditions were required to facilitate fast cell growth. Subsequently, during the second-stage oxygen-limited fermentation, l -valine was produced with high yield. Although the decoupling of cell growth and production resulted in an enhanced l -valine yield compared to the conventional aerobic fermentation, the two-stage fermentation approach still encountered challenges, including a prolonged fermentation duration and a relatively low yield during the aerobic cell growth phase. To address these problems, we aim to employ adaptive laboratory evolution (ALE) to establish a single-stage, oxygen-limited fermentation process and curtail the bypass consumption of the precursor pyruvate via the TCA cycle. However, several key issues must be taken into consideration before conducting ALE.

### 3.7. Fed-batch fermentation of the engineered strains in a 5-L bioreactor under oxygen-limited conditions
To conduct a thorough assessment of the fermentation performance of the engineered strain VAL18 and VAL19, 5-L bioreactor fermentation was carried out. An oxygen-limited fermentation condition was created with the stirrer speed setting to mere 300 rpm and aeration setting to as low as 0.2 L/min. The results are shown in Fig. 6 E (VAL18) and Fig. 6 F (VAL19). Owing to the limited solubility of l -valine, the fermentation process was terminated at 28 h, when the concentration of l -valine reached its saturation point and began to precipitate ( Fig. 6 E). It is noteworthy that the cell growth of strain VAL19 outperformed that of VAL18 at 12 h and 16 h, while exhibited a decline in comparison to that of VAL18 after 24 h. The final biomass levels (measured as OD 600 ) for strains VAL18 and VAL19 were 22.5 and 20.5, respectively. This phenomenon is most probably attributable to the fact that, during the cell growth phase, P fliC facilitates transcriptional initiation with a certain strength, and as cell growth progresses into the late phase, it exerts a suppressive effect on transcriptional initiation. Consequently, a partial decoupling of cell growth from l -valine synthesis was achieved, contributing to the enhanced synthesis of l -valine from glucose in VAL19. The l -valine titer in strain VAL19 reached a remarkable 93.7 g/L. This value represents a 3.4% increase compared to the l -valine titer of strain VAL18, which stood at 90.6 g/L. Moreover, referring to Fig. 3 , when compared with VAL06 (78.5 g/L) and ALE2-40 (85.4 g/L), the l -valine titer in VAL19 was 19.4% and 9.7% higher, respectively. In addition, strain VAL19 demonstrated a yield as high as 60.4%, which corresponds to 92.9% of the theoretical yield of 65% from glucose, outperforming VAL18's yield of 59.2%.

### 4. Discussion
In the construction of microbial cell factories, the quest for achieving high titer, yield, and productivity stands as the most prevalent objective within laboratory settings. However, in the actual industrial application, high titer does not invariably represent the optimal target. For instance, certain chemicals exhibit low solubility, and an excessively high titer can result in crystal precipitation. Such precipitation poses a significant obstacle to downstream purification processes. In such scenarios, a combination of higher yield and productivity, along with an appropriate titer, proves to be more applicable and economically efficient. The production of l -valine is a typical process that is confronted with such issue, as an elevated titer is often accompanied by the issue of l -valine crystallization. Under such circumstances, the increase of yield derived from glucose serves as a critical bottleneck and a primary concern for reducing production costs.
Due to the function of TCA cycle under aerobic conditions, the divert of pyruvate to TCA cycle has been a main factor limiting the yield of l -valine. In this study, the adaptive evolution of an engineered l -valine producing strain in sealed bottle filled with fermentation medium improved the cell growth under oxygen-limited conditions and significantly benefited the yield of l -valine from glucose. It demonstrated that the “live or die” stress constitutes a direct and highly effective driving force for evolution. After the ALE experiment, strain ALE2-40 acquired both better cell growth and enhanced l -valine production. To our surprise, the genomic mutations identified were limited to merely four single-nucleotide variants. Notably, the mutation in the lpd gene has been validated to effectively elevate both the NADH/NAD + ratio and the activity of pyruvate dehydrogenase. ### Abstract
In industrial production, the yield of desired targets derived from carbon sources is frequently diminished by the competitive influence of cellular metabolism within microbial cell factories. The bioproduction of l -valine exemplifies a classic process that is confronted with such a dilemma, substantially hindering its economic industrial-scale production. In this study, we aim to engineer a cell factory capable of efficiently synthesizing l -valine with high yield by minimizing the consumption of its precursor pyruvate through the TCA cycle under oxygen-limited conditions. Metabolic engineering-based adaptive laboratory evolution (ALE) under oxygen-limited conditions resulted in the development of an evolved strain ALE2-40 with better cell growth and enhanced l -valine yield. Through comparative omics analysis and validation experiments, it was uncovered that during the ALE process, both pyruvate dehydrogenase activity and NADH availability were significantly improved. Moreover, beneficial targets have the potential to contribute to the NADH and ATP pools, thereby further promoting l -valine synthesis. Based on these results, reverse engineering of the evolved strain ALE2-40 was further conducted. Ultimately, the final strain VAL19 demonstrated remarkable performance, achieving an impressive l -valine titer of 93.7 g/L within 28 h in a 5-L bioreactor under oxygen-limited conditions, with a remarkable yield of 60.4% from glucose—equivalent to 92.9% of the theoretical yield—and a productivity of 3.35 g/L/h. These results set a new benchmark for the fermentative production of l -valine, with the highest yield and productivity reported so far.

### 1. Introduction
In nature, microorganisms exist in environments that may or may not contain oxygen. Consequently, they usually rely on either the respiratory chain or the substrate-level phosphorylation to generate the energy necessary for sustaining viability. In comparison, the efficiency of ATP production via the respiratory chain usually significantly surpasses that achieved through substrate-level phosphorylation [ 1 ]. Nevertheless, the operation of the respiratory chain necessitates the availability of oxygen, and meanwhile, the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle becomes active, which poses a significant competing pathway for the synthesis of chemicals that utilize pyruvate as a precursor [ 2 ]. Thus, for the production of pyruvate or derivatives using facultative anaerobic bacteria such as Escherichia coli and Corynebacterium glutamicum , efforts have been made to shift the fermentation process from aerobic conditions to oxygen-limited or anaerobic conditions, aiming to minimize the consumption of pyruvate via the TCA cycle, and to increase the yield of target products from carbon sources [ [3] , [4] , [5] ]. Under oxygen-limited or anaerobic conditions, the re-oxidation of nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NADH) generated during glycolysis is vital for maintaining cofactor balance [ 6 ], otherwise, cell growth and the biosynthesis of target products may be impeded due to the accumulation of fermentative byproducts such as acetic acid, lactic acid and succinic acid [ 6 ]. A critical challenge in the efficient production of pyruvate and derivatives lies in rewiring intracellular metabolic pathways to facilitate high-yield production under oxygen-limited or anaerobic fermentation conditions [ 7 ]. The rewiring of the metabolic pathway necessitates achieving an equilibrium between the central carbon metabolism and the synthetic pathway, as well as ensuring cofactor balance [ 8 ].

### 2.4. Adaptive laboratory evolution under oxygen-limited conditions
To enhance the strain's tolerance to oxygen-limited conditions and promote high-yield l -valine production, we employed ALE to optimize its growth performance when subjected to the stress of oxygen-limited condition. A tightly sealed reagent bottle filled with fermentation medium was employed to establish an oxygen-deprived environment. The culture medium used in ALE contained 20 g/L glucose, 1 g/L yeast extract, 4 g/L citrate, 6 g/L K 2 HPO 4 , 5 g/L (NH 4 ) 2 SO 4 , 1 g/L MgSO 4 ·7H 2 O, 60 mg/L FeSO 4 ·7H 2 O, 5 mg/L MnSO 4 ·H 2 O, 0.5 mg/L V H , 20 g/L MOPS to sustain pH stability, and 8 mg/L phenol red. During serial-passage, a 1-mL aliquot of the cell culture was transferred to a fresh reagent bottle. The culture was then incubated at 37 °C, with continuous shaking at a rate of 220 rpm.

### 2.5. Oxygen-limited fermentation of the ALE-derived engineered strains
After the ALE experiment, an initial fermentation screening of the evolved strains was conducted in 96-deep-well plates. Single colonies were picked using sterile toothpicks from LB solid plates and then cultured in 96 deep-well plates containing 450 μL LB medium for seed culture. The seed culture was carried out at a temperature of 37 °C and a shaking rate of 220 rpm for 12 h. Following which, the seed culture was transferred to a new 96 deep-well plate containing 450 μL fermentation medium and added glucose solution (60%, w/v) to a final concentration of 10 g/L. The 96 deep-well plate was sealed with a sealing mat to establish an oxygen-limited condition. The fermentation process was conducted at 37 °C with a shaking rate of 220 rpm.

### 3.1. Metabolic engineering of the starting strain to build a suitable chassis for adaptive evolution under oxygen-limited conditions
In our previous study, an l -valine-hyperproducing strain designated as VAL0 was constructed, which demonstrated the capacity for efficient l -valine production through a two-stage fermentation process, with similar performance as our previously reported VHY18 strain [ 2 ]. Specifically, during the first-stage fermentation, aerobic conditions were required to facilitate fast cell growth. Subsequently, during the second-stage oxygen-limited fermentation, l -valine was produced with high yield. Although the decoupling of cell growth and production resulted in an enhanced l -valine yield compared to the conventional aerobic fermentation, the two-stage fermentation approach still encountered challenges, including a prolonged fermentation duration and a relatively low yield during the aerobic cell growth phase. To address these problems, we aim to employ adaptive laboratory evolution (ALE) to establish a single-stage, oxygen-limited fermentation process and curtail the bypass consumption of the precursor pyruvate via the TCA cycle. However, several key issues must be taken into consideration before conducting ALE.
Firstly, to facilitate the transition from aerobic fermentation to oxygen-limited fermentation, it is imperative to block the acetic acid synthesis pathway in the starting strain. This is because E. coli strains inherently possess a tendency to accumulate acetic acid as a by-product when cultivated under anaerobic or oxygen-limited conditions with abundant glucose. Specifically, gene poxB encoding the pyruvate dehydrogenase, gene ackA encoding the acetate kinase, gene tdcD encoding the propionate kinase, and pta encoding the phosphate acetyltransferase, were sequentially knocked out in VAL0, constructing strains VAL01, VAL02, VAL03, and VAL04, respectively. ### 3.3. Fermentation assessment of the evolved strain in a 5-L bioreactor
The fermentation performance of the evolved strain ALE2-40 was further assessed in a 5-L bioreactor under oxygen-limited conditions, with the starting strain VAL06 as the control under the same conditions ( Fig. 3 ). As depicted in Fig. 3 , throughout the overall fermentation process, both the cell growth and l -valine production of ALE2-40 demonstrated a marked enhancement in comparison to VAL06, achieving a final l -valine titer of 85.4 g/L at 28 h ( Fig. 3 B), whereas, at the same time, VAL06 attained an l -valine titer of 78.5 g/L ( Fig. 3 A). Strain ALE2-40 attained its peak OD 600 value of 25.9 at 20 h, whereas strain VAL06 reached a comparable OD 600 level of 25.1 up to 28 h of fermentation. Despite of the fact that enhanced cell growth would unavoidably consume more carbon sources, the l -valine yield derived from the total consumed glucose utilized by ALE2-40 still surpassed that of VAL06. To be precise, ALE2-40 attained an l -valine yield of 58.0% from glucose, marking a 3.8% increase over the yield of 55.9% observed in VAL06. Furthermore, no notable accumulation of frequently encountered organic acids, such as acetic acid and succinic acid, was detected. These results clearly indicate that, as a result of the ALE experiment, the enhanced cell growth exhibited by strain ALE2-40 significantly contributed to the efficient l -valine production under oxygen-limited conditions. As shown in Fig. 3 B, l -valine crystals began to precipitate from the fermentation broth of ALE2-40 at 28 h, indicating the end of the fermentation process to prevent imposing an additional burden on the following extraction procedure. Thus, for the 5-L bioreactor fermentation in this study, the fermentation period was set at 28 h. ### 3.6. Transcriptomics-guided reverse metabolic engineering
Through comparative transcriptomic analysis, differentially expressed genes within strain ALE2-40 were identified, and their impact on l -valine production was rigorously validated. The findings revealed that the upregulation of genes such as ybcL , yihA , and napF , as well as the downregulation of rsxE , speC , yjiG , argI , and fimG , significantly enhanced l -valine titer. Building on these insights, reverse metabolic engineering strategies were implemented to further enhance the fermentation performance of strain ALE2-40. Specifically, the P J23105 promoter was employed to overexpress the upregulated genes, while downregulated genes underwent systematic pairwise deletion analyses to identify optimal combinations for enhancing l -valine production synergistically.
First, the overexpression of previously identified upregulated genes was carried out in strain ALE2-40. Specifically, the ybcL , yihA , and napF genes were individually overexpressed in the genome of ALE2-40, resulting in the construction of strains VAL09 (ALE2-40, yciQ ::P J23105 - ybcL ), VAL10 (ALE2-40, ylbE ::P J23105 - yihA ), and VAL11 (ALE2-40, gapC ::P J23105 - napF ). The results of oxygen-limited shake-flask fermentation for these strains are depicted in Fig. 6 A. The l -valine titers recorded for VAL09, VAL10, and VAL11 were 15.9, 27.6, and 27.0 g/L, respectively, revealing no significant enhancement in comparison to the control strain ALE2-40, which yielded 26.1 g/L l -valine. ### 3.7. Fed-batch fermentation of the engineered strains in a 5-L bioreactor under oxygen-limited conditions
To conduct a thorough assessment of the fermentation performance of the engineered strain VAL18 and VAL19, 5-L bioreactor fermentation was carried out. An oxygen-limited fermentation condition was created with the stirrer speed setting to mere 300 rpm and aeration setting to as low as 0.2 L/min. The results are shown in Fig. 6 E (VAL18) and Fig. 6 F (VAL19). Owing to the limited solubility of l -valine, the fermentation process was terminated at 28 h, when the concentration of l -valine reached its saturation point and began to precipitate ( Fig. 6 E). It is noteworthy that the cell growth of strain VAL19 outperformed that of VAL18 at 12 h and 16 h, while exhibited a decline in comparison to that of VAL18 after 24 h. The final biomass levels (measured as OD 600 ) for strains VAL18 and VAL19 were 22.5 and 20.5, respectively. This phenomenon is most probably attributable to the fact that, during the cell growth phase, P fliC facilitates transcriptional initiation with a certain strength, and as cell growth progresses into the late phase, it exerts a suppressive effect on transcriptional initiation. Consequently, a partial decoupling of cell growth from l -valine synthesis was achieved, contributing to the enhanced synthesis of l -valine from glucose in VAL19. The l -valine titer in strain VAL19 reached a remarkable 93.7 g/L. This value represents a 3.4% increase compared to the l -valine titer of strain VAL18, which stood at 90.6 g/L. Moreover, referring to Fig. 3 , when compared with VAL06 (78.5 g/L) and ALE2-40 (85.4 g/L), the l -valine titer in VAL19 was 19.4% and 9.7% higher, respectively. In addition, strain VAL19 demonstrated a yield as high as 60.4%, which corresponds to 92.9% of the theoretical yield of 65% from glucose, outperforming VAL18's yield of 59.2%.

### 4. Discussion
In the construction of microbial cell factories, the quest for achieving high titer, yield, and productivity stands as the most prevalent objective within laboratory settings. However, in the actual industrial application, high titer does not invariably represent the optimal target. For instance, certain chemicals exhibit low solubility, and an excessively high titer can result in crystal precipitation. Such precipitation poses a significant obstacle to downstream purification processes. In such scenarios, a combination of higher yield and productivity, along with an appropriate titer, proves to be more applicable and economically efficient. The production of l -valine is a typical process that is confronted with such issue, as an elevated titer is often accompanied by the issue of l -valine crystallization. Under such circumstances, the increase of yield derived from glucose serves as a critical bottleneck and a primary concern for reducing production costs.
Due to the function of TCA cycle under aerobic conditions, the divert of pyruvate to TCA cycle has been a main factor limiting the yield of l -valine. In this study, the adaptive evolution of an engineered l -valine producing strain in sealed bottle filled with fermentation medium improved the cell growth under oxygen-limited conditions and significantly benefited the yield of l -valine from glucose. It demonstrated that the “live or die” stress constitutes a direct and highly effective driving force for evolution. After the ALE experiment, strain ALE2-40 acquired both better cell growth and enhanced l -valine production. To our surprise, the genomic mutations identified were limited to merely four single-nucleotide variants. Notably, the mutation in the lpd gene has been validated to effectively elevate both the NADH/NAD + ratio and the activity of pyruvate dehydrogenase. ### Abstract
In industrial production, the yield of desired targets derived from carbon sources is frequently diminished by the competitive influence of cellular metabolism within microbial cell factories. The bioproduction of l -valine exemplifies a classic process that is confronted with such a dilemma, substantially hindering its economic industrial-scale production. In this study, we aim to engineer a cell factory capable of efficiently synthesizing l -valine with high yield by minimizing the consumption of its precursor pyruvate through the TCA cycle under oxygen-limited conditions. Metabolic engineering-based adaptive laboratory evolution (ALE) under oxygen-limited conditions resulted in the development of an evolved strain ALE2-40 with better cell growth and enhanced l -valine yield. Through comparative omics analysis and validation experiments, it was uncovered that during the ALE process, both pyruvate dehydrogenase activity and NADH availability were significantly improved. Moreover, beneficial targets have the potential to contribute to the NADH and ATP pools, thereby further promoting l -valine synthesis. Based on these results, reverse engineering of the evolved strain ALE2-40 was further conducted. Ultimately, the final strain VAL19 demonstrated remarkable performance, achieving an impressive l -valine titer of 93.7 g/L within 28 h in a 5-L bioreactor under oxygen-limited conditions, with a remarkable yield of 60.4% from glucose—equivalent to 92.9% of the theoretical yield—and a productivity of 3.35 g/L/h. These results set a new benchmark for the fermentative production of l -valine, with the highest yield and productivity reported so far.

### 1. Introduction
In nature, microorganisms exist in environments that may or may not contain oxygen. Consequently, they usually rely on either the respiratory chain or the substrate-level phosphorylation to generate the energy necessary for sustaining viability. In comparison, the efficiency of ATP production via the respiratory chain usually significantly surpasses that achieved through substrate-level phosphorylation [ 1 ]. Nevertheless, the operation of the respiratory chain necessitates the availability of oxygen, and meanwhile, the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle becomes active, which poses a significant competing pathway for the synthesis of chemicals that utilize pyruvate as a precursor [ 2 ]. Thus, for the production of pyruvate or derivatives using facultative anaerobic bacteria such as Escherichia coli and Corynebacterium glutamicum , efforts have been made to shift the fermentation process from aerobic conditions to oxygen-limited or anaerobic conditions, aiming to minimize the consumption of pyruvate via the TCA cycle, and to increase the yield of target products from carbon sources [ [3] , [4] , [5] ]. Under oxygen-limited or anaerobic conditions, the re-oxidation of nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NADH) generated during glycolysis is vital for maintaining cofactor balance [ 6 ], otherwise, cell growth and the biosynthesis of target products may be impeded due to the accumulation of fermentative byproducts such as acetic acid, lactic acid and succinic acid [ 6 ]. A critical challenge in the efficient production of pyruvate and derivatives lies in rewiring intracellular metabolic pathways to facilitate high-yield production under oxygen-limited or anaerobic fermentation conditions [ 7 ]. The rewiring of the metabolic pathway necessitates achieving an equilibrium between the central carbon metabolism and the synthetic pathway, as well as ensuring cofactor balance [ 8 ].

### 2.4. Adaptive laboratory evolution under oxygen-limited conditions
To enhance the strain's tolerance to oxygen-limited conditions and promote high-yield l -valine production, we employed ALE to optimize its growth performance when subjected to the stress of oxygen-limited condition. A tightly sealed reagent bottle filled with fermentation medium was employed to establish an oxygen-deprived environment. The culture medium used in ALE contained 20 g/L glucose, 1 g/L yeast extract, 4 g/L citrate, 6 g/L K 2 HPO 4 , 5 g/L (NH 4 ) 2 SO 4 , 1 g/L MgSO 4 ·7H 2 O, 60 mg/L FeSO 4 ·7H 2 O, 5 mg/L MnSO 4 ·H 2 O, 0.5 mg/L V H , 20 g/L MOPS to sustain pH stability, and 8 mg/L phenol red. During serial-passage, a 1-mL aliquot of the cell culture was transferred to a fresh reagent bottle. The culture was then incubated at 37 °C, with continuous shaking at a rate of 220 rpm.

### 2.5. Oxygen-limited fermentation of the ALE-derived engineered strains
After the ALE experiment, an initial fermentation screening of the evolved strains was conducted in 96-deep-well plates. Single colonies were picked using sterile toothpicks from LB solid plates and then cultured in 96 deep-well plates containing 450 μL LB medium for seed culture. The seed culture was carried out at a temperature of 37 °C and a shaking rate of 220 rpm for 12 h. Following which, the seed culture was transferred to a new 96 deep-well plate containing 450 μL fermentation medium and added glucose solution (60%, w/v) to a final concentration of 10 g/L. The 96 deep-well plate was sealed with a sealing mat to establish an oxygen-limited condition. The fermentation process was conducted at 37 °C with a shaking rate of 220 rpm.

### 3.1. Metabolic engineering of the starting strain to build a suitable chassis for adaptive evolution under oxygen-limited conditions
In our previous study, an l -valine-hyperproducing strain designated as VAL0 was constructed, which demonstrated the capacity for efficient l -valine production through a two-stage fermentation process, with similar performance as our previously reported VHY18 strain [ 2 ]. Specifically, during the first-stage fermentation, aerobic conditions were required to facilitate fast cell growth. Subsequently, during the second-stage oxygen-limited fermentation, l -valine was produced with high yield. Although the decoupling of cell growth and production resulted in an enhanced l -valine yield compared to the conventional aerobic fermentation, the two-stage fermentation approach still encountered challenges, including a prolonged fermentation duration and a relatively low yield during the aerobic cell growth phase. To address these problems, we aim to employ adaptive laboratory evolution (ALE) to establish a single-stage, oxygen-limited fermentation process and curtail the bypass consumption of the precursor pyruvate via the TCA cycle. However, several key issues must be taken into consideration before conducting ALE.
Firstly, to facilitate the transition from aerobic fermentation to oxygen-limited fermentation, it is imperative to block the acetic acid synthesis pathway in the starting strain. This is because E. coli strains inherently possess a tendency to accumulate acetic acid as a by-product when cultivated under anaerobic or oxygen-limited conditions with abundant glucose. Specifically, gene poxB encoding the pyruvate dehydrogenase, gene ackA encoding the acetate kinase, gene tdcD encoding the propionate kinase, and pta encoding the phosphate acetyltransferase, were sequentially knocked out in VAL0, constructing strains VAL01, VAL02, VAL03, and VAL04, respectively. ### 3.3. Fermentation assessment of the evolved strain in a 5-L bioreactor

### 3.6. Transcriptomics-guided reverse metabolic engineering

### 3.7. Fed-batch fermentation of the engineered strains in a 5-L bioreactor under oxygen-limited conditions
To conduct a thorough assessment of the fermentation performance of the engineered strain VAL18 and VAL19, 5-L bioreactor fermentation was carried out. An oxygen-limited fermentation condition was created with the stirrer speed setting to mere 300 rpm and aeration setting to as low as 0.2 L/min. The results are shown in Fig. 6 E (VAL18) and Fig. 6 F (VAL19). Owing to the limited solubility of l -valine, the fermentation process was terminated at 28 h, when the concentration of l -valine reached its saturation point and began to precipitate ( Fig. 6 E). It is noteworthy that the cell growth of strain VAL19 outperformed that of VAL18 at 12 h and 16 h, while exhibited a decline in comparison to that of VAL18 after 24 h. The final biomass levels (measured as OD 600 ) for strains VAL18 and VAL19 were 22.5 and 20.5, respectively. This phenomenon is most probably attributable to the fact that, during the cell growth phase, P fliC facilitates transcriptional initiation with a certain strength, and as cell growth progresses into the late phase, it exerts a suppressive effect on transcriptional initiation. Consequently, a partial decoupling of cell growth from l -valine synthesis was achieved, contributing to the enhanced synthesis of l -valine from glucose in VAL19. The l -valine titer in strain VAL19 reached a remarkable 93.7 g/L. This value represents a 3.4% increase compared to the l -valine titer of strain VAL18, which stood at 90.6 g/L. Moreover, referring to Fig. 3 , when compared with VAL06 (78.5 g/L) and ALE2-40 (85.4 g/L), the l -valine titer in VAL19 was 19.4% and 9.7% higher, respectively. In addition, strain VAL19 demonstrated a yield as high as 60.4%, which corresponds to 92.9% of the theoretical yield of 65% from glucose, outperforming VAL18's yield of 59.2%.

### 4. Discussion
In the construction of microbial cell factories, the quest for achieving high titer, yield, and productivity stands as the most prevalent objective within laboratory settings. However, in the actual industrial application, high titer does not invariably represent the optimal target. For instance, certain chemicals exhibit low solubility, and an excessively high titer can result in crystal precipitation. Such precipitation poses a significant obstacle to downstream purification processes. In such scenarios, a combination of higher yield and productivity, along with an appropriate titer, proves to be more applicable and economically efficient. The production of l -valine is a typical process that is confronted with such issue, as an elevated titer is often accompanied by the issue of l -valine crystallization. Under such circumstances, the increase of yield derived from glucose serves as a critical bottleneck and a primary concern for reducing production costs.
Due to the function of TCA cycle under aerobic conditions, the divert of pyruvate to TCA cycle has been a main factor limiting the yield of l -valine. In this study, the adaptive evolution of an engineered l -valine producing strain in sealed bottle filled with fermentation medium improved the cell growth under oxygen-limited conditions and significantly benefited the yield of l -valine from glucose. It demonstrated that the “live or die” stress constitutes a direct and highly effective driving force for evolution. After the ALE experiment, strain ALE2-40 acquired both better cell growth and enhanced l -valine production. ### Abstract
In industrial production, the yield of desired targets derived from carbon sources is frequently diminished by the competitive influence of cellular metabolism within microbial cell factories. The bioproduction of l -valine exemplifies a classic process that is confronted with such a dilemma, substantially hindering its economic industrial-scale production. In this study, we aim to engineer a cell factory capable of efficiently synthesizing l -valine with high yield by minimizing the consumption of its precursor pyruvate through the TCA cycle under oxygen-limited conditions. Metabolic engineering-based adaptive laboratory evolution (ALE) under oxygen-limited conditions resulted in the development of an evolved strain ALE2-40 with better cell growth and enhanced l -valine yield. Through comparative omics analysis and validation experiments, it was uncovered that during the ALE process, both pyruvate dehydrogenase activity and NADH availability were significantly improved. Moreover, beneficial targets have the potential to contribute to the NADH and ATP pools, thereby further promoting l -valine synthesis. Based on these results, reverse engineering of the evolved strain ALE2-40 was further conducted. Ultimately, the final strain VAL19 demonstrated remarkable performance, achieving an impressive l -valine titer of 93.7 g/L within 28 h in a 5-L bioreactor under oxygen-limited conditions, with a remarkable yield of 60.4% from glucose—equivalent to 92.9% of the theoretical yield—and a productivity of 3.35 g/L/h. These results set a new benchmark for the fermentative production of l -valine, with the highest yield and productivity reported so far.

### 1. Introduction
In nature, microorganisms exist in environments that may or may not contain oxygen. Consequently, they usually rely on either the respiratory chain or the substrate-level phosphorylation to generate the energy necessary for sustaining viability. In comparison, the efficiency of ATP production via the respiratory chain usually significantly surpasses that achieved through substrate-level phosphorylation [ 1 ]. Nevertheless, the operation of the respiratory chain necessitates the availability of oxygen, and meanwhile, the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle becomes active, which poses a significant competing pathway for the synthesis of chemicals that utilize pyruvate as a precursor [ 2 ]. Thus, for the production of pyruvate or derivatives using facultative anaerobic bacteria such as Escherichia coli and Corynebacterium glutamicum , efforts have been made to shift the fermentation process from aerobic conditions to oxygen-limited or anaerobic conditions, aiming to minimize the consumption of pyruvate via the TCA cycle, and to increase the yield of target products from carbon sources [ [3] , [4] , [5] ]. Under oxygen-limited or anaerobic conditions, the re-oxidation of nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NADH) generated during glycolysis is vital for maintaining cofactor balance [ 6 ], otherwise, cell growth and the biosynthesis of target products may be impeded due to the accumulation of fermentative byproducts such as acetic acid, lactic acid and succinic acid [ 6 ]. A critical challenge in the efficient production of pyruvate and derivatives lies in rewiring intracellular metabolic pathways to facilitate high-yield production under oxygen-limited or anaerobic fermentation conditions [ 7 ]. The rewiring of the metabolic pathway necessitates achieving an equilibrium between the central carbon metabolism and the synthetic pathway, as well as ensuring cofactor balance [ 8 ].

### 2.4. Adaptive laboratory evolution under oxygen-limited conditions
To enhance the strain's tolerance to oxygen-limited conditions and promote high-yield l -valine production, we employed ALE to optimize its growth performance when subjected to the stress of oxygen-limited condition. A tightly sealed reagent bottle filled with fermentation medium was employed to establish an oxygen-deprived environment. The culture medium used in ALE contained 20 g/L glucose, 1 g/L yeast extract, 4 g/L citrate, 6 g/L K 2 HPO 4 , 5 g/L (NH 4 ) 2 SO 4 , 1 g/L MgSO 4 ·7H 2 O, 60 mg/L FeSO 4 ·7H 2 O, 5 mg/L MnSO 4 ·H 2 O, 0.5 mg/L V H , 20 g/L MOPS to sustain pH stability, and 8 mg/L phenol red. During serial-passage, a 1-mL aliquot of the cell culture was transferred to a fresh reagent bottle. The culture was then incubated at 37 °C, with continuous shaking at a rate of 220 rpm.

### 2.5. Oxygen-limited fermentation of the ALE-derived engineered strains
After the ALE experiment, an initial fermentation screening of the evolved strains was conducted in 96-deep-well plates. Single colonies were picked using sterile toothpicks from LB solid plates and then cultured in 96 deep-well plates containing 450 μL LB medium for seed culture. The seed culture was carried out at a temperature of 37 °C and a shaking rate of 220 rpm for 12 h. Following which, the seed culture was transferred to a new 96 deep-well plate containing 450 μL fermentation medium and added glucose solution (60%, w/v) to a final concentration of 10 g/L. The 96 deep-well plate was sealed with a sealing mat to establish an oxygen-limited condition. The fermentation process was conducted at 37 °C with a shaking rate of 220 rpm.

### 3.1. Metabolic engineering of the starting strain to build a suitable chassis for adaptive evolution under oxygen-limited conditions
In our previous study, an l -valine-hyperproducing strain designated as VAL0 was constructed, which demonstrated the capacity for efficient l -valine production through a two-stage fermentation process, with similar performance as our previously reported VHY18 strain [ 2 ]. Specifically, during the first-stage fermentation, aerobic conditions were required to facilitate fast cell growth. Subsequently, during the second-stage oxygen-limited fermentation, l -valine was produced with high yield. Although the decoupling of cell growth and production resulted in an enhanced l -valine yield compared to the conventional aerobic fermentation, the two-stage fermentation approach still encountered challenges, including a prolonged fermentation duration and a relatively low yield during the aerobic cell growth phase. To address these problems, we aim to employ adaptive laboratory evolution (ALE) to establish a single-stage, oxygen-limited fermentation process and curtail the bypass consumption of the precursor pyruvate via the TCA cycle. However, several key issues must be taken into consideration before conducting ALE.

This is because E. coli strains inherently possess a tendency to accumulate acetic acid as a by-product when cultivated under anaerobic or oxygen-limited conditions with abundant glucose. ### 3.7. Fed-batch fermentation of the engineered strains in a 5-L bioreactor under oxygen-limited conditions
To conduct a thorough assessment of the fermentation performance of the engineered strain VAL18 and VAL19, 5-L bioreactor fermentation was carried out. An oxygen-limited fermentation condition was created with the stirrer speed setting to mere 300 rpm and aeration setting to as low as 0.2 L/min. The results are shown in Fig. 6 E (VAL18) and Fig. 6 F (VAL19). Owing to the limited solubility of l -valine, the fermentation process was terminated at 28 h, when the concentration of l -valine reached its saturation point and began to precipitate ( Fig. 6 E). It is noteworthy that the cell growth of strain VAL19 outperformed that of VAL18 at 12 h and 16 h, while exhibited a decline in comparison to that of VAL18 after 24 h. The final biomass levels (measured as OD 600 ) for strains VAL18 and VAL19 were 22.5 and 20.5, respectively. This phenomenon is most probably attributable to the fact that, during the cell growth phase, P fliC facilitates transcriptional initiation with a certain strength, and as cell growth progresses into the late phase, it exerts a suppressive effect on transcriptional initiation. Consequently, a partial decoupling of cell growth from l -valine synthesis was achieved, contributing to the enhanced synthesis of l -valine from glucose in VAL19. The l -valine titer in strain VAL19 reached a remarkable 93.7 g/L. This value represents a 3.4% increase compared to the l -valine titer of strain VAL18, which stood at 90.6 g/L. Moreover, referring to Fig. 3 , when compared with VAL06 (78.5 g/L) and ALE2-40 (85.4 g/L), the l -valine titer in VAL19 was 19.4% and 9.7% higher, respectively. In addition, strain VAL19 demonstrated a yield as high as 60.4%, which corresponds to 92.9% of the theoretical yield of 65% from glucose, outperforming VAL18's yield of 59.2%.

### 4. Discussion
In the construction of microbial cell factories, the quest for achieving high titer, yield, and productivity stands as the most prevalent objective within laboratory settings. However, in the actual industrial application, high titer does not invariably represent the optimal target. For instance, certain chemicals exhibit low solubility, and an excessively high titer can result in crystal precipitation. Such precipitation poses a significant obstacle to downstream purification processes. In such scenarios, a combination of higher yield and productivity, along with an appropriate titer, proves to be more applicable and economically efficient. The production of l -valine is a typical process that is confronted with such issue, as an elevated titer is often accompanied by the issue of l -valine crystallization. Under such circumstances, the increase of yield derived from glucose serves as a critical bottleneck and a primary concern for reducing production costs.
Due to the function of TCA cycle under aerobic conditions, the divert of pyruvate to TCA cycle has been a main factor limiting the yield of l -valine. In this study, the adaptive evolution of an engineered l -valine producing strain in sealed bottle filled with fermentation medium improved the cell growth under oxygen-limited conditions and significantly benefited the yield of l -valine from glucose. It demonstrated that the “live or die” stress constitutes a direct and highly effective driving force for evolution. After the ALE experiment, strain ALE2-40 acquired both better cell growth and enhanced l -valine production. To our surprise, the genomic mutations identified were limited to merely four single-nucleotide variants. Notably, the mutation in the lpd gene has been validated to effectively elevate both the NADH/NAD + ratio and the activity of pyruvate dehydrogenase. ### Abstract
In industrial production, the yield of desired targets derived from carbon sources is frequently diminished by the competitive influence of cellular metabolism within microbial cell factories. The bioproduction of l -valine exemplifies a classic process that is confronted with such a dilemma, substantially hindering its economic industrial-scale production. In this study, we aim to engineer a cell factory capable of efficiently synthesizing l -valine with high yield by minimizing the consumption of its precursor pyruvate through the TCA cycle under oxygen-limited conditions. Metabolic engineering-based adaptive laboratory evolution (ALE) under oxygen-limited conditions resulted in the development of an evolved strain ALE2-40 with better cell growth and enhanced l -valine yield. Through comparative omics analysis and validation experiments, it was uncovered that during the ALE process, both pyruvate dehydrogenase activity and NADH availability were significantly improved. Moreover, beneficial targets have the potential to contribute to the NADH and ATP pools, thereby further promoting l -valine synthesis. Based on these results, reverse engineering of the evolved strain ALE2-40 was further conducted. Ultimately, the final strain VAL19 demonstrated remarkable performance, achieving an impressive l -valine titer of 93.7 g/L within 28 h in a 5-L bioreactor under oxygen-limited conditions, with a remarkable yield of 60.4% from glucose—equivalent to 92.9% of the theoretical yield—and a productivity of 3.35 g/L/h. These results set a new benchmark for the fermentative production of l -valine, with the highest yield and productivity reported so far.

### 1. Introduction
In nature, microorganisms exist in environments that may or may not contain oxygen. Consequently, they usually rely on either the respiratory chain or the substrate-level phosphorylation to generate the energy necessary for sustaining viability. In comparison, the efficiency of ATP production via the respiratory chain usually significantly surpasses that achieved through substrate-level phosphorylation [ 1 ]. Nevertheless, the operation of the respiratory chain necessitates the availability of oxygen, and meanwhile, the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle becomes active, which poses a significant competing pathway for the synthesis of chemicals that utilize pyruvate as a precursor [ 2 ]. Thus, for the production of pyruvate or derivatives using facultative anaerobic bacteria such as Escherichia coli and Corynebacterium glutamicum , efforts have been made to shift the fermentation process from aerobic conditions to oxygen-limited or anaerobic conditions, aiming to minimize the consumption of pyruvate via the TCA cycle, and to increase the yield of target products from carbon sources [ [3] , [4] , [5] ]. Under oxygen-limited or anaerobic conditions, the re-oxidation of nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NADH) generated during glycolysis is vital for maintaining cofactor balance [ 6 ], otherwise, cell growth and the biosynthesis of target products may be impeded due to the accumulation of fermentative byproducts such as acetic acid, lactic acid and succinic acid [ 6 ]. A critical challenge in the efficient production of pyruvate and derivatives lies in rewiring intracellular metabolic pathways to facilitate high-yield production under oxygen-limited or anaerobic fermentation conditions [ 7 ]. The rewiring of the metabolic pathway necessitates achieving an equilibrium between the central carbon metabolism and the synthetic pathway, as well as ensuring cofactor balance [ 8 ].

### 2.4. Adaptive laboratory evolution under oxygen-limited conditions
To enhance the strain's tolerance to oxygen-limited conditions and promote high-yield l -valine production, we employed ALE to optimize its growth performance when subjected to the stress of oxygen-limited condition. A tightly sealed reagent bottle filled with fermentation medium was employed to establish an oxygen-deprived environment. The culture medium used in ALE contained 20 g/L glucose, 1 g/L yeast extract, 4 g/L citrate, 6 g/L K 2 HPO 4 , 5 g/L (NH 4 ) 2 SO 4 , 1 g/L MgSO 4 ·7H 2 O, 60 mg/L FeSO 4 ·7H 2 O, 5 mg/L MnSO 4 ·H 2 O, 0.5 mg/L V H , 20 g/L MOPS to sustain pH stability, and 8 mg/L phenol red. During serial-passage, a 1-mL aliquot of the cell culture was transferred to a fresh reagent bottle. The culture was then incubated at 37 °C, with continuous shaking at a rate of 220 rpm.

### 2.5. Oxygen-limited fermentation of the ALE-derived engineered strains
After the ALE experiment, an initial fermentation screening of the evolved strains was conducted in 96-deep-well plates. Single colonies were picked using sterile toothpicks from LB solid plates and then cultured in 96 deep-well plates containing 450 μL LB medium for seed culture. The seed culture was carried out at a temperature of 37 °C and a shaking rate of 220 rpm for 12 h. Following which, the seed culture was transferred to a new 96 deep-well plate containing 450 μL fermentation medium and added glucose solution (60%, w/v) to a final concentration of 10 g/L. The 96 deep-well plate was sealed with a sealing mat to establish an oxygen-limited condition. The fermentation process was conducted at 37 °C with a shaking rate of 220 rpm.

### 3.1. Metabolic engineering of the starting strain to build a suitable chassis for adaptive evolution under oxygen-limited conditions
In our previous study, an l -valine-hyperproducing strain designated as VAL0 was constructed, which demonstrated the capacity for efficient l -valine production through a two-stage fermentation process, with similar performance as our previously reported VHY18 strain [ 2 ]. Specifically, during the first-stage fermentation, aerobic conditions were required to facilitate fast cell growth. Subsequently, during the second-stage oxygen-limited fermentation, l -valine was produced with high yield. Although the decoupling of cell growth and production resulted in an enhanced l -valine yield compared to the conventional aerobic fermentation, the two-stage fermentation approach still encountered challenges, including a prolonged fermentation duration and a relatively low yield during the aerobic cell growth phase. To address these problems, we aim to employ adaptive laboratory evolution (ALE) to establish a single-stage, oxygen-limited fermentation process and curtail the bypass consumption of the precursor pyruvate via the TCA cycle. However, several key issues must be taken into consideration before conducting ALE.

### 3.7. Fed-batch fermentation of the engineered strains in a 5-L bioreactor under oxygen-limited conditions
To conduct a thorough assessment of the fermentation performance of the engineered strain VAL18 and VAL19, 5-L bioreactor fermentation was carried out. An oxygen-limited fermentation condition was created with the stirrer speed setting to mere 300 rpm and aeration setting to as low as 0.2 L/min. The results are shown in Fig. 6 E (VAL18) and Fig. 6 F (VAL19). Owing to the limited solubility of l -valine, the fermentation process was terminated at 28 h, when the concentration of l -valine reached its saturation point and began to precipitate ( Fig. 6 E). It is noteworthy that the cell growth of strain VAL19 outperformed that of VAL18 at 12 h and 16 h, while exhibited a decline in comparison to that of VAL18 after 24 h. The final biomass levels (measured as OD 600 ) for strains VAL18 and VAL19 were 22.5 and 20.5, respectively. This phenomenon is most probably attributable to the fact that, during the cell growth phase, P fliC facilitates transcriptional initiation with a certain strength, and as cell growth progresses into the late phase, it exerts a suppressive effect on transcriptional initiation. Consequently, a partial decoupling of cell growth from l -valine synthesis was achieved, contributing to the enhanced synthesis of l -valine from glucose in VAL19. The l -valine titer in strain VAL19 reached a remarkable 93.7 g/L. This value represents a 3.4% increase compared to the l -valine titer of strain VAL18, which stood at 90.6 g/L. Moreover, referring to Fig. 3 , when compared with VAL06 (78.5 g/L) and ALE2-40 (85.4 g/L), the l -valine titer in VAL19 was 19.4% and 9.7% higher, respectively. In addition, strain VAL19 demonstrated a yield as high as 60.4%, which corresponds to 92.9% of the theoretical yield of 65% from glucose, outperforming VAL18's yield of 59.2%.

### 4. Discussion
In the construction of microbial cell factories, the quest for achieving high titer, yield, and productivity stands as the most prevalent objective within laboratory settings. However, in the actual industrial application, high titer does not invariably represent the optimal target. For instance, certain chemicals exhibit low solubility, and an excessively high titer can result in crystal precipitation. Such precipitation poses a significant obstacle to downstream purification processes. In such scenarios, a combination of higher yield and productivity, along with an appropriate titer, proves to be more applicable and economically efficient. The production of l -valine is a typical process that is confronted with such issue, as an elevated titer is often accompanied by the issue of l -valine crystallization. Under such circumstances, the increase of yield derived from glucose serves as a critical bottleneck and a primary concern for reducing production costs.
Due to the function of TCA cycle under aerobic conditions, the divert of pyruvate to TCA cycle has been a main factor limiting the yield of l -valine. In this study, the adaptive evolution of an engineered l -valine producing strain in sealed bottle filled with fermentation medium improved the cell growth under oxygen-limited conditions and significantly benefited the yield of l -valine from glucose. It demonstrated that the “live or die” stress constitutes a direct and highly effective driving force for evolution. After the ALE experiment, strain ALE2-40 acquired both better cell growth and enhanced l -valine production. To our surprise, the genomic mutations identified were limited to merely four single-nucleotide variants. Notably, the mutation in the lpd gene has been validated to effectively elevate both the NADH/NAD + ratio and the activity of pyruvate dehydrogenase. ### Abstract
In industrial production, the yield of desired targets derived from carbon sources is frequently diminished by the competitive influence of cellular metabolism within microbial cell factories. The bioproduction of l -valine exemplifies a classic process that is confronted with such a dilemma, substantially hindering its economic industrial-scale production. In this study, we aim to engineer a cell factory capable of efficiently synthesizing l -valine with high yield by minimizing the consumption of its precursor pyruvate through the TCA cycle under oxygen-limited conditions. Metabolic engineering-based adaptive laboratory evolution (ALE) under oxygen-limited conditions resulted in the development of an evolved strain ALE2-40 with better cell growth and enhanced l -valine yield. Through comparative omics analysis and validation experiments, it was uncovered that during the ALE process, both pyruvate dehydrogenase activity and NADH availability were significantly improved. Moreover, beneficial targets have the potential to contribute to the NADH and ATP pools, thereby further promoting l -valine synthesis. Based on these results, reverse engineering of the evolved strain ALE2-40 was further conducted. Ultimately, the final strain VAL19 demonstrated remarkable performance, achieving an impressive l -valine titer of 93.7 g/L within 28 h in a 5-L bioreactor under oxygen-limited conditions, with a remarkable yield of 60.4% from glucose—equivalent to 92.9% of the theoretical yield—and a productivity of 3.35 g/L/h. These results set a new benchmark for the fermentative production of l -valine, with the highest yield and productivity reported so far.

### 1. Introduction
In nature, microorganisms exist in environments that may or may not contain oxygen. Consequently, they usually rely on either the respiratory chain or the substrate-level phosphorylation to generate the energy necessary for sustaining viability. In comparison, the efficiency of ATP production via the respiratory chain usually significantly surpasses that achieved through substrate-level phosphorylation [ 1 ]. Nevertheless, the operation of the respiratory chain necessitates the availability of oxygen, and meanwhile, the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle becomes active, which poses a significant competing pathway for the synthesis of chemicals that utilize pyruvate as a precursor [ 2 ]. Thus, for the production of pyruvate or derivatives using facultative anaerobic bacteria such as Escherichia coli and Corynebacterium glutamicum , efforts have been made to shift the fermentation process from aerobic conditions to oxygen-limited or anaerobic conditions, aiming to minimize the consumption of pyruvate via the TCA cycle, and to increase the yield of target products from carbon sources [ [3] , [4] , [5] ]. Under oxygen-limited or anaerobic conditions, the re-oxidation of nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NADH) generated during glycolysis is vital for maintaining cofactor balance [ 6 ], otherwise, cell growth and the biosynthesis of target products may be impeded due to the accumulation of fermentative byproducts such as acetic acid, lactic acid and succinic acid [ 6 ]. A critical challenge in the efficient production of pyruvate and derivatives lies in rewiring intracellular metabolic pathways to facilitate high-yield production under oxygen-limited or anaerobic fermentation conditions [ 7 ]. The rewiring of the metabolic pathway necessitates achieving an equilibrium between the central carbon metabolism and the synthetic pathway, as well as ensuring cofactor balance [ 8 ].

### 2.4. Adaptive laboratory evolution under oxygen-limited conditions
To enhance the strain's tolerance to oxygen-limited conditions and promote high-yield l -valine production, we employed ALE to optimize its growth performance when subjected to the stress of oxygen-limited condition. A tightly sealed reagent bottle filled with fermentation medium was employed to establish an oxygen-deprived environment. The culture medium used in ALE contained 20 g/L glucose, 1 g/L yeast extract, 4 g/L citrate, 6 g/L K 2 HPO 4 , 5 g/L (NH 4 ) 2 SO 4 , 1 g/L MgSO 4 ·7H 2 O, 60 mg/L FeSO 4 ·7H 2 O, 5 mg/L MnSO 4 ·H 2 O, 0.5 mg/L V H , 20 g/L MOPS to sustain pH stability, and 8 mg/L phenol red. During serial-passage, a 1-mL aliquot of the cell culture was transferred to a fresh reagent bottle. The culture was then incubated at 37 °C, with continuous shaking at a rate of 220 rpm.

### 2.5. Oxygen-limited fermentation of the ALE-derived engineered strains
After the ALE experiment, an initial fermentation screening of the evolved strains was conducted in 96-deep-well plates. Single colonies were picked using sterile toothpicks from LB solid plates and then cultured in 96 deep-well plates containing 450 μL LB medium for seed culture. The seed culture was carried out at a temperature of 37 °C and a shaking rate of 220 rpm for 12 h. Following which, the seed culture was transferred to a new 96 deep-well plate containing 450 μL fermentation medium and added glucose solution (60%, w/v) to a final concentration of 10 g/L. The 96 deep-well plate was sealed with a sealing mat to establish an oxygen-limited condition. The fermentation process was conducted at 37 °C with a shaking rate of 220 rpm.

### 3.1. Metabolic engineering of the starting strain to build a suitable chassis for adaptive evolution under oxygen-limited conditions
In our previous study, an l -valine-hyperproducing strain designated as VAL0 was constructed, which demonstrated the capacity for efficient l -valine production through a two-stage fermentation process, with similar performance as our previously reported VHY18 strain [ 2 ]. Specifically, during the first-stage fermentation, aerobic conditions were required to facilitate fast cell growth. Subsequently, during the second-stage oxygen-limited fermentation, l -valine was produced with high yield. Although the decoupling of cell growth and production resulted in an enhanced l -valine yield compared to the conventional aerobic fermentation, the two-stage fermentation approach still encountered challenges, including a prolonged fermentation duration and a relatively low yield during the aerobic cell growth phase. To address these problems, we aim to employ adaptive laboratory evolution (ALE) to establish a single-stage, oxygen-limited fermentation process and curtail the bypass consumption of the precursor pyruvate via the TCA cycle. However, several key issues must be taken into consideration before conducting ALE.
Firstly, to facilitate the transition from aerobic fermentation to oxygen-limited fermentation, it is imperative to block the acetic acid synthesis pathway in the starting strain. This is because E. coli strains inherently possess a tendency to accumulate acetic acid as a by-product when cultivated under anaerobic or oxygen-limited conditions with abundant glucose. Specifically, gene poxB encoding the pyruvate dehydrogenase, gene ackA encoding the acetate kinase, gene tdcD encoding the propionate kinase, and pta encoding the phosphate acetyltransferase, were sequentially knocked out in VAL0, constructing strains VAL01, VAL02, VAL03, and VAL04, respectively. ### 3.3. Fermentation assessment of the evolved strain in a 5-L bioreactor
The fermentation performance of the evolved strain ALE2-40 was further assessed in a 5-L bioreactor under oxygen-limited conditions, with the starting strain VAL06 as the control under the same conditions ( Fig. 3 ). As depicted in Fig. 3 , throughout the overall fermentation process, both the cell growth and l -valine production of ALE2-40 demonstrated a marked enhancement in comparison to VAL06, achieving a final l -valine titer of 85.4 g/L at 28 h ( Fig. 3 B), whereas, at the same time, VAL06 attained an l -valine titer of 78.5 g/L ( Fig. 3 A). Strain ALE2-40 attained its peak OD 600 value of 25.9 at 20 h, whereas strain VAL06 reached a comparable OD 600 level of 25.1 up to 28 h of fermentation. Despite of the fact that enhanced cell growth would unavoidably consume more carbon sources, the l -valine yield derived from the total consumed glucose utilized by ALE2-40 still surpassed that of VAL06. To be precise, ALE2-40 attained an l -valine yield of 58.0% from glucose, marking a 3.8% increase over the yield of 55.9% observed in VAL06. Furthermore, no notable accumulation of frequently encountered organic acids, such as acetic acid and succinic acid, was detected. These results clearly indicate that, as a result of the ALE experiment, the enhanced cell growth exhibited by strain ALE2-40 significantly contributed to the efficient l -valine production under oxygen-limited conditions. As shown in Fig. 3 B, l -valine crystals began to precipitate from the fermentation broth of ALE2-40 at 28 h, indicating the end of the fermentation process to prevent imposing an additional burden on the following extraction procedure. Thus, for the 5-L bioreactor fermentation in this study, the fermentation period was set at 28 h. ### 3.6. Transcriptomics-guided reverse metabolic engineering
Through comparative transcriptomic analysis, differentially expressed genes within strain ALE2-40 were identified, and their impact on l -valine production was rigorously validated. The findings revealed that the upregulation of genes such as ybcL , yihA , and napF , as well as the downregulation of rsxE , speC , yjiG , argI , and fimG , significantly enhanced l -valine titer. Building on these insights, reverse metabolic engineering strategies were implemented to further enhance the fermentation performance of strain ALE2-40. Specifically, the P J23105 promoter was employed to overexpress the upregulated genes, while downregulated genes underwent systematic pairwise deletion analyses to identify optimal combinations for enhancing l -valine production synergistically.
First, the overexpression of previously identified upregulated genes was carried out in strain ALE2-40. Specifically, the ybcL , yihA , and napF genes were individually overexpressed in the genome of ALE2-40, resulting in the construction of strains VAL09 (ALE2-40, yciQ ::P J23105 - ybcL ), VAL10 (ALE2-40, ylbE ::P J23105 - yihA ), and VAL11 (ALE2-40, gapC ::P J23105 - napF ). The results of oxygen-limited shake-flask fermentation for these strains are depicted in Fig. 6 A. The l -valine titers recorded for VAL09, VAL10, and VAL11 were 15.9, 27.6, and 27.0 g/L, respectively, revealing no significant enhancement in comparison to the control strain ALE2-40, which yielded 26.1 g/L l -valine. ### 3.7. Fed-batch fermentation of the engineered strains in a 5-L bioreactor under oxygen-limited conditions
To conduct a thorough assessment of the fermentation performance of the engineered strain VAL18 and VAL19, 5-L bioreactor fermentation was carried out. An oxygen-limited fermentation condition was created with the stirrer speed setting to mere 300 rpm and aeration setting to as low as 0.2 L/min. The results are shown in Fig. 6 E (VAL18) and Fig. 6 F (VAL19). Owing to the limited solubility of l -valine, the fermentation process was terminated at 28 h, when the concentration of l -valine reached its saturation point and began to precipitate ( Fig. 6 E). It is noteworthy that the cell growth of strain VAL19 outperformed that of VAL18 at 12 h and 16 h, while exhibited a decline in comparison to that of VAL18 after 24 h. The final biomass levels (measured as OD 600 ) for strains VAL18 and VAL19 were 22.5 and 20.5, respectively. This phenomenon is most probably attributable to the fact that, during the cell growth phase, P fliC facilitates transcriptional initiation with a certain strength, and as cell growth progresses into the late phase, it exerts a suppressive effect on transcriptional initiation. Consequently, a partial decoupling of cell growth from l -valine synthesis was achieved, contributing to the enhanced synthesis of l -valine from glucose in VAL19. The l -valine titer in strain VAL19 reached a remarkable 93.7 g/L. This value represents a 3.4% increase compared to the l -valine titer of strain VAL18, which stood at 90.6 g/L. Moreover, referring to Fig. 3 , when compared with VAL06 (78.5 g/L) and ALE2-40 (85.4 g/L), the l -valine titer in VAL19 was 19.4% and 9.7% higher, respectively. In addition, strain VAL19 demonstrated a yield as high as 60.4%, which corresponds to 92.9% of the theoretical yield of 65% from glucose, outperforming VAL18's yield of 59.2%.

### 4. Discussion
In the construction of microbial cell factories, the quest for achieving high titer, yield, and productivity stands as the most prevalent objective within laboratory settings. However, in the actual industrial application, high titer does not invariably represent the optimal target. For instance, certain chemicals exhibit low solubility, and an excessively high titer can result in crystal precipitation. Such precipitation poses a significant obstacle to downstream purification processes. In such scenarios, a combination of higher yield and productivity, along with an appropriate titer, proves to be more applicable and economically efficient. The production of l -valine is a typical process that is confronted with such issue, as an elevated titer is often accompanied by the issue of l -valine crystallization. Under such circumstances, the increase of yield derived from glucose serves as a critical bottleneck and a primary concern for reducing production costs.
Due to the function of TCA cycle under aerobic conditions, the divert of pyruvate to TCA cycle has been a main factor limiting the yield of l -valine. In this study, the adaptive evolution of an engineered l -valine producing strain in sealed bottle filled with fermentation medium improved the cell growth under oxygen-limited conditions and significantly benefited the yield of l -valine from glucose. It demonstrated that the “live or die” stress constitutes a direct and highly effective driving force for evolution. After the ALE experiment, strain ALE2-40 acquired both better cell growth and enhanced l -valine production. To our surprise, the genomic mutations identified were limited to merely four single-nucleotide variants. Notably, the mutation in the lpd gene has been validated to effectively elevate both the NADH/NAD + ratio and the activity of pyruvate dehydrogenase.

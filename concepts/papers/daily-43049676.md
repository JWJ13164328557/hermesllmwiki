# A Tsr-family Chemoreceptor Controls Xylem Sap Chemo-sensing, Swimming Motility, and Several Virulence Associated Functions in Xanthomonas oryzae pv. oryzae.

- **PMID**: [43049676](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/43049676/)
- **DOI**: [10.1094/MPMI-08-26-0093-R](https://doi.org/10.1094/MPMI-08-26-0093-R)

- **作者**: Arkaprabha China, Amaresh Kumar Mohanty, Subhadeep Chatterjee

## 摘要
Xanthomonas oryzae pv. oryzae (Xoo) is a major phytopathogen that causes devastating diseases in rice. Although several virulence-associated factors have been identified in Xanthomonas spp., the functions of many predicted proteins remain poorly understood. Here, we identify and characterize Mcp3, a membrane-associated putative chemoreceptor of the Tsr (transducer of serine receptor) family, as a critical determinant of virulence in X. oryzae. Deletion of mcp3 resulted in reduced virulence and impaired host colonization. Bioinformatic analyses, homology modelling, and membrane localization studies demonstrated that Mcp3 is a membrane-associated protein. Functional characterization of the Δmcp3 mutant revealed defects in chemotaxis and flagellum-mediated motility, indicating a role in bacterial movement and environmental sensing. In addition, loss of Mcp3 altered multiple virulence-associated traits, including cellulase production, biofilm formation, and exopolysaccharide production, uncovering a previously unrecognized role for this chemoreceptor in the regulation of key virulence-associated traits. Ligand interaction analyses along with chemotaxis assays further revealed that Mcp3 recognizes multiple xylem sap-derived chemical cues, while deletion of mcp3 reduced chemotaxis toward serine and xylose but enhanced chemotaxis toward methionine, suggesting a role in fine-tuning chemotaxis, host niche sensing, and adaptive navigation during infection. Collectively, these findings establish Mcp3 as a previously uncharacterized regulator of virulence in Xoo and reveal a novel role for a Tsr-family chemoreceptor in controlling biofilm and EPS formation, in addition to mediating chemotaxis-driven host niche sensing and adaptation during infection.

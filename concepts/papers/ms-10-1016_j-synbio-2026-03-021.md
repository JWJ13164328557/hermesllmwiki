### Abstract
Retinol is widely used in skin anti-aging, yet its effects exhibit significant concentration dependence. However, the underlying biphasic mechanism, that is low concentrations promote cell proliferation while high concentrations are inhibitory, remains incompletely understood. Here, we employed multi-omics analysis and genome-scale metabolic models (GEMs) to systematically infer the key molecular networks underlying the distinct effects of retinol concentrations on human foreskin fibroblasts (HFFs). Our results suggest that low retinol concentrations appear to promote proliferation by activating the canonical retinoic acid signaling pathway and inducing sophisticated metabolic reprogramming. This reprogramming appears to involve prioritizing the coenzyme NADPH for retinol processing and antioxidant defense, which is associated with a compensatory suppression of NADPH-consuming cholesterol biosynthesis. This metabolic shift may foster a favorable intracellular environment for growth. Conversely, high concentrations are linked to a multi-system injury cascade. The observed key feature likely involves significant oxidative stress, associated with the buildup of toxic metabolic intermediates and a pro-inflammatory lipid storm marked by elevated leukotrienes. These stress responses align with the signatures of ferroptosis, a form of programmed cell death characterized by glutathione (GSH) defense system collapse and downregulation of the key regulator GPX4 . ### 1. Introduction
Skin aging is a complex biological process driven by intrinsic factors (e.g., genetics) and extrinsic stressors (e.g., UV exposure). Its core pathophysiology involves the progressive deterioration of dermal structure and function [ [1] , [2] , [3] ]. Dermal fibroblasts, the predominant cell type, maintain skin mechanical strength and elasticity by regulating the dynamic balance of collagen, elastin, and other extracellular matrix (ECM) components. However, aging fibroblasts exhibit a marked decrease in proliferative capacity, biosynthetic activity, and mechanosensitivity [ 4 , 5 ]. This impairment stems from the dysregulation of key signaling networks, such as the inhibition of the YAP/TAZ pathway and the aberrant activation of the CCN1/TGF-β/AP-1 signaling axis, which together accelerate ECM degradation and the emergence of skin aging phenotypes [ 6 , 7 ].
Accumulating evidence implicates oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, and aberrant lipid metabolism as fundamental drivers of skin cell dysfunction and structural degradation [ 8 ]. Among these, ferroptosis – an iron-dependent programmed cell death driven by lipid peroxidation, is gaining increasing attention for its role in skin pathologies, fibroblast dysfunction, and oxidative stress-associated aging [ 9 ]. Hallmarks include diminished or reduced activity of glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4), glutathione (GSH) depletion, and toxic lipid peroxide accumulation, culminating in catastrophic membrane damage [ 10 ]. ### 3. Discussion
This study employed an integrative systems biology approach, combining multi-omics analysis with GEMs to elucidate retinol's biphasic effects on proliferation of HFFs. The results suggest that the concentration-dependent effects of retinol on HFF proliferation may be governed by a metabolic transition: at low concentrations, retinol appears to optimize the metabolic network to support signal transduction and promote proliferative capacity. In contrast, at high concentrations, it appears to provoke metabolic dysregulation and oxidative stress, potentially leading to ferroptosis.
At low concentrations, retinol seems to promote cell proliferation by inducing an NADPH-centric metabolic reprogramming ( Fig. 7 ). Driven by a robust activation of retinol-specific signaling ( DHRS3, CRABP2 ), this reprogramming may involve a dual metabolic adjustment: on one hand, the cell likely enhances the NADPH regeneration by upregulating G6PD and PGD ( Fig. 7 A and B), the rate-limiting enzyme of the pentose phosphate pathway (PPP). This is further supported by the downregulation of the competing enzyme AKR1B1 and the high weight assigned to the pentose and glucuronate interconversions pathway in our metabolic model. On the other hand, to prevent the exhaustion of this critical resource, cells actively suppress major NADPH-consuming pathways. ### 2.1. Phenotypic and molecular characterization of the biphasic regulation of HFFs proliferation by retinol

### 2.5. Multi-omics data drives the pinpointing of core toxicity pathways
To systematically integrate multidimensional datasets and elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying the biphasic effect of retinol, we performed a multi-omics integrative analysis. First, the global comparison of the transcriptome and proteome revealed that extensive post-transcriptional regulation is a key feature of retinol action. ### 3. Discussion
This study employed an integrative systems biology approach, combining multi-omics analysis with GEMs to elucidate retinol's biphasic effects on proliferation of HFFs. The results suggest that the concentration-dependent effects of retinol on HFF proliferation may be governed by a metabolic transition: at low concentrations, retinol appears to optimize the metabolic network to support signal transduction and promote proliferative capacity. In contrast, at high concentrations, it appears to provoke metabolic dysregulation and oxidative stress, potentially leading to ferroptosis.
At low concentrations, retinol seems to promote cell proliferation by inducing an NADPH-centric metabolic reprogramming ( Fig. 7 ). Driven by a robust activation of retinol-specific signaling ( DHRS3, CRABP2 ), this reprogramming may involve a dual metabolic adjustment: on one hand, the cell likely enhances the NADPH regeneration by upregulating G6PD and PGD ( Fig. 7 A and B), the rate-limiting enzyme of the pentose phosphate pathway (PPP). This is further supported by the downregulation of the competing enzyme AKR1B1 and the high weight assigned to the pentose and glucuronate interconversions pathway in our metabolic model. On the other hand, to prevent the exhaustion of this critical resource, cells actively suppress major NADPH-consuming pathways. Specifically, both multi-omics profiling and targeted qPCR confirmed a dramatic transcriptional suppression of central cholesterol biosynthesis hubs, including HMGCR and LDLR ( Fig. 7 A and B). This dual metabolic mechanism—boosting NADPH synthesis while curtailing its consumption in lipid metabolism—may contributes to the maintenance of a robust NADPH pool [ 50 ], which in turn likely enhances both the antioxidant systems [ 51 ] and retinoic acid (RA) metabolism [ 52 ], ultimately likely contributing to a favorable redox microenvironment for cell proliferation ( Fig. 7 C).

### Abstract
Retinol is widely used in skin anti-aging, yet its effects exhibit significant concentration dependence. However, the underlying biphasic mechanism, that is low concentrations promote cell proliferation while high concentrations are inhibitory, remains incompletely understood. Here, we employed multi-omics analysis and genome-scale metabolic models (GEMs) to systematically infer the key molecular networks underlying the distinct effects of retinol concentrations on human foreskin fibroblasts (HFFs). Our results suggest that low retinol concentrations appear to promote proliferation by activating the canonical retinoic acid signaling pathway and inducing sophisticated metabolic reprogramming. This reprogramming appears to involve prioritizing the coenzyme NADPH for retinol processing and antioxidant defense, which is associated with a compensatory suppression of NADPH-consuming cholesterol biosynthesis. This metabolic shift may foster a favorable intracellular environment for growth. Conversely, high concentrations are linked to a multi-system injury cascade. The observed key feature likely involves significant oxidative stress, associated with the buildup of toxic metabolic intermediates and a pro-inflammatory lipid storm marked by elevated leukotrienes. ### 1. Introduction
Skin aging is a complex biological process driven by intrinsic factors (e.g., genetics) and extrinsic stressors (e.g., UV exposure). Its core pathophysiology involves the progressive deterioration of dermal structure and function [ [1] , [2] , [3] ]. Dermal fibroblasts, the predominant cell type, maintain skin mechanical strength and elasticity by regulating the dynamic balance of collagen, elastin, and other extracellular matrix (ECM) components. However, aging fibroblasts exhibit a marked decrease in proliferative capacity, biosynthetic activity, and mechanosensitivity [ 4 , 5 ]. This impairment stems from the dysregulation of key signaling networks, such as the inhibition of the YAP/TAZ pathway and the aberrant activation of the CCN1/TGF-β/AP-1 signaling axis, which together accelerate ECM degradation and the emergence of skin aging phenotypes [ 6 , 7 ].
Accumulating evidence implicates oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, and aberrant lipid metabolism as fundamental drivers of skin cell dysfunction and structural degradation [ 8 ]. Among these, ferroptosis – an iron-dependent programmed cell death driven by lipid peroxidation, is gaining increasing attention for its role in skin pathologies, fibroblast dysfunction, and oxidative stress-associated aging [ 9 ]. Hallmarks include diminished or reduced activity of glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4), glutathione (GSH) depletion, and toxic lipid peroxide accumulation, culminating in catastrophic membrane damage [ 10 ]. Indeed, emerging studies link ferroptosis during the skin aging process with collagen degradation, fibroblast death, and compromised ECM stability. For instance, critical environmental stressors such as ultraviolet radiation (UVR) have been shown to significantly promote the activation of ferroptosis in fibroblasts, thereby exacerbating their functional decline and dermal structural damage [ 11 ].

### 2.1. Phenotypic and molecular characterization of the biphasic regulation of HFFs proliferation by retinol
To systematically characterize the concentration-dependent effects of retinol on HFF proliferation and identify optimal concentrations for subsequent multi-omics analyses, we performed a series of phenotypic and molecular assessments. First, the cell viability assays revealed that retinol exerts a pronounced biphasic, concentration-dependent regulation on HFF proliferation ( Fig. 1 A). At a low concentration (≤10 μg/mL), retinol significantly promoted HFF proliferation, peaking at 5 μg/mL ( P &lt; 0.001). Conversely, at a high concentration (≥30 μg/mL), retinol strongly inhibited HFF proliferation ( P &lt; 0.001).

### 2.5. Multi-omics data drives the pinpointing of core toxicity pathways

### 3. Discussion
This study employed an integrative systems biology approach, combining multi-omics analysis with GEMs to elucidate retinol's biphasic effects on proliferation of HFFs. The results suggest that the concentration-dependent effects of retinol on HFF proliferation may be governed by a metabolic transition: at low concentrations, retinol appears to optimize the metabolic network to support signal transduction and promote proliferative capacity. In contrast, at high concentrations, it appears to provoke metabolic dysregulation and oxidative stress, potentially leading to ferroptosis.
At low concentrations, retinol seems to promote cell proliferation by inducing an NADPH-centric metabolic reprogramming ( Fig. 7 ). Driven by a robust activation of retinol-specific signaling ( DHRS3, CRABP2 ), this reprogramming may involve a dual metabolic adjustment: on one hand, the cell likely enhances the NADPH regeneration by upregulating G6PD and PGD ( Fig. 7 A and B), the rate-limiting enzyme of the pentose phosphate pathway (PPP). This is further supported by the downregulation of the competing enzyme AKR1B1 and the high weight assigned to the pentose and glucuronate interconversions pathway in our metabolic model. On the other hand, to prevent the exhaustion of this critical resource, cells actively suppress major NADPH-consuming pathways. Specifically, both multi-omics profiling and targeted qPCR confirmed a dramatic transcriptional suppression of central cholesterol biosynthesis hubs, including HMGCR and LDLR ( Fig. 7 A and B). This dual metabolic mechanism—boosting NADPH synthesis while curtailing its consumption in lipid metabolism—may contributes to the maintenance of a robust NADPH pool [ 50 ], which in turn likely enhances both the antioxidant systems [ 51 ] and retinoic acid (RA) metabolism [ 52 ], ultimately likely contributing to a favorable redox microenvironment for cell proliferation ( Fig. 7 C).

### Abstract
Retinol is widely used in skin anti-aging, yet its effects exhibit significant concentration dependence. However, the underlying biphasic mechanism, that is low concentrations promote cell proliferation while high concentrations are inhibitory, remains incompletely understood. Here, we employed multi-omics analysis and genome-scale metabolic models (GEMs) to systematically infer the key molecular networks underlying the distinct effects of retinol concentrations on human foreskin fibroblasts (HFFs). Our results suggest that low retinol concentrations appear to promote proliferation by activating the canonical retinoic acid signaling pathway and inducing sophisticated metabolic reprogramming. This reprogramming appears to involve prioritizing the coenzyme NADPH for retinol processing and antioxidant defense, which is associated with a compensatory suppression of NADPH-consuming cholesterol biosynthesis. This metabolic shift may foster a favorable intracellular environment for growth. Conversely, high concentrations are linked to a multi-system injury cascade. The observed key feature likely involves significant oxidative stress, associated with the buildup of toxic metabolic intermediates and a pro-inflammatory lipid storm marked by elevated leukotrienes. These stress responses align with the signatures of ferroptosis, a form of programmed cell death characterized by glutathione (GSH) defense system collapse and downregulation of the key regulator GPX4 . Our findings suggest a potential mechanistic transition from a state of metabolic optimization to the activation of stress-induced injury pathways in response to varying retinol concentrations. This study provides a systems-level framework for understanding retinol's biphasic effects and highlights key regulatory nodes (e.g., DHRS3 , GCLM ) as potential targets for future research. ### 1. Introduction
Skin aging is a complex biological process driven by intrinsic factors (e.g., genetics) and extrinsic stressors (e.g., UV exposure). Its core pathophysiology involves the progressive deterioration of dermal structure and function [ [1] , [2] , [3] ]. Dermal fibroblasts, the predominant cell type, maintain skin mechanical strength and elasticity by regulating the dynamic balance of collagen, elastin, and other extracellular matrix (ECM) components. However, aging fibroblasts exhibit a marked decrease in proliferative capacity, biosynthetic activity, and mechanosensitivity [ 4 , 5 ]. This impairment stems from the dysregulation of key signaling networks, such as the inhibition of the YAP/TAZ pathway and the aberrant activation of the CCN1/TGF-β/AP-1 signaling axis, which together accelerate ECM degradation and the emergence of skin aging phenotypes [ 6 , 7 ].
Accumulating evidence implicates oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, and aberrant lipid metabolism as fundamental drivers of skin cell dysfunction and structural degradation [ 8 ]. Among these, ferroptosis – an iron-dependent programmed cell death driven by lipid peroxidation, is gaining increasing attention for its role in skin pathologies, fibroblast dysfunction, and oxidative stress-associated aging [ 9 ]. Hallmarks include diminished or reduced activity of glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4), glutathione (GSH) depletion, and toxic lipid peroxide accumulation, culminating in catastrophic membrane damage [ 10 ]. Indeed, emerging studies link ferroptosis during the skin aging process with collagen degradation, fibroblast death, and compromised ECM stability. For instance, critical environmental stressors such as ultraviolet radiation (UVR) have been shown to significantly promote the activation of ferroptosis in fibroblasts, thereby exacerbating their functional decline and dermal structural damage [ 11 ].

### 2.1. Phenotypic and molecular characterization of the biphasic regulation of HFFs proliferation by retinol
To systematically characterize the concentration-dependent effects of retinol on HFF proliferation and identify optimal concentrations for subsequent multi-omics analyses, we performed a series of phenotypic and molecular assessments. First, the cell viability assays revealed that retinol exerts a pronounced biphasic, concentration-dependent regulation on HFF proliferation ( Fig. 1 A). At a low concentration (≤10 μg/mL), retinol significantly promoted HFF proliferation, peaking at 5 μg/mL ( P &lt; 0.001). Conversely, at a high concentration (≥30 μg/mL), retinol strongly inhibited HFF proliferation ( P &lt; 0.001).

### 2.2. Concentration-dependent reprogramming of HFF transcriptional networks by retinol

### 2.5. Multi-omics data drives the pinpointing of core toxicity pathways
To systematically integrate multidimensional datasets and elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying the biphasic effect of retinol, we performed a multi-omics integrative analysis. First, the global comparison of the transcriptome and proteome revealed that extensive post-transcriptional regulation is a key feature of retinol action. The nine-quadrant plot ( Fig. 5 A) showed that, under both treatment concentrations, a large number of gene-protein pairs deviated from the diagonal, suggesting the induction of complex post-transcriptional regulatory patterns by retinol treatment. In the A5 group, fewer gene–protein pairs exhibited consistent changes, and no statistically significant pathway enrichment was observed. Functional annotations of key molecules such as dehydrogenase/reductase 3 ( DHRS3 ), G protein-coupled receptor class C group 5 member A ( GPRC5A ), inhibitor of DNA binding 1 ( ID1 ), helicase, lymphoid specific ( HELLS ), and SMAD family member 3 ( SMAD3 ) indicated their involvement in retinoic acid signaling, DNA replication, and differentiation inhibition. In contrast, the A30 group showed a significant enrichment of coordinately changed genes and proteins in several core pathways ( Fig. 5 B). ### 3. Discussion
This study employed an integrative systems biology approach, combining multi-omics analysis with GEMs to elucidate retinol's biphasic effects on proliferation of HFFs. The results suggest that the concentration-dependent effects of retinol on HFF proliferation may be governed by a metabolic transition: at low concentrations, retinol appears to optimize the metabolic network to support signal transduction and promote proliferative capacity. In contrast, at high concentrations, it appears to provoke metabolic dysregulation and oxidative stress, potentially leading to ferroptosis.
At low concentrations, retinol seems to promote cell proliferation by inducing an NADPH-centric metabolic reprogramming ( Fig. 7 ). Driven by a robust activation of retinol-specific signaling ( DHRS3, CRABP2 ), this reprogramming may involve a dual metabolic adjustment: on one hand, the cell likely enhances the NADPH regeneration by upregulating G6PD and PGD ( Fig. 7 A and B), the rate-limiting enzyme of the pentose phosphate pathway (PPP). This is further supported by the downregulation of the competing enzyme AKR1B1 and the high weight assigned to the pentose and glucuronate interconversions pathway in our metabolic model. On the other hand, to prevent the exhaustion of this critical resource, cells actively suppress major NADPH-consuming pathways. Specifically, both multi-omics profiling and targeted qPCR confirmed a dramatic transcriptional suppression of central cholesterol biosynthesis hubs, including HMGCR and LDLR ( Fig. 7 A and B). This dual metabolic mechanism—boosting NADPH synthesis while curtailing its consumption in lipid metabolism—may contributes to the maintenance of a robust NADPH pool [ 50 ], which in turn likely enhances both the antioxidant systems [ 51 ] and retinoic acid (RA) metabolism [ 52 ], ultimately likely contributing to a favorable redox microenvironment for cell proliferation ( Fig. 7 C).

### Abstract
Retinol is widely used in skin anti-aging, yet its effects exhibit significant concentration dependence. However, the underlying biphasic mechanism, that is low concentrations promote cell proliferation while high concentrations are inhibitory, remains incompletely understood. Here, we employed multi-omics analysis and genome-scale metabolic models (GEMs) to systematically infer the key molecular networks underlying the distinct effects of retinol concentrations on human foreskin fibroblasts (HFFs). Our results suggest that low retinol concentrations appear to promote proliferation by activating the canonical retinoic acid signaling pathway and inducing sophisticated metabolic reprogramming. This reprogramming appears to involve prioritizing the coenzyme NADPH for retinol processing and antioxidant defense, which is associated with a compensatory suppression of NADPH-consuming cholesterol biosynthesis. This metabolic shift may foster a favorable intracellular environment for growth. Conversely, high concentrations are linked to a multi-system injury cascade. The observed key feature likely involves significant oxidative stress, associated with the buildup of toxic metabolic intermediates and a pro-inflammatory lipid storm marked by elevated leukotrienes. These stress responses align with the signatures of ferroptosis, a form of programmed cell death characterized by glutathione (GSH) defense system collapse and downregulation of the key regulator GPX4 . Our findings suggest a potential mechanistic transition from a state of metabolic optimization to the activation of stress-induced injury pathways in response to varying retinol concentrations. This study provides a systems-level framework for understanding retinol's biphasic effects and highlights key regulatory nodes (e.g., DHRS3 , GCLM ) as potential targets for future research. ### 1. Introduction
Skin aging is a complex biological process driven by intrinsic factors (e.g., genetics) and extrinsic stressors (e.g., UV exposure). Its core pathophysiology involves the progressive deterioration of dermal structure and function [ [1] , [2] , [3] ]. Dermal fibroblasts, the predominant cell type, maintain skin mechanical strength and elasticity by regulating the dynamic balance of collagen, elastin, and other extracellular matrix (ECM) components. However, aging fibroblasts exhibit a marked decrease in proliferative capacity, biosynthetic activity, and mechanosensitivity [ 4 , 5 ]. This impairment stems from the dysregulation of key signaling networks, such as the inhibition of the YAP/TAZ pathway and the aberrant activation of the CCN1/TGF-β/AP-1 signaling axis, which together accelerate ECM degradation and the emergence of skin aging phenotypes [ 6 , 7 ].
Accumulating evidence implicates oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, and aberrant lipid metabolism as fundamental drivers of skin cell dysfunction and structural degradation [ 8 ]. Among these, ferroptosis – an iron-dependent programmed cell death driven by lipid peroxidation, is gaining increasing attention for its role in skin pathologies, fibroblast dysfunction, and oxidative stress-associated aging [ 9 ]. Hallmarks include diminished or reduced activity of glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4), glutathione (GSH) depletion, and toxic lipid peroxide accumulation, culminating in catastrophic membrane damage [ 10 ]. Indeed, emerging studies link ferroptosis during the skin aging process with collagen degradation, fibroblast death, and compromised ECM stability. For instance, critical environmental stressors such as ultraviolet radiation (UVR) have been shown to significantly promote the activation of ferroptosis in fibroblasts, thereby exacerbating their functional decline and dermal structural damage [ 11 ].

### 2.1. Phenotypic and molecular characterization of the biphasic regulation of HFFs proliferation by retinol
To systematically characterize the concentration-dependent effects of retinol on HFF proliferation and identify optimal concentrations for subsequent multi-omics analyses, we performed a series of phenotypic and molecular assessments. First, the cell viability assays revealed that retinol exerts a pronounced biphasic, concentration-dependent regulation on HFF proliferation ( Fig. 1 A). At a low concentration (≤10 μg/mL), retinol significantly promoted HFF proliferation, peaking at 5 μg/mL ( P &lt; 0.001). Conversely, at a high concentration (≥30 μg/mL), retinol strongly inhibited HFF proliferation ( P &lt; 0.001).

### 2.2. Concentration-dependent reprogramming of HFF transcriptional networks by retinol
To investigate the concentration-dependent effects of retinol on the transcriptional landscape of HFF cells, we treated samples with three concentrations: untreated control group (WT), low concentration retinol (5 μg/mL, A5), and high concentration retinol (30 μg/mL, A30), followed by RNA sequencing for each group. The t-SNE cluster analysis confirmed high reproducibility within each group, and showed that the samples from different groups were separated into three distinct clusters ( Fig. 2 A). Differential expression analysis showed that, compared with the WT group, we identified 1348 significantly differentially expressed genes (DEGs) (680 down-regulated), whereas the A30 group induced a more extensive transcriptional reprogramming with 3135 DEGs (1729 down-regulated) ( Fig. 2 B and C). Among these, 508 genes were commonly altered in both treatment groups ( Fig. 2 B), suggesting that the addition of retinol has a broad effect on gene expression.

### 2.5. Multi-omics data drives the pinpointing of core toxicity pathways
To systematically integrate multidimensional datasets and elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying the biphasic effect of retinol, we performed a multi-omics integrative analysis. First, the global comparison of the transcriptome and proteome revealed that extensive post-transcriptional regulation is a key feature of retinol action. The nine-quadrant plot ( Fig. 5 A) showed that, under both treatment concentrations, a large number of gene-protein pairs deviated from the diagonal, suggesting the induction of complex post-transcriptional regulatory patterns by retinol treatment. In the A5 group, fewer gene–protein pairs exhibited consistent changes, and no statistically significant pathway enrichment was observed. Functional annotations of key molecules such as dehydrogenase/reductase 3 ( DHRS3 ), G protein-coupled receptor class C group 5 member A ( GPRC5A ), inhibitor of DNA binding 1 ( ID1 ), helicase, lymphoid specific ( HELLS ), and SMAD family member 3 ( SMAD3 ) indicated their involvement in retinoic acid signaling, DNA replication, and differentiation inhibition. In contrast, the A30 group showed a significant enrichment of coordinately changed genes and proteins in several core pathways ( Fig. 5 B). The downregulated pathways were significantly enriched in cell-related growth, including cell cycle, cholesterol metabolism, and the Hippo signaling pathway. The upregulated pathways were enriched in ferroptosis ( P = 6.60∗10 −6 ) and phagosome ( P = 2.05∗10 −3 ).

### 2.6. GEMs uncover metabolic flux remodeling: a model-based framework for retinol's biphasic effect

### 3. Discussion
This study employed an integrative systems biology approach, combining multi-omics analysis with GEMs to elucidate retinol's biphasic effects on proliferation of HFFs. The results suggest that the concentration-dependent effects of retinol on HFF proliferation may be governed by a metabolic transition: at low concentrations, retinol appears to optimize the metabolic network to support signal transduction and promote proliferative capacity. In contrast, at high concentrations, it appears to provoke metabolic dysregulation and oxidative stress, potentially leading to ferroptosis.
At low concentrations, retinol seems to promote cell proliferation by inducing an NADPH-centric metabolic reprogramming ( Fig. 7 ). Driven by a robust activation of retinol-specific signaling ( DHRS3, CRABP2 ), this reprogramming may involve a dual metabolic adjustment: on one hand, the cell likely enhances the NADPH regeneration by upregulating G6PD and PGD ( Fig. 7 A and B), the rate-limiting enzyme of the pentose phosphate pathway (PPP). This is further supported by the downregulation of the competing enzyme AKR1B1 and the high weight assigned to the pentose and glucuronate interconversions pathway in our metabolic model. On the other hand, to prevent the exhaustion of this critical resource, cells actively suppress major NADPH-consuming pathways. Specifically, both multi-omics profiling and targeted qPCR confirmed a dramatic transcriptional suppression of central cholesterol biosynthesis hubs, including HMGCR and LDLR ( Fig. 7 A and B). This dual metabolic mechanism—boosting NADPH synthesis while curtailing its consumption in lipid metabolism—may contributes to the maintenance of a robust NADPH pool [ 50 ], which in turn likely enhances both the antioxidant systems [ 51 ] and retinoic acid (RA) metabolism [ 52 ], ultimately likely contributing to a favorable redox microenvironment for cell proliferation ( Fig. 7 C).

### Abstract
Retinol is widely used in skin anti-aging, yet its effects exhibit significant concentration dependence. However, the underlying biphasic mechanism, that is low concentrations promote cell proliferation while high concentrations are inhibitory, remains incompletely understood. Here, we employed multi-omics analysis and genome-scale metabolic models (GEMs) to systematically infer the key molecular networks underlying the distinct effects of retinol concentrations on human foreskin fibroblasts (HFFs). Our results suggest that low retinol concentrations appear to promote proliferation by activating the canonical retinoic acid signaling pathway and inducing sophisticated metabolic reprogramming. This reprogramming appears to involve prioritizing the coenzyme NADPH for retinol processing and antioxidant defense, which is associated with a compensatory suppression of NADPH-consuming cholesterol biosynthesis. This metabolic shift may foster a favorable intracellular environment for growth. Conversely, high concentrations are linked to a multi-system injury cascade. The observed key feature likely involves significant oxidative stress, associated with the buildup of toxic metabolic intermediates and a pro-inflammatory lipid storm marked by elevated leukotrienes. These stress responses align with the signatures of ferroptosis, a form of programmed cell death characterized by glutathione (GSH) defense system collapse and downregulation of the key regulator GPX4 . Our findings suggest a potential mechanistic transition from a state of metabolic optimization to the activation of stress-induced injury pathways in response to varying retinol concentrations. This study provides a systems-level framework for understanding retinol's biphasic effects and highlights key regulatory nodes (e.g., DHRS3 , GCLM ) as potential targets for future research. ### 1. Introduction
Skin aging is a complex biological process driven by intrinsic factors (e.g., genetics) and extrinsic stressors (e.g., UV exposure). Its core pathophysiology involves the progressive deterioration of dermal structure and function [ [1] , [2] , [3] ]. Dermal fibroblasts, the predominant cell type, maintain skin mechanical strength and elasticity by regulating the dynamic balance of collagen, elastin, and other extracellular matrix (ECM) components. However, aging fibroblasts exhibit a marked decrease in proliferative capacity, biosynthetic activity, and mechanosensitivity [ 4 , 5 ]. This impairment stems from the dysregulation of key signaling networks, such as the inhibition of the YAP/TAZ pathway and the aberrant activation of the CCN1/TGF-β/AP-1 signaling axis, which together accelerate ECM degradation and the emergence of skin aging phenotypes [ 6 , 7 ].
Accumulating evidence implicates oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, and aberrant lipid metabolism as fundamental drivers of skin cell dysfunction and structural degradation [ 8 ]. Among these, ferroptosis – an iron-dependent programmed cell death driven by lipid peroxidation, is gaining increasing attention for its role in skin pathologies, fibroblast dysfunction, and oxidative stress-associated aging [ 9 ]. Hallmarks include diminished or reduced activity of glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4), glutathione (GSH) depletion, and toxic lipid peroxide accumulation, culminating in catastrophic membrane damage [ 10 ]. Indeed, emerging studies link ferroptosis during the skin aging process with collagen degradation, fibroblast death, and compromised ECM stability. For instance, critical environmental stressors such as ultraviolet radiation (UVR) have been shown to significantly promote the activation of ferroptosis in fibroblasts, thereby exacerbating their functional decline and dermal structural damage [ 11 ].

### 2.1. Phenotypic and molecular characterization of the biphasic regulation of HFFs proliferation by retinol
To systematically characterize the concentration-dependent effects of retinol on HFF proliferation and identify optimal concentrations for subsequent multi-omics analyses, we performed a series of phenotypic and molecular assessments. First, the cell viability assays revealed that retinol exerts a pronounced biphasic, concentration-dependent regulation on HFF proliferation ( Fig. 1 A). At a low concentration (≤10 μg/mL), retinol significantly promoted HFF proliferation, peaking at 5 μg/mL ( P &lt; 0.001). Conversely, at a high concentration (≥30 μg/mL), retinol strongly inhibited HFF proliferation ( P &lt; 0.001).

### 2.2. Concentration-dependent reprogramming of HFF transcriptional networks by retinol
To investigate the concentration-dependent effects of retinol on the transcriptional landscape of HFF cells, we treated samples with three concentrations: untreated control group (WT), low concentration retinol (5 μg/mL, A5), and high concentration retinol (30 μg/mL, A30), followed by RNA sequencing for each group. The t-SNE cluster analysis confirmed high reproducibility within each group, and showed that the samples from different groups were separated into three distinct clusters ( Fig. 2 A). Differential expression analysis showed that, compared with the WT group, we identified 1348 significantly differentially expressed genes (DEGs) (680 down-regulated), whereas the A30 group induced a more extensive transcriptional reprogramming with 3135 DEGs (1729 down-regulated) ( Fig. 2 B and C). Among these, 508 genes were commonly altered in both treatment groups ( Fig. 2 B), suggesting that the addition of retinol has a broad effect on gene expression.

### 2.5. Multi-omics data drives the pinpointing of core toxicity pathways
To systematically integrate multidimensional datasets and elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying the biphasic effect of retinol, we performed a multi-omics integrative analysis. First, the global comparison of the transcriptome and proteome revealed that extensive post-transcriptional regulation is a key feature of retinol action. The nine-quadrant plot ( Fig. 5 A) showed that, under both treatment concentrations, a large number of gene-protein pairs deviated from the diagonal, suggesting the induction of complex post-transcriptional regulatory patterns by retinol treatment. In the A5 group, fewer gene–protein pairs exhibited consistent changes, and no statistically significant pathway enrichment was observed. Functional annotations of key molecules such as dehydrogenase/reductase 3 ( DHRS3 ), G protein-coupled receptor class C group 5 member A ( GPRC5A ), inhibitor of DNA binding 1 ( ID1 ), helicase, lymphoid specific ( HELLS ), and SMAD family member 3 ( SMAD3 ) indicated their involvement in retinoic acid signaling, DNA replication, and differentiation inhibition. In contrast, the A30 group showed a significant enrichment of coordinately changed genes and proteins in several core pathways ( Fig. 5 B). The downregulated pathways were significantly enriched in cell-related growth, including cell cycle, cholesterol metabolism, and the Hippo signaling pathway. The upregulated pathways were enriched in ferroptosis ( P = 6.60∗10 −6 ) and phagosome ( P = 2.05∗10 −3 ).

### 2.6. GEMs uncover metabolic flux remodeling: a model-based framework for retinol's biphasic effect

### 3. Discussion
This study employed an integrative systems biology approach, combining multi-omics analysis with GEMs to elucidate retinol's biphasic effects on proliferation of HFFs. The results suggest that the concentration-dependent effects of retinol on HFF proliferation may be governed by a metabolic transition: at low concentrations, retinol appears to optimize the metabolic network to support signal transduction and promote proliferative capacity. In contrast, at high concentrations, it appears to provoke metabolic dysregulation and oxidative stress, potentially leading to ferroptosis.
At low concentrations, retinol seems to promote cell proliferation by inducing an NADPH-centric metabolic reprogramming ( Fig. 7 ). Driven by a robust activation of retinol-specific signaling ( DHRS3, CRABP2 ), this reprogramming may involve a dual metabolic adjustment: on one hand, the cell likely enhances the NADPH regeneration by upregulating G6PD and PGD ( Fig. 7 A and B), the rate-limiting enzyme of the pentose phosphate pathway (PPP). This is further supported by the downregulation of the competing enzyme AKR1B1 and the high weight assigned to the pentose and glucuronate interconversions pathway in our metabolic model. On the other hand, to prevent the exhaustion of this critical resource, cells actively suppress major NADPH-consuming pathways. Specifically, both multi-omics profiling and targeted qPCR confirmed a dramatic transcriptional suppression of central cholesterol biosynthesis hubs, including HMGCR and LDLR ( Fig. 7 A and B). This dual metabolic mechanism—boosting NADPH synthesis while curtailing its consumption in lipid metabolism—may contributes to the maintenance of a robust NADPH pool [ 50 ], which in turn likely enhances both the antioxidant systems [ 51 ] and retinoic acid (RA) metabolism [ 52 ], ultimately likely contributing to a favorable redox microenvironment for cell proliferation ( Fig. 7 C).

### 4.1. Cell culture with retinol treatment
Human foreskin fibroblasts (HFFs; Chinese Academy of Sciences Stem Cell Bank) were routinely cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Gibco) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (Gibco) at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO 2 . ### Abstract
Retinol is widely used in skin anti-aging, yet its effects exhibit significant concentration dependence. However, the underlying biphasic mechanism, that is low concentrations promote cell proliferation while high concentrations are inhibitory, remains incompletely understood. Here, we employed multi-omics analysis and genome-scale metabolic models (GEMs) to systematically infer the key molecular networks underlying the distinct effects of retinol concentrations on human foreskin fibroblasts (HFFs). Our results suggest that low retinol concentrations appear to promote proliferation by activating the canonical retinoic acid signaling pathway and inducing sophisticated metabolic reprogramming. This reprogramming appears to involve prioritizing the coenzyme NADPH for retinol processing and antioxidant defense, which is associated with a compensatory suppression of NADPH-consuming cholesterol biosynthesis. This metabolic shift may foster a favorable intracellular environment for growth. Conversely, high concentrations are linked to a multi-system injury cascade. The observed key feature likely involves significant oxidative stress, associated with the buildup of toxic metabolic intermediates and a pro-inflammatory lipid storm marked by elevated leukotrienes. These stress responses align with the signatures of ferroptosis, a form of programmed cell death characterized by glutathione (GSH) defense system collapse and downregulation of the key regulator GPX4 . Our findings suggest a potential mechanistic transition from a state of metabolic optimization to the activation of stress-induced injury pathways in response to varying retinol concentrations. This study provides a systems-level framework for understanding retinol's biphasic effects and highlights key regulatory nodes (e.g., DHRS3 , GCLM ) as potential targets for future research. ### 1. Introduction
Skin aging is a complex biological process driven by intrinsic factors (e.g., genetics) and extrinsic stressors (e.g., UV exposure). Its core pathophysiology involves the progressive deterioration of dermal structure and function [ [1] , [2] , [3] ]. Dermal fibroblasts, the predominant cell type, maintain skin mechanical strength and elasticity by regulating the dynamic balance of collagen, elastin, and other extracellular matrix (ECM) components. However, aging fibroblasts exhibit a marked decrease in proliferative capacity, biosynthetic activity, and mechanosensitivity [ 4 , 5 ]. This impairment stems from the dysregulation of key signaling networks, such as the inhibition of the YAP/TAZ pathway and the aberrant activation of the CCN1/TGF-β/AP-1 signaling axis, which together accelerate ECM degradation and the emergence of skin aging phenotypes [ 6 , 7 ].
Accumulating evidence implicates oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, and aberrant lipid metabolism as fundamental drivers of skin cell dysfunction and structural degradation [ 8 ]. Among these, ferroptosis – an iron-dependent programmed cell death driven by lipid peroxidation, is gaining increasing attention for its role in skin pathologies, fibroblast dysfunction, and oxidative stress-associated aging [ 9 ]. Hallmarks include diminished or reduced activity of glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4), glutathione (GSH) depletion, and toxic lipid peroxide accumulation, culminating in catastrophic membrane damage [ 10 ]. Indeed, emerging studies link ferroptosis during the skin aging process with collagen degradation, fibroblast death, and compromised ECM stability. For instance, critical environmental stressors such as ultraviolet radiation (UVR) have been shown to significantly promote the activation of ferroptosis in fibroblasts, thereby exacerbating their functional decline and dermal structural damage [ 11 ].

### 2.1. Phenotypic and molecular characterization of the biphasic regulation of HFFs proliferation by retinol
To systematically characterize the concentration-dependent effects of retinol on HFF proliferation and identify optimal concentrations for subsequent multi-omics analyses, we performed a series of phenotypic and molecular assessments. First, the cell viability assays revealed that retinol exerts a pronounced biphasic, concentration-dependent regulation on HFF proliferation ( Fig. 1 A). At a low concentration (≤10 μg/mL), retinol significantly promoted HFF proliferation, peaking at 5 μg/mL ( P &lt; 0.001). Conversely, at a high concentration (≥30 μg/mL), retinol strongly inhibited HFF proliferation ( P &lt; 0.001).

### 2.2. Concentration-dependent reprogramming of HFF transcriptional networks by retinol
To investigate the concentration-dependent effects of retinol on the transcriptional landscape of HFF cells, we treated samples with three concentrations: untreated control group (WT), low concentration retinol (5 μg/mL, A5), and high concentration retinol (30 μg/mL, A30), followed by RNA sequencing for each group. The t-SNE cluster analysis confirmed high reproducibility within each group, and showed that the samples from different groups were separated into three distinct clusters ( Fig. 2 A). Differential expression analysis showed that, compared with the WT group, we identified 1348 significantly differentially expressed genes (DEGs) (680 down-regulated), whereas the A30 group induced a more extensive transcriptional reprogramming with 3135 DEGs (1729 down-regulated) ( Fig. 2 B and C). Among these, 508 genes were commonly altered in both treatment groups ( Fig. 2 B), suggesting that the addition of retinol has a broad effect on gene expression.

### 2.5. Multi-omics data drives the pinpointing of core toxicity pathways
To systematically integrate multidimensional datasets and elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying the biphasic effect of retinol, we performed a multi-omics integrative analysis. First, the global comparison of the transcriptome and proteome revealed that extensive post-transcriptional regulation is a key feature of retinol action. The nine-quadrant plot ( Fig. 5 A) showed that, under both treatment concentrations, a large number of gene-protein pairs deviated from the diagonal, suggesting the induction of complex post-transcriptional regulatory patterns by retinol treatment. In the A5 group, fewer gene–protein pairs exhibited consistent changes, and no statistically significant pathway enrichment was observed. Functional annotations of key molecules such as dehydrogenase/reductase 3 ( DHRS3 ), G protein-coupled receptor class C group 5 member A ( GPRC5A ), inhibitor of DNA binding 1 ( ID1 ), helicase, lymphoid specific ( HELLS ), and SMAD family member 3 ( SMAD3 ) indicated their involvement in retinoic acid signaling, DNA replication, and differentiation inhibition. In contrast, the A30 group showed a significant enrichment of coordinately changed genes and proteins in several core pathways ( Fig. 5 B). The downregulated pathways were significantly enriched in cell-related growth, including cell cycle, cholesterol metabolism, and the Hippo signaling pathway. The upregulated pathways were enriched in ferroptosis ( P = 6.60∗10 −6 ) and phagosome ( P = 2.05∗10 −3 ).

### 3. Discussion
This study employed an integrative systems biology approach, combining multi-omics analysis with GEMs to elucidate retinol's biphasic effects on proliferation of HFFs. The results suggest that the concentration-dependent effects of retinol on HFF proliferation may be governed by a metabolic transition: at low concentrations, retinol appears to optimize the metabolic network to support signal transduction and promote proliferative capacity. In contrast, at high concentrations, it appears to provoke metabolic dysregulation and oxidative stress, potentially leading to ferroptosis.
At low concentrations, retinol seems to promote cell proliferation by inducing an NADPH-centric metabolic reprogramming ( Fig. 7 ). Driven by a robust activation of retinol-specific signaling ( DHRS3, CRABP2 ), this reprogramming may involve a dual metabolic adjustment: on one hand, the cell likely enhances the NADPH regeneration by upregulating G6PD and PGD ( Fig. 7 A and B), the rate-limiting enzyme of the pentose phosphate pathway (PPP). This is further supported by the downregulation of the competing enzyme AKR1B1 and the high weight assigned to the pentose and glucuronate interconversions pathway in our metabolic model. On the other hand, to prevent the exhaustion of this critical resource, cells actively suppress major NADPH-consuming pathways. Specifically, both multi-omics profiling and targeted qPCR confirmed a dramatic transcriptional suppression of central cholesterol biosynthesis hubs, including HMGCR and LDLR ( Fig. 7 A and B). This dual metabolic mechanism—boosting NADPH synthesis while curtailing its consumption in lipid metabolism—may contributes to the maintenance of a robust NADPH pool [ 50 ], which in turn likely enhances both the antioxidant systems [ 51 ] and retinoic acid (RA) metabolism [ 52 ], ultimately likely contributing to a favorable redox microenvironment for cell proliferation ( Fig. 7 C).

### Abstract
Retinol is widely used in skin anti-aging, yet its effects exhibit significant concentration dependence. However, the underlying biphasic mechanism, that is low concentrations promote cell proliferation while high concentrations are inhibitory, remains incompletely understood. Here, we employed multi-omics analysis and genome-scale metabolic models (GEMs) to systematically infer the key molecular networks underlying the distinct effects of retinol concentrations on human foreskin fibroblasts (HFFs). Our results suggest that low retinol concentrations appear to promote proliferation by activating the canonical retinoic acid signaling pathway and inducing sophisticated metabolic reprogramming. This reprogramming appears to involve prioritizing the coenzyme NADPH for retinol processing and antioxidant defense, which is associated with a compensatory suppression of NADPH-consuming cholesterol biosynthesis. This metabolic shift may foster a favorable intracellular environment for growth. Conversely, high concentrations are linked to a multi-system injury cascade. The observed key feature likely involves significant oxidative stress, associated with the buildup of toxic metabolic intermediates and a pro-inflammatory lipid storm marked by elevated leukotrienes. These stress responses align with the signatures of ferroptosis, a form of programmed cell death characterized by glutathione (GSH) defense system collapse and downregulation of the key regulator GPX4 . Our findings suggest a potential mechanistic transition from a state of metabolic optimization to the activation of stress-induced injury pathways in response to varying retinol concentrations. This study provides a systems-level framework for understanding retinol's biphasic effects and highlights key regulatory nodes (e.g., DHRS3 , GCLM ) as potential targets for future research. ### 1. Introduction
Skin aging is a complex biological process driven by intrinsic factors (e.g., genetics) and extrinsic stressors (e.g., UV exposure). Its core pathophysiology involves the progressive deterioration of dermal structure and function [ [1] , [2] , [3] ]. Dermal fibroblasts, the predominant cell type, maintain skin mechanical strength and elasticity by regulating the dynamic balance of collagen, elastin, and other extracellular matrix (ECM) components. However, aging fibroblasts exhibit a marked decrease in proliferative capacity, biosynthetic activity, and mechanosensitivity [ 4 , 5 ]. This impairment stems from the dysregulation of key signaling networks, such as the inhibition of the YAP/TAZ pathway and the aberrant activation of the CCN1/TGF-β/AP-1 signaling axis, which together accelerate ECM degradation and the emergence of skin aging phenotypes [ 6 , 7 ].
Accumulating evidence implicates oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, and aberrant lipid metabolism as fundamental drivers of skin cell dysfunction and structural degradation [ 8 ]. Among these, ferroptosis – an iron-dependent programmed cell death driven by lipid peroxidation, is gaining increasing attention for its role in skin pathologies, fibroblast dysfunction, and oxidative stress-associated aging [ 9 ]. Hallmarks include diminished or reduced activity of glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4), glutathione (GSH) depletion, and toxic lipid peroxide accumulation, culminating in catastrophic membrane damage [ 10 ]. Indeed, emerging studies link ferroptosis during the skin aging process with collagen degradation, fibroblast death, and compromised ECM stability. For instance, critical environmental stressors such as ultraviolet radiation (UVR) have been shown to significantly promote the activation of ferroptosis in fibroblasts, thereby exacerbating their functional decline and dermal structural damage [ 11 ].

### 2.1. Phenotypic and molecular characterization of the biphasic regulation of HFFs proliferation by retinol
To systematically characterize the concentration-dependent effects of retinol on HFF proliferation and identify optimal concentrations for subsequent multi-omics analyses, we performed a series of phenotypic and molecular assessments. First, the cell viability assays revealed that retinol exerts a pronounced biphasic, concentration-dependent regulation on HFF proliferation ( Fig. 1 A). At a low concentration (≤10 μg/mL), retinol significantly promoted HFF proliferation, peaking at 5 μg/mL ( P &lt; 0.001). Conversely, at a high concentration (≥30 μg/mL), retinol strongly inhibited HFF proliferation ( P &lt; 0.001).

### 2.2. Concentration-dependent reprogramming of HFF transcriptional networks by retinol
To investigate the concentration-dependent effects of retinol on the transcriptional landscape of HFF cells, we treated samples with three concentrations: untreated control group (WT), low concentration retinol (5 μg/mL, A5), and high concentration retinol (30 μg/mL, A30), followed by RNA sequencing for each group. The t-SNE cluster analysis confirmed high reproducibility within each group, and showed that the samples from different groups were separated into three distinct clusters ( Fig. 2 A). Differential expression analysis showed that, compared with the WT group, we identified 1348 significantly differentially expressed genes (DEGs) (680 down-regulated), whereas the A30 group induced a more extensive transcriptional reprogramming with 3135 DEGs (1729 down-regulated) ( Fig. 2 B and C). Among these, 508 genes were commonly altered in both treatment groups ( Fig. 2 B), suggesting that the addition of retinol has a broad effect on gene expression.

### 2.5. Multi-omics data drives the pinpointing of core toxicity pathways
To systematically integrate multidimensional datasets and elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying the biphasic effect of retinol, we performed a multi-omics integrative analysis. First, the global comparison of the transcriptome and proteome revealed that extensive post-transcriptional regulation is a key feature of retinol action. The nine-quadrant plot ( Fig. 5 A) showed that, under both treatment concentrations, a large number of gene-protein pairs deviated from the diagonal, suggesting the induction of complex post-transcriptional regulatory patterns by retinol treatment. In the A5 group, fewer gene–protein pairs exhibited consistent changes, and no statistically significant pathway enrichment was observed. Functional annotations of key molecules such as dehydrogenase/reductase 3 ( DHRS3 ), G protein-coupled receptor class C group 5 member A ( GPRC5A ), inhibitor of DNA binding 1 ( ID1 ), helicase, lymphoid specific ( HELLS ), and SMAD family member 3 ( SMAD3 ) indicated their involvement in retinoic acid signaling, DNA replication, and differentiation inhibition. In contrast, the A30 group showed a significant enrichment of coordinately changed genes and proteins in several core pathways ( Fig. 5 B). The downregulated pathways were significantly enriched in cell-related growth, including cell cycle, cholesterol metabolism, and the Hippo signaling pathway. The upregulated pathways were enriched in ferroptosis ( P = 6.60∗10 −6 ) and phagosome ( P = 2.05∗10 −3 ).

### 3. Discussion
This study employed an integrative systems biology approach, combining multi-omics analysis with GEMs to elucidate retinol's biphasic effects on proliferation of HFFs. The results suggest that the concentration-dependent effects of retinol on HFF proliferation may be governed by a metabolic transition: at low concentrations, retinol appears to optimize the metabolic network to support signal transduction and promote proliferative capacity. In contrast, at high concentrations, it appears to provoke metabolic dysregulation and oxidative stress, potentially leading to ferroptosis.
At low concentrations, retinol seems to promote cell proliferation by inducing an NADPH-centric metabolic reprogramming ( Fig. 7 ). Driven by a robust activation of retinol-specific signaling ( DHRS3, CRABP2 ), this reprogramming may involve a dual metabolic adjustment: on one hand, the cell likely enhances the NADPH regeneration by upregulating G6PD and PGD ( Fig. 7 A and B), the rate-limiting enzyme of the pentose phosphate pathway (PPP). This is further supported by the downregulation of the competing enzyme AKR1B1 and the high weight assigned to the pentose and glucuronate interconversions pathway in our metabolic model. On the other hand, to prevent the exhaustion of this critical resource, cells actively suppress major NADPH-consuming pathways. Specifically, both multi-omics profiling and targeted qPCR confirmed a dramatic transcriptional suppression of central cholesterol biosynthesis hubs, including HMGCR and LDLR ( Fig. 7 A and B). This dual metabolic mechanism—boosting NADPH synthesis while curtailing its consumption in lipid metabolism—may contributes to the maintenance of a robust NADPH pool [ 50 ], which in turn likely enhances both the antioxidant systems [ 51 ] and retinoic acid (RA) metabolism [ 52 ], ultimately likely contributing to a favorable redox microenvironment for cell proliferation ( Fig. 7 C).

2. This study provides a systems-level framework for understanding retinol's biphasic effects and highlights key regulatory nodes (e.g., DHRS3, GCLM) as potential targets for future research.
3. Our results suggest that low retinol concentra­ tions appear to promote proliferation by activating the canonical retinoic acid signaling pathway and inducing sophisticated metabolic reprogramming.
4. Our findings suggest a potential mechanistic transition from a state of metabolic optimization to the activation of stress-induced injury pathways in response to varying retinol concentrations.
5. We found that, under retinol treatment, several key biological processes are consistently enriched, including cell cycle, cell adhesion, and extracel­ lular matrix (ECM)-related terms.
6. In the A5 group, we found signifi­ cant activation of key pathways like focal adhesion, cell adhesion mol­ ecules, and cytochrome P450 drug metabolism.
7. GCLM, representing a critical node that gauges cellular stress and probably determines progression toward fer­ roptosis, may serve as a therapeutic target to alleviate retinol-induced stress [58].
8. In sum, this study provides a systems-level insight into the potential molecular basis for retinol's biphasic effects.

# Evaluation of a novel laser-system operating at 1940 nm for weed control and determining the influence of biological and operational factors.

## 摘要
This study evaluates a novel laser-based pulse-system for weed control characterized by two primary innovations: a thulium-doped yttrium aluminum perovskite (Tm:YAP) laser operating at a 1940 nm wavelength aligned with the peak water absorption band to maximize photothermal efficiency and the implementation of pulsed laser operation. The system's efficacy was examined across varying weed species (dicots and monocots), growth stages (2-3, 4-5, and 7-8 leaf stages), and target locations (growth meristem and stem), alongside technical parameters including beam diameter (0.1-4.7 mm) and exposure time (0.25-2.5 s) and compared against a continuous-wave (CW) system.

Targeting the plant stem was significantly more effective than the meristem with mean biomass (proportion of control) values of approximately 0.0 and 0.25, respectively, following a treatment of 1.5 s with beam diameter of 1.6 mm. Across the majority of tested parameters, the pulsed configuration demonstrated no significant advantage over the CW system. Early treatment of the dicot Solanum nigrum at the 2-3 leaf stage resulted with 6% survival using energy density of only 1.5 J mm-2 (0.25 s exposure). However, sensitivity varied greatly by species; Abutilon theophrasti was significantly more susceptible than grass species (e.g., Sorghum halepense), and had a higher effective dose for a 50% response (ED50).

While pulsed and CW modes yielded similar results, indicating that total energy delivery governs the thermal response, the 1940 nm wavelength demonstrated superior energy efficiency compared to previously reported systems. These findings provide a solid foundation for developing energy-efficient, site-specific robotic weeding technologies. © 2026 The Author(s). ### 核心发现
1. The system's efficacy was examined across varying weed species (dicots and monocots), growth stages (2–3, 4–5, and 7–8 leaf stages), and target locations (growth meristem and stem), alongside technical parameters including beam diameter (0.1–4.7 mm) and exposure time (0.25–2.5 s) and compared against a continuous‐wave (CW) system.
2. The diameter of the laser beam plays a crucial role in outcome control.
3. A smaller beam reduces energy demands but makes it more challenging to accurately target the developing meristem.
4. Conversely, a larger beam requires less precision but may lead to inadequate damage or unintended energy dispersion beyond the target area.
5. These findings suggest that water absorption has a greater impact on control results than pigment absorption.
6. Therefore, the objectives of this study were to: (1) evaluate the efficacy of a laser system tuned to the water absorption peak at 1940 nm for weed control; (2) compare the performance of pulsed versus CW systems; and (3) determine the impact of biological (weed species, growth stage, and target location) and technical factors (beam diameter and exposure time) on the performance of this system.
7. These spot sizes were determined by the availability of optical components and to span a practical range of beam diameters relevant to the scale of the target plants.
8. While previous setups typically target the top‐facing meristem from above using a broad 6 mm spot profile and prolonged exposure times (1.0–4.0 s), our system utilizes a more effective stem‐cutting strategy compressed into a narrow focal point (0.4 and 0.8 mm diameters in the dose–response experiment).

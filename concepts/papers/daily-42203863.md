## 摘要
Domestication of indica rice facilitated better harvest and yield, however the molecular mechanisms that drove multitude of the associated phenotypes is poorly understood. A few genetic and epigenetic mechanisms have been attributed to indica rice domestication; however, upstream regulators of these variations are unknown. Here, we identified a copper (Cu)-dependent regulatory module, involved in the regulation of OsSPL9 TF and two classes of RNAs under its control. Differential accumulation of Cu-associated micro(mi)RNAs and Cu-associated protein-coding RNAs were a major portion of the differences between wild (Oryza nivara) and cultivated rice lines. We identified OsSPL9 as an upstream regulator of these changes through genetic and molecular analysis as well as by using Cu stressed conditions. OsSPL9 bound to the promoters of these genes through a conserved GTAC enriched motif. Although OsSPL9 might have Cu-independent roles, Cu-associated miRNAs and their targets acted as a regulatory loop with OsSPL9. Mis-expression of SPL9 alone, or any individual Cu-associated miRNA, also altered levels of other Cu-miRNAs and their cognate targets. OsSPL9-mediated regulation was closely linked to Cu accumulation and metabolism, indicating previously unappreciated roles of metal ions in mediating domestication-associated phenotypes. Our study facilitates a better understanding of the crosstalk between genetic and epigenetic regulation that contributed to indica rice domestication.

### Abstract
Domestication of indica rice facilitated better harvest and yield, however the molecular mechanisms that drove multitude of the associated phenotypes is poorly understood. A few genetic and epigenetic mechanisms have been attributed to indica rice domestication; however, upstream regulators of these variations are unknown. Here, we identified a copper (Cu)-dependent regulatory module, involved in the regulation of OsSPL9 TF and two classes of RNAs under its control. Differential accumulation of Cu-associated micro(mi)RNAs and Cu-associated protein-coding RNAs were a major portion of the differences between wild ( Oryza nivara ) and cultivated rice lines. We identified OsSPL9 as an upstream regulator of these changes through genetic and molecular analysis as well as by using Cu stressed conditions. OsSPL9 bound to the promoters of these genes through a conserved GTAC enriched motif. Although OsSPL9 might have Cu-independent roles, Cu-associated miRNAs and their targets acted as a regulatory loop with OsSPL9. Mis-expression of SPL9 alone, or any individual Cu-associated miRNA, also altered levels of other Cu-miRNAs and their cognate targets. OsSPL9-mediated regulation was closely linked to Cu accumulation and metabolism, indicating previously unappreciated roles of metal ions in mediating domestication-associated phenotypes. Our study facilitates a better understanding of the crosstalk between genetic and epigenetic regulation that contributed to indica rice domestication.

### Introduction
Domestication is a phenomenon of the evolution of crops assisted by cultivation, and it has induced diverse changes in plants, often referred to as domestication-associated traits 1 . Although genomic loci attributed to these phenotypes have been characterised to a greater extent from several studies in diverse crops, the mechanisms underlying this process have not been well understood. This difficulty was largely attributed to the fact that not all phenotypic changes in the course of domestication could be attributed to protein-coding genes 2 . Specifically, a majority of the QTLs attributed to distinct crop phenotypes did not overlap with specific protein-coding genes, and this led to the hypothesis that small (s)RNAs or other epigenetic regulators might be playing a dominant role in establishing domestication-associated phenotypes 3 – 7 . sRNAs as major regulators of gene expression and phenotypes in plants were well elucidated, along with their role in inducing heritable epigenetic marks such as DNA methylation and histone modifications 8 – 11 . Indeed, several landmark studies have revealed that there is a major influence of sRNAs, including miRNAs, in regulating gene expression changes resulting in crop domestication phenotypes. In agreement with this, a significant number of yield-related QTLs across crops overlapped with sRNAs, their targets or players involved in epigenetic modifications 12 – 15 .
The cultivated rice is thought to have originated from two wild relatives of cultivated rice, namely, Oryza nivara and O. rufipogon 16 . Among these, indica rice is of generally shorter stature with morphological and physiological features distinct from those of japonica and javanica rice lines. Several studies identified genetic and epigenetic regulators of indica rice domestication. Well-known phenotypes and associated genes identified in indica rice include an increase in grain shape 17 , grain colour, heading date 18 , loss of shattering 19 , cold tolerance 20

### Substantial changes in micronutrient-related genes between wild and cultivated rice
In order to identify differentially expressing genes between wild and cultivated rice line Pusa Basmati 1 (henceforth PB-1) across various growth stages, we performed RNA-seq analysis and obtained a mapping efficiency of 95% to the rice genome (IRGSP-1.0) (Supplementary Table 1 ). Nearly 25% of the genes that showed higher expression in O. nivara when compared to PB-1 were associated with disease resistance and secondary metabolic pathways (Supplementary Fig. 1A, B ). Such a relationship has been well-explained across several wild species and domesticated crops 65 – 68 . Among the prominent genes that exhibited reduced expression in cultivated rice PB-1 included disease resistance-associated MYBs, WRKY, RALF and bZIP TFs (Fig. 1A lower panel). About 713 genes out of 1335 DEGs had higher expression in PB-1 (Fig. 1A upper panel). As expected, growth-related and yield-associated genes, such as OsGLW7 69 and OsCLV2c 70 , were upregulated in PB-1, along with some of the most prominent macro and micronutrient and metal ion-associated genes. It is well known that improved nitrate metabolism is a major change responsible for increased yield across cultivated lines 71 and in agreement with this, around 70 nitrate-responsive genes showed higher expression in PB-1 (Supplementary Fig. 1C ). Similarly, iron-responsive genes also exhibited higher expression in PB-1 importance of which is unknown (Supplementary Fig. 1D ). Additionally, the expression of several phosphate-responsive genes was high in wild rice when compared to PB-1 (Supplementary Fig. 1E ). For example, a central regulator of phosphate signalling in rice named OsPHR2 and a high affinity phosphate transporter-OsPT4 were upregulated 2-fold and 16-fold, respectively, in O. nivara (Fig. 1B ). As implicated previously, phosphate levels and expression of phosphate-responsive genes are important factors in disease resistance 72 , 73 . ### Cu-associated miRNAs and their targets are differentially expressed in wild and cultivated rice
miRNAs are well-known regulators of a large family of genes, and they fine-tune gene expression during development and stress responses 5 , 8 , 82 . Several domestication-associated DEGs and phenotypes are regulated by miRNAs and other sRNAs in rice 3 , 12 , 83 – 86 . Based on degradome analysis 87 , we identified several differentially expressing miRNAs between wild and cultivated rice that targeted genes such as Os5NG4 and R2R3MYB. In order to get a comprehensive view of nutrient-associated miRNAs and their target genes, we analysed sRNA datasets across different tissues of wild and cultivated rice lines. Several nutrient associated miRNAs 88 were unchanged in seedling, flag leaf and panicle tissues across wild and cultivated lines (Fig. 2A ). These include miR390 which is cadmium-responsive 89 , miR395, sulfate-responsive miRNA 25 , miR156 responsive to phosphate and potassium 90 , miR393 associated with nitrogen 91 , and miR169,171 and 172 that are associated with Fe accumulation 92 . The miRNAs that showed differential expression between wild and cultivated rice varieties were miR397, miR398, miR408 and miR528 across several different tissues such as seedling, flag leaf and panicles (Supplementary Fig. 2A ). This set of miRNAs is known to establish Cu homoeostasis and is known to differentially express upon sensing Cu levels 33 , 35 . These miRNAs majorly targeted genes encoding Cu-binding proteins 33 , 34 .

### OsSPL9 is an SBP-domain containing TF upstream of domestication-associated miRNAs
miRNA-TF associated networks are well studied in plants 96 – 99 . TFs associated with basal metabolism and development remain conserved across land plants, but several nutrient-related TFs were co-opted with the downstream gene regulatory networks to elicit specific responses in changing environmental conditions 100 . In Arabidopsis , the Cu-responsive miRNAs (miR397, miR398, miR408, miR857) are regulated by the SBP-domain containing TF named AtSPL7 51 . To identify the rice homologue of AtSPL7 , we performed a phylogenetic analysis for the SPL family of genes in Arabidopsis and rice (Fig. 3A ), having 16 and 19 members, respectively 101 . This analysis indicated that OsSPL9 is homologous to AtSPL7 . Interestingly, OsSPL9 in rice was identified as a regulator of miR528 40 . In agreement with a possible role for OsSPL9 as an upstream regulator of Cu-associated miRNAs, Cu-miRNA precursor levels were reduced in specific tissues of Osspl9ko lines in japonica rice 55 .

### SPL9 binds to Cu-associated gene promoters enriched with the conserved GTAC motifs
Although the SPL family of TFs in Arabidopsis are known to bind to GTAC-containing motifs, the global profile of regions bound by OsSPL9 or its other monocot homologues is unknown. Although it was shown that OsSPL9 can bind to the miR528 promoter in ChIP-qPCR 40 , it is unknown if it can also bind to promoters of other Cu-associated miRNAs and Cu-associated genes. Since SPL9 regulates the expression of miRNAs, we profiled SPL9-bound regions in wild-type and under Cu-stressed conditions.
ChIP-seq was performed using seedling tissues using the Illumina platform, and we obtained more than 20 million reads mapping to IRGSP-1.0. We obtained more than 8000 peaks enriched with SPL9 binding. Among these peaks, most were located in intergenic regions, especially among promoters (62.11%), while distal intergenic regions (36.26%) peaks were also found in introns and exons (Fig. 4A, B ). Preferred binding to genic promoters by OsSPL9 is in agreement with similar observations in Arabidopsis 58 . The consensus motifs across the peaks were analysed, and these had multiple GTAC motifs as expected, usually in the distal intergenic regions. Cu-sufficient conditions also showed wider stretches of GTAC motifs in SPL9-bound genomic regions (Fig. 4D ). Interestingly, several Cu-associated genes, as well as several stress- associated genes, had SPL9 peaks in their promoter as well as in distal regions validated by ChIP sequencing and ChIP qPCR (Fig. 4E and Supplementary Fig. 7A–I ). SPL9 appears to be regulated by itself as it bound to GTAC motifs in its promoter (Supplementary Fig. 5A ). To further validate the binding of SPL9 to its consensus GTAC motif, we performed electrophoretic mobility shift assay (EMSA) using GTAC motif-containing probes of the key copper associated genes and targets of miRNAs. We observed clear binding to these regions by OsSPL9, and the mutated GATC probes showed weaker binding or no binding (Fig. 4F and Supplementary Fig. 6A–D ).

### OsSPL9 regulates the expression of Cu-associated miRNAs and protein-coding genes
To further understand the fundamental role of SPL9 TF in regulating gene expression under homoeostatic conditions, we generated RNA-seq datasets (Supplementary Table 1 ) from both spl9ko and SPL9 OE lines. In SPL9 OE, 698 genes were upregulated and 773 genes were downregulated (Fig. 5E ). The upregulated genes belong to categories involved in Cu-chaperone associated superoxide activity, nutrient reservoir activity, enzyme inhibitor activity etc. and some of these were downregulated in spl9ko as expected (Fig. 5F ). Since OsSPL9 has specific roles in Cu-deficiency responses and under copper deprivation the SPL7 undergoes post-transcriptional regulation with changes in its target activity 55 , 102 , we analysed the transcriptomic responses upon low Cu and excess Cu conditions in cultivated rice. The DEGs obtained in both categories were correlated with the transcriptomic responses of spl9ko . Interestingly, we observed that spl9ko mimics an excess Cu transcriptome (Fig. 5G ).
The spl9ko lines showed 1217 upregulated and 1376 downregulated genes; the latter set of DEGs included copper transporter, COPT3 and several metallothionein proteins (Fig. 5H ). This is in agreement with observations in Arabidopsis SPL7 where the protein activated the expression of Cu-transporters, such as COPT1 and COPT2 51 . Increased Cu-miRNA target expression in spl9ko lines was also observed. The miRNA targets such as CuZnSOD2 , CuZnSOD3 , SOD4 , Uclacyanin 8 , Uclacyanin 9 , Uclacyanin 15, Uclacyanin 23 , Uclacyanin 30 and Laccase 14 were upregulated several-fold in spl9ko (Fig. 5H ). Among these, most of them showed reduced expression in O. nivara , while their levels were upregulated in spl9ko , when compared to PB-1 (Fig. 5I and Supplementary Data 4 ). The major family of DEGs in cultivated rice, compared to wild rice, also had Uclacyanins. This set of genes was not observed in Arabidopsis upon spl7ko as indicated 51 . ### Cu-associated miRNAs form a feedback loop
Several studies have shown that the stability of the miRNAs is regulated by their targets, association with Argonaute proteins or their modifications 103 , indicating the presence of a feedback loop. A number of targets associated with Cu-binding and/or associated genes are regulated by the miRNAs downstream of SPL9. However, the role of individual miRNAs or their targets in regulating the Cu-responses remained unclear. It is also intriguing that Cu, an element, the accumulation of which might vary between two nearby sites, was central to these regulatory modules. We hypothesised that regulatory roles played by individual Cu-miRNA, its target(s) and Cu-availability/accumulation might be strongly linked in such a way that small perturbations do not lead to a drastic growth penalty. In order to understand the crosstalk by SPL9-mediated regulation of miRNAs and its targets, we generated individual knockouts of these domestication-associated miRNAs in cultivated rice background (Supplementary Figs. 10 and 11C ). The knockout of miR397 resulted in a 4 nt deletion in the stem loop of the precursor miRNA, perturbing the formation of mature miR397 (Supplementary Fig. 10A ). miR528ko mutant harbours a single nucleotide insertion (Supplementary Fig. 10B ) and the knockout of miR408 using CRISPR led to 8 nt deletion in the stem-loop region of the miRNA (Supplementary Fig. 10C ). The mature miR408 with this mutation will be only 18 nt long when compared to 21 nt in WT. All these knockout plants showed severe growth phenotypes when compared to the WT even in the seedling stage, indicating the role of these miRNAs and their targets in early development (Fig. 6A–D ).

### Perturbation of Cu levels in wild and cultivated lines led to yield-compromised phenotypes
In order to understand the role of Cu in mediating SPL9 and domestication-associated miRNAs, we subjected wild and cultivated rice lines to different concentrations of Cu (Fig. 7A ). Levels of miRNAs across wild and cultivated lines in Cu-deficient, sufficient and excess conditions were assayed using northern analysis. Under Cu-deficient conditions, the levels of domestication-associated miRNAs remained unchanged in O. nivara , while their expression was induced in PB-1. However, upon Cu-excess conditions, miRNA levels were reduced in both O. nivara and PB-1 (Fig. 7B ). Similarly, SPL9 OE lines mimicked O. nivara in that the levels of Cu-miRNAs were unaffected in Cu-deficient conditions, whereas Cu-excess led to a reduction in miRNAs. However, partial retention of miRNA levels of miR528 and miR397 were observed in SPL9OE lines only upon high Cu, indicating that the levels of SPL9 protein dictate the miRNA levels. It is possible that miRNA targets, including those required for Cu-binding proteins, are already downregulated in O. nivara due to higher accumulation of miRNAs.

### Discussion
Cereal crops grown in agricultural ecosystems are genetically distinct from wild relatives and are subjected to varied water and fertiliser supplies. Ability to acquire and utilise the minimal requirements for plant growth, must have contributed to the process of domestication in these crops, to enable them to have high photosynthetic rate, broader leaves, and increased growth rate 110 – 112 . Increased growth might have demanded more nitrogen and phosphorus uptake and utilisation 113 , when compared to other elements such as Cu and micronutrients that are part of defense proteins and promoters of secondary metabolites 114 . Micronutrients such as cobalt is also essential for plants in the form of metalloproteins such as carboxypeptidases and ureases 115 . Uptake and accumulation of several of these elements, including metal ions, were altered in many agronomic crops during domestication 116 . Studies have suggested that genetic as well as epigenetic factors alter the histone marks at the level of transport, metabolism as well as signalling for macronutrients such as nitrogen and phosphorus 117 . Cu, being a micronutrient, an essential cofactor for a wide range of cellular enzymes and proteins, has importance in several key processes. Cu-proteins are functionally redundant to existing iron enzymes and have evolved much later upon oxygenation, indicating their possible redundant but equally important roles in the metalloproteomes 118 . It is therefore not surprising that Cu build-up and its utilisation and incorporation appear to be a major determinant for traits somewhat similar to the well-established macronutrient nitrogen 119 – 125 . Even though Zn, Fe and Mn-associated metalloproteins exist, there is no clear association of them being key regulators of domestication-associated traits 126 . ### Binary vector construction and Agrobacterium mobilisation
For the generation of overexpression lines of SPL9, the CDS of the gene Chr 5: 19923167-19932333 (Os05g0408200) was amplified and cloned with an N-terminal 3xFLAG tag into a derivative of pCAMBIA1300 driven by a maize Ubiquitin1 promoter. The spl9ko mutant was generated using CRISPR-Cas9 mutagenesis, and the gRNA was designed using CRISPR-PLANT 167 . A similar strategy was followed for the miRNA knockouts as well. The gRNA sequences and the reverse primers for all constructs are mentioned in the Supplementary Table 5 . The amplicon harbouring the gRNA sequence was cloned and inserted into plasmid pRGEB32 168 . Screening of mutants was performed by PCR and sequencing to identify the indel (insertion/deletion) in the targeted region. The gRNAs were designed using CRISPR-PLANT tool and Class 1.0 gRNAs with no off-targeting ability under the stringency criteria were chosen for the study (Supplementary Fig. 15A ). These gRNAs were further subjected to RAPDB BLAST and only single hit was found across the genome for the respective miRNA (Supplementary Fig. 15B ). Additionally, the miRNA ko lines were further sequenced for indel in other miRNA regions using primers flanking the respective genomic loci and no indel was found for any miRNA other than for the gRNA: target miRNA (Supplementary Fig. 15C ).

### Chromatin immunoprecipitation-sequencing (ChIP-seq) and analysis
ChIP was performed as described earlier 180 – 182 . About 1–1.5 g of the 21-day old seedling tissue grown in respective copper conditions were collected and cross-linked with 1% formaldehyde with intermittent mixing and quenched with 0.125 M glycine in 1× PBS. The tissues were finely ground in liquid nitrogen, and the nuclei isolation was performed. The nuclei were lysed and sheared using the Covaris ultrasonicator till the fragments were of 150–350 bp in size. The chromatin post shearing was incubated overnight with Anti-FLAG M2 Magnetic Beads-40 μl (Sigma-M8823) at 4 °C. The purified IP products were taken for library preparation using the NEBNext Ultra II DNA library prep kit with sample purification beads (NEB E7103L) as per the manufacturer’s protocol. The libraries (with replicates) were sequenced on an Illumina Novaseq 6000 platform (Supplementary Table 3 ).
The datasets from the sequencing were used for cutadapt-based adaptor trimming. The alignment was performed using Bowtie2 183 , and the alignment parameters were as follows: -v 1 -k 1 -y -a --best –strata. The PCR duplicates were removed, and the alignment files were converted to coverage files and normalised with input samples, using deeptools 184 , 185 . The ComputeMatrix tool was used to compute the average signals over the annotated regions, and plotProfile was used to generate the metaplots. The peak calling was performed using MACS2 with the narrow peak calling option. The consistency across replicates was validated using BEDTools intersect. Intervene was used to obtain the intersections with the RNA-seq data as performed earlier 186 .

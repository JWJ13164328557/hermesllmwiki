# The degree of facilitation of the spider mite Tetranychus evansi by tomato spotted wilt virus depends on the order of infection.

## 论文信息
- **期刊**: Philosophical transactions of the Royal Society of London. Series B, Biological sciences (2026)
- **PMID**: [43094425](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/43094425/)
- **DOI**: [10.1098/rstb.2025.0286](https://doi.org/10.1098/rstb.2025.0286)
- **第一作者**: Vandana Gupta
- **作者**: Vandana Gupta, Marion Szadkowski, David Carbonell, Yannis Michalakis, Benoît Moury, Ann T Tate, Alison B Duncan

## 摘要
Priority effects can change the outcome of host-mediated interactions among co-infecting parasites. In a plant-virus-mite system, prior infection with tomato spotted wilt virus (TSWV) increases the number of spider mite (Tetranychus evansi) offspring. This is likely owing to negative immune crosstalk suppressing activation of the jasmonic acid pathway or virus-mediated increase in free amino acid levels in host plants. In this system, we coupled infection order experiments with a mathematical model of virus, mite and plant hormone dynamics to evaluate whether immune- or resource-mediated interactions among parasites might best explain observed priority effects. The model predicted facilitation of mites by the virus in all treatment modalities (i.e. prior inoculation with TSWV, prior exposure to mites and synchronous exposure) via immune-mediated and resource-mediated mechanisms, with the strongest facilitation when TSWV inoculation is first. Our experimental results were broadly consistent with model predictions, exhibiting the greatest facilitation of mites when the virus arrives first. Contrary to model predictions, however, facilitation under simultaneous exposure was only observed when correcting for plant size. Host traits may thus need to be incorporated into appropriate metrics to capture facilitation. This shows that integration of models with experiments can be useful for improving understanding of disease outcomes. This article is part of the theme issue 'Ecological and evolutionary interactions between parasites across scales'.

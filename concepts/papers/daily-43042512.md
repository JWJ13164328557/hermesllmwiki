# Adaptation of an herbivorous arthropod to green tea plants by overcoming catechin defenses.

## 论文信息

- **PMID**: [43042512](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/43042512/)
- **DOI**: [10.7554/eLife.110082](https://doi.org/10.7554/eLife.110082)
- **第一作者**: Naoki Takeda
- **作者**: Naoki Takeda, Brendan Abiskaroon, Ricardo Hernandez Arriaza, Ryutaro Murakami, Shogo Sasaki, Masanobu Yamamoto, Vladimir Zhurov, Vojislava Grbic

## 摘要
Green tea catechins protect tea plants from biotic stressors. Among herbivorous mites, the generalist Tetranychus urticae has not been reported as a tea pest. In contrast, some populations of Tetranychus kanzawai thrive on tea plants. Here, we investigated the mechanism of this adaptation. Green tea catechins were repellent and toxic to T. urticae and tea non-adapted T. kanzawai and had an antifeedant effect on the latter. We identified an intradiol ring-cleavage dioxygenase DOG15, a gene horizontally transferred from fungi, as required for the adaptation of T. kanzawai to tea plants. Tea feeding strongly induces DOG15 expression in tea-adapted T. kanzawai, with mRNA and protein levels up to 31.6 and 12.1 times higher than in tea-fed T. urticae. We also identified two amino acid substitutions in DOG15 between the two Tetranychus species that increased the catechin-cleaving activity of the T. kanzawai enzyme. These substitutions predispose T. kanzawai but not T. urticae for the adaptation to tea plants. Inducible expression of modified DOG15 then enables T. kanzawai to efficiently detoxify green tea catechins. Our findings reveal how a horizontally transferred gene can be co-opted through structural and regulatory changes to overcome plant chemical defenses.

Green tea is well known for its potential health benefits due to the antioxidative compounds it contains. For the plant, however, these molecules, also known as catechins, serve as a chemical defense against insects and other small animals that feed on its leaves. Spider mites, tiny relatives of spiders, feed on a wide range of crops. The two-spotted spider mite, for example, attacks more than a thousand plant species but seems to avoid tea plants. However, a close relative, the Kanzawa spider mite, can thrive on tea and is a major pest in tea plantations. Scientists think spider mite ancestors might have acquired genes from fungi in the distant past, which encode enzymes capable of breaking down ring-shaped molecules, including catechins. So far, it was unclear whether these enzymes explain why some spider mites can feed on tea plants while others cannot. Takeda et al. set out to understand why some Kanzawa spider mites can thrive on tea plants, while other mite species cannot. The researchers compared tea-adapted and non-adapted Kanzawa spider mites with two-spotted spider mites using feeding, survival and behavioural assays to investigate whether tea-adapted mites overcome the harmful effects of catechins by changing their behaviour, breaking down the compounds, or both. They also examined which genes and proteins were activated when the mites fed on tea leaves. The results showed that catechins repelled and poisoned non-adapted mites, whereas tea-adapted mites neither avoided catechins nor succumbed to the same doses. Instead, tea-adapted mites strongly increased production of a fungus-derived enzyme, DOG15, when feeding on tea. Reducing expression of the gene encoding DOG15 decreased mite survival on tea leaves. The enzyme also differs at two positions in the genetic code between the two mite species. Moreover, the tea-adapted spider mite version breaks down catechins more efficiently. These findings could offer tea growers a new avenue for pest control. Understanding these mechanisms could reveal how pests overcome plant chemical defenses and help explain how they adapt to new host plants. Identifying DOG15 as an important factor in mite survival on tea points to a potential target for developing more selective control strategies. More broadly, these results demonstrate how a gene acquired from fungi can be modified and upregulated to help an animal overcome a plant's chemical defenses, shedding light on how pests adapt to and colonise new crops. However, further development and field testing of DOG15-based control strategies are needed before practical application can be possible.

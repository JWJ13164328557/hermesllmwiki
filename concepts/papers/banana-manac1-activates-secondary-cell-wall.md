# Banana MaNAC1 activates secondary cell wall cellulose biosynthesis to enhance chilling resistance in fruit

## 摘要
SummaryChilling injury has a negative impact on the quantity and quality of crops, especially subtropical and tropical plants. The plant cell wall is not only the main source of biomass production, but also the first barrier to various stresses. Therefore, improving the understanding of the alterations in cell wall architecture is of great significance for both biomass production and stress adaptation. Herein, we demonstrated that the cell wall principal component cellulose accumulated during chilling stress, which was caused by the activation of MaCESA proteins. The sequence‐multiple comparisons show that a cold‐inducible NAC transcriptional factor MaNAC1, a homologue of Secondary Wall NAC transcription factors, has high sequence similarity with Arabidopsis SND3. An increase in cell wall thickness and cellulosic glucan content was observed in MaNAC1‐overexpressing Arabidopsis lines, indicating that MaNAC1 participates in cellulose biosynthesis. Over‐expression of MaNAC1 in Arabidopsis mutant snd3 restored the defective secondary growth of thinner cell walls and increased cellulosic glucan content. Furthermore, the activation of MaCESA7 and MaCESA6B cellulose biosynthesis genes can be directly induced by MaNAC1 through binding to SNBE motifs within their promoters, leading to enhanced cellulose content during low‐temperature stress. Ultimately, tomato fruit showed greater cold resistance in MaNAC1 overexpression lines with thickened cell walls and increased cellulosic glucan content. Our findings revealed that MaNAC1 performs a vital role as a positive modulator in modulating cell wall cellulose metabolism within banana fruit under chilling stress.

### Introduction
The changing global climate is increasingly threatening food security. Banana, a tropical and subtropical fruit, is the fourth tonnage agricultural product in the world. As a typical cold‐sensitive horticultural product, storage temperatures less than 13 °C cause physiological disorders in banana fruit (Putra et al ., 2010 ), which is termed chilling injury (CI). Apparently, CI causes discoloration and obvious deep brown spots on the peel skin (Promyou et al ., 2008 ), leading to a great loss of security and market value of banana fruit. Although an enormous range of physiological responses has evolved to cope with less favourable conditions with the constant domestication, unpredictable weather momently threatens plants' survival. A sudden change to low temperatures for tropical originating plants, such as bananas, can result in severe irreversible physiological damage. Studies about CI in plants have focused mostly on membrane damage, reactive oxygen species (ROS) production and scavenging systems, proline accumulation, marked changes in gene expression, and biochemical pathways affecting photosynthesis (Asghari and Aghdam, 2010 ; Baslam et al ., 2020 ; Gill and Tuteja, 2010 ; Liang et al ., 2020 ; Theocharis et al ., 2012 ). However, less attention has been paid to the link between low‐temperature stress with plant cell wall components and architecture.
Transcriptional regulation is a critical biological process. The NAC transcription factor (TF) is a prominent family of plant‐specific TFs that perform a critical function in plants' growth, development and reaction to ecological stress. NACs from Arabidopsis and rice are broadly categorized into six important branches, including MAM/CUC3, secondary wall NAC (SWN), TIP, SNAC (Stress‐Responsive NAC), ANAC034 and OsNAC4 (Nakashima et al ., 2012 ). ### Thickened cell wall and accumulated cellulose in banana peels under chilling stress
The appearance of postharvest bananas under CI stress has been investigated. To identify the main changes in the postharvest banana peel under CI, we paid more attention to the cell wall of the plant, which serves as the main defence mechanism in plants when subjected to stress. We observed some browning spots under chilling injury (7 °C) (Figure 1a ), which is consistent with previous research (Zhou et al ., 2022 ). In comparison with fruit stored at 22 °C (room temperature), we found that the texture was firmer with the extension of storage at 7 °C (Figure 1b ), the peel tissue showed a significantly thicker cell wall under CI (Figures 1c,d ). Furthermore, the cellulosic glucan content in the peels of chilling bananas significantly increased compared to that at room temperature (Figure 1e ).

### Discussion
Bananas are distributed in more than 120 tropical and subtropical countries and are recognized for their high carbohydrates content, as well as their rich abundance of dietary fibres, specific vitamins and minerals (Pareek, 2016 ). Although banana production ranks fourth globally after rice, wheat and maize, only 65% of harvested banana fruits are produced for commercial purposes due to biotic and abiotic stresses (Le et al ., 2019 ). It is vital to explore what triggers cells to perceive signals, execute gene function and then accumulate metabolites to cope with CI. In this study, we found a novel mechanism for thickening the cell wall of banana peel under chilling stress. The firmness of the peel, the thickness of the cell wall and the content of cellulosic glucan responded closely to low temperatures (7 °C). Enhanced expression of most MaCESA genes in banana peel under CI is the key to the accumulation of cellulosic glucan content, and we obtained MaNAC1 from banana fruit, a homologous SWN, which activated the transcription of MaCESA genes under CI. Furthermore, overexpression of MaNAC1 in Arabidopsis led to a thickened cell wall and higher cellulose content of the stems and recovered the defective cell wall and secondary growth of the snd3 mutant. Importantly, MaNAC1 overexpression can improve the cold tolerance of tomato fruits due to thickened cell walls and accumulated cellulose. According to our research results, a working model of the function of MaNAC1 in banana CI was proposed (Figure 7 ). At standard temperature, MaNAC1 can bind to SNBE sites in the MaCESA6B and MaCESA7 promoters to control their transcription activities. Stable transcription and action of MaCESA6B and MaCESA7 maintain normal cell wall architecture. Under chilling stress, CI activates MaNAC1 , which in turn promotes the transcription activities of MaCESA6B and MaCESA7 , eventually leading to the promotion of cellulose biosynthesis and thickened cell walls (Figure 7 ). A working model of MaNAC1 in regulating cellulose of banana fruit under low‐temperature stress. Under CI (7 °C), MaICE1 is induced, which in turn activates MaNAC1 (Shan et al ., 2014 ), thereby up‐regulating the MaNAC1 expression. NAC TFs regulate most physiological processes in plants, such as developmental processes, stress perception and hormone signalling (Jensen et al ., 2010 ). Previously, NAC proteins have been reported to be involved in low‐temperature stress. For example, the binding of MdNAC029 to the ACACGT motifs of MdCBF1 / MdCBF4 promoters has been observed in apples, inhibiting their expression and hence affecting the cold tolerance (An et al ., 2018 ). Tomato SlNAC1 overexpression in tomato seedlings increased the maximum photochemical efficacy of photosystem II and the oxygen‐evolving activities under chilling injury (Ma et al ., 2013 ). Pyrus betulifolia PbeNAC1 induces greater cold tolerance through the interaction between PbeDREB1 and PbeDREB2A to activate expression of stress‐responsive genes. Furthermore, overexpression of PbeNAC1 in tobacco confers lower levels of ROS, a higher survival rate and higher enzyme activities (Jin et al ., 2017 ). Reports involving fruits under cold stress focus on oxidative metabolism, but it is not yet clear how changes in cell wall cellulose and morphology have been observed. In Musa acuminata , MaNAC1, a direct target gene of MaICE1, exhibits interaction with MaCBF1 and the response to tolerance to cold in banana fruits (Shan et al ., 2014 ).

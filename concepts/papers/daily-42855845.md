# Identification of germplasm and genomic markers associated with reduced T-2 and HT-2 mycotoxin accumulation in oat.

## 摘要
Harmful T-2 and HT-2 mycotoxins are produced by Fusarium langsethiae and Fusarium sporotrichioides in developing oat (Avena sativa L.) grain. This study identified oat genotypes with consistently low toxin accumulation across environments. Toxin enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay and fungal DNA (quantitative polymerase chain reaction) levels were quantified in 19 oat genotypes grown at two Irish sites under natural infection. F. langsethiae and T-2 + HT-2 were detected at both sites, while F. sporotrichioides was absent. Genotype effects were significant for both F. langsethiae DNA and toxin levels, and the traits were positively correlated (Spearman's ρ = 0.62, p < 0.001). Eleven genotypes were also evaluated in an F. langsethiae-inoculated glasshouse experiment within a larger panel of 190 genotypes. Across field and glasshouse environments, toxin levels were consistently low in Tempo, Sandy, and Jokikyla ME0501 and high in Stormont Arrow. Glasshouse T-2 + HT-2 levels ranged from below detection to 1000 µg kg- 1. genome-wide association study using 4881 single-nucleotide polymorphisms identified five significant markers on Mrg11, mapping to two loci on chromosomes 6A and 4C associated with reduced toxin accumulation. The 6A locus was near HIPP39 (where HIPP is heavy metal-associated isoprenylated plant protein), whereas no coding genes occurred within ± 30 kb of the 4C locus. These associations were detected in only one of the two glasshouse runs and differed from loci previously identified under field conditions, highlighting the need for a better understanding of the elusive and variable nature of Fusarium-oat interactions. These results highlight genotypes and candidate loci to support breeding for reduced T-2 and HT-2 risk in oats.

Mycotoxins produced by certain fungi can contaminate oat grain and pose risks to food and feed safety. The fungus Fusarium langsethiae commonly infects oats without causing visible disease symptoms, yet it can lead to the accumulation of the harmful mycotoxins T‐2 and HT‐2. In this study, we examined how different oat varieties respond to this fungus under natural field conditions and under controlled glasshouse inoculation. We identified several oat varieties that consistently accumulated very low toxin levels across both environments. Using toxin data from a larger panel of oats grown under controlled conditions, we linked reduction in toxin accumulation to specific regions of the oat genome. These findings show that genetic differences among oat varieties play a key role in determining toxin risk and identify genomic regions that can be used by plant breeders to develop oat varieties with reduced mycotoxin contamination.

### 核心发现
1. Overall, we found no significant correlation in the toxin levels detected for the 11 varieties commonly tested in both the field and the glasshouse; nor was there any correlation between the results previously obtained for the 190 genotypes in the field and our glasshouse results (results not shown).
2. Several cereal HIPP genes exhibited induction during pathogen attack, and constituents of this family have emerged as critical host targets for fungal effectors.
3. For example, the Magnaporthe oryzae effector Pwl2 has been shown to interact with barley HIPP43, leading to its displacement from plasmodesmata and subsequent inhibition of PAMP (pathogen‐associated molecular patterns)‐triggered immunity (PTI) and increased disease susceptibility (Were et al., ).
4. Taken together, these findings indicate that HIPP proteins are evolutionarily conserved host factors implicated in immune regulation and are frequently targeted by pathogens.
5. langsethiae remains poorly understood, and loci such as HIPP39 that are associated with toxin accumulation and previously found to encode effector targets provide a potential entry point for examining whether host–pathogen co‐evolution has shaped toxin‐related traits in oats.
6. To harness this variation for breeding, it is essential to identify the underlying genetic basis of the resistance mechanisms.
7. More importantly, our findings highlight a major biological knowledge gap.

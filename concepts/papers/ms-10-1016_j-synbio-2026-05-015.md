### Abstract
Cytochrome P450 enzymes are promising for synthesizing therapeutically beneficial bile acids murideoxycholic acid (MDCA) and ursodeoxycholic acid (UDCA) from lithocholic acid (LCA). However, the catalytic efficiency of P450 enzymes remains inadequate for large-scale production. Here, we address this limitation by engineering P450 OleP, achieving high activity for the efficient synthesis of MDCA and UDCA. Firstly, an improved high-throughput detection method for UDCA was developed to accelerate the screening process for mutants with improved UDCA synthesis capabilities. Next, the mutant S240A was generated through semi-rational design guided by the crystal structure of the OleP-LCA complex, resulting in an improvement in the MDCA conversion rate from 32.5% to 98.3% using 0.5 g L −1 of LCA as the substrate. Furthermore, the crystal structure of the S240A mutant was resolved and analyzed. Subsequently, the mutant F84Q/S240A/V291G/E89 N was developed through rational design, improving the UDCA conversion rate from 6.5% to 12.1% with 0.5 g L −1 of LCA as the substrate. Finally, molecular dynamics simulations were employed to elucidate the mechanisms underlying the enhanced catalytic activity of the S240A and F84Q/S240A/V291G/E89 N mutants. This study provides optimized P450 enzyme mutants for the synthesis of MDCA and UDCA and offers significant insights into the catalytic mechanisms underlying the production of hydroxylated, LCA-derived high-value-added compounds.

Bile acids are a class of steroid compounds characterized by multiple hydroxyl groups. Similarly, MDCA synthesis from hyodeoxycholic acid requires harsh conditions and hazardous reagents (e.g., hypochlorous acid), posing potential environmental risks [ 11 ]. ### 4. Conclusion
In this study, P450 OleP was systematically evaluated for its capacity to hydroxylate LCA into high-value bile acid derivatives, MDCA and UDCA. An improved high-throughput detection method was established to efficiently facilitate the screening of mutants with enhanced UDCA synthesis activity, overcoming the limitations of traditional screening approaches. ### Abstract
Cytochrome P450 enzymes are promising for synthesizing therapeutically beneficial bile acids murideoxycholic acid (MDCA) and ursodeoxycholic acid (UDCA) from lithocholic acid (LCA). However, the catalytic efficiency of P450 enzymes remains inadequate for large-scale production. Here, we address this limitation by engineering P450 OleP, achieving high activity for the efficient synthesis of MDCA and UDCA. Firstly, an improved high-throughput detection method for UDCA was developed to accelerate the screening process for mutants with improved UDCA synthesis capabilities. Next, the mutant S240A was generated through semi-rational design guided by the crystal structure of the OleP-LCA complex, resulting in an improvement in the MDCA conversion rate from 32.5% to 98.3% using 0.5 g L −1 of LCA as the substrate. Furthermore, the crystal structure of the S240A mutant was resolved and analyzed. Subsequently, the mutant F84Q/S240A/V291G/E89 N was developed through rational design, improving the UDCA conversion rate from 6.5% to 12.1% with 0.5 g L −1 of LCA as the substrate. Finally, molecular dynamics simulations were employed to elucidate the mechanisms underlying the enhanced catalytic activity of the S240A and F84Q/S240A/V291G/E89 N mutants. This study provides optimized P450 enzyme mutants for the synthesis of MDCA and UDCA and offers significant insights into the catalytic mechanisms underlying the production of hydroxylated, LCA-derived high-value-added compounds.

### 1. Introduction
Bile acids are a class of steroid compounds characterized by multiple hydroxyl groups. Derived from cholesterol metabolism in the liver, they play integral roles in the digestion and absorption of lipids and fat-soluble vitamins [ 1 ]. Furthermore, they act as pivotal signaling molecules regulating lipid, glucose, steroid, and energy metabolism throughout the body [ 2 ]. They also possess notable pharmacological properties and are employed in the treatment of hepatobiliary disorders and as anti-inflammatory agents [ 3 , 4 ]. Among them, MDCA is crucial for maintaining bile acid homeostasis and preventing gallstone formation [ 5 , 6 ]. UDCA, another important bile acid and a natural active component found in bear bile, is approved for therapeutic use in various liver and gallbladder diseases [ 7 ].
Currently, production methods for MDCA and UDCA face significant limitations. The conventional approach of extracting these compounds from animal livers is constrained by ethical considerations [ 8 ]. Chemoenzymatic synthesis, while popular, often involves multi-step reactions with low yields (e.g., 30% for UDCA from cholic acid) [ 9 ] and suffers from high energy consumption and waste generation [ 10 ]. Similarly, MDCA synthesis from hyodeoxycholic acid requires harsh conditions and hazardous reagents (e.g., hypochlorous acid), posing potential environmental risks [ 11 ]. With advancements in biotechnology and the development of green biomanufacturing, enzyme-based biosynthesis technologies for MDCA and UDCA have emerged, offering advantages in terms of environmental sustainability, efficiency, and safety. Currently, UDCA is predominantly produced via a two-step enzymatic process using 7α-/7β-hydroxysteroid dehydrogenases with chenodeoxycholic acid as the substrate [ 12 , 13 ]. Notably, chenodeoxycholic acid is utilized in medical treatment due to its strong ability to dissolve cholesterol gallstones [ 14 ].

### 2.3. Production conditions for the P450 candidates and purification for OleP
The successfully engineered recombinant E. coli strains, each harboring a candidate P450 enzyme and redox partners, were initially activated by culturing on LB agar plates supplemented with 50 μg mL −1 of kanamycin at 37 °C for 12 h. A single colony was picked and inoculated into 2 mL of LB broth containing 50 μg mL −1 of kanamycin, followed by incubation at 37 °C with shaking at 220 rpm for 12 h to prepare the seed culture. Subsequently, 1 mL of the seed culture was transferred into 250 mL shaking flasks containing 50 mL of TB medium supplemented with 50 μg mL −1 of kanamycin, and the cultures were incubated at 37 °C with shaking at 220 rpm. When the optical density at 600 nm (OD 600 ) reached 0.6–0.8 (usually after 3–5 h of incubation), the culture was cooled to 16 °C and supplemented with 0.4 mM ALA and 0.2 mM FeCl 3 . Production of P450s proteins was induced by adding 0.2 mM IPTG, and the culture was further incubated for another 36 h. After cultivation, the cells were harvested by centrifugation at 18,400g for 10 min, washed twice with 100 mM potassium phosphate buffer (pH 7.4), and then prepared for subsequent purification and whole-cell catalysis experiments.

### 2.6. High-throughput screening method for UDCA and MDCA
The gene encoding 7β-HSDH ( A4ECA9 .1) from Collinsella aerofaciens was codon-optimized and synthesized by GenScript (Nanjing, China). The 7β-HSDH gene was subsequently subcloned into the C-terminal region of the OleP gene in plasmid pRSF-OleP-CamAB, with the incorporation of a ribosome binding site (RBS; GGATCCGAATTCGAGCTCATAAAAGGAGGAAAATAT), yielding the recombinant plasmid pRSF-OleP-CamAB-HSDH. After the whole-cell reaction, 50 μL of the reaction product was transferred to a 96-well microplate. Subsequently, 100 μL of a 2,4-dinitrophenylhydrazine solution (10 mM, dissolved in a 1:1 v/v mixture of dimethyl sulfoxide and ethanol) was added to the each well. The microplate was placed on a shaker and incubated at 25 °C with shaking at 800 rpm for 30 min. Following this, 100 μL of NaOH solution (4 M, dissolved in a 40% v/v ethanol aqueous solution) was added to the wells. Following the 30 min standing period at room temperature, the mixture was centrifuged at 18,400 g for 5 min to collect the supernatant. A 100 μL aliquot of the supernatant was transferred to a new 96-well microplate, and the absorbance at 440 nm was measured. Samples with high absorbance values were selected for further HPLC analysis.

### 2.7. Molecular docking and molecular dynamics (MD) simulations
The substrate LCA was docked into the active site of the OleP S240A crystal structure using Auto-Dock Vina in Chimera [ 23 ]. Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations of the mutant-substrate complex were performed to analyze the dynamic structural characteristics of the protein complexes. The protonation states of titratable residues (His, Glu, and Asp) were determined based on p K a values using PROPKA3.1 and through visual inspection of local H-bonding networks [ 24 , 25 ]. Histidine residues His 30, His 138, His 246, His 333, and His 354 were protonated at the δ position, while His 23, His 101, His 228, His 258, His 320, His 340, His 346, His 351, and His 355 were protonated at the ε position. Except for amino acids E89 and D91, which remain protonated, all other acidic residues are deprotonated. The substrate LCA was parameterized using the general AMBER Force Field (GAFF) [ 26 ], with atomic charge parameters fitted via the restrained electrostatic potential (RESP) method [ 27 ]. The RESP charges were calculated using Multiwfn software [ 28 , 29 ]. The force field parameters for Cpd I were sourced from a previous study [ 30 ]. The Amber ff14SB force field was employed for the protein residues [ 31 ]. The complex system was solvated in a 16 Å TIP3P water box, and Na + was added to the protein surface to neutralize the total charge of the system.

### 3.1. Screening of efficient P450 enzymes for converting LCA to MDCA and UDCA
According to previous reports, the three-component P450 OleP can catalyze the production of 6β-OH deoxycholic acid from deoxycholic acid, as well as the synthesis of MDCA from LCA. However, when whole-cell catalysts were prepared using E. coli C43(DE3) strain harboring the pET28a-oleP and pACYCDuet-camA-camB plasmids, the conversion rate of LCA to MDCA was merely 1.6% [ 33 ]. To achieve efficient conversion of LCA to MDCA and UDCA, several P450s were selected to investigate their hydroxylation activity towards LCA, including the self-sufficient P450 BM3, two-component P450 enzymes (CYP5150AP3, P450 cur , and P450 FE ), and other three-component P450 enzymes (CYP105A1, CYP105A3, CYP105AB3, CYP105D7, and CYP105P2).
An efficient redox partner Sm CPR I453V from Silybum marianum was employed to construct a whole-cell biocatalysis system for two-component P450s, while putidaredoxin reductase (CamA) and putidaredoxin (CamB) from Pseudomonas putida were used to establish the catalytic system for three-component P450s. The genes encoding theses redox partners were co-expressed with the corresponding P450 genes in the BL21(DE3) using a high-copy number plasmid pRSFDuet-1, resulting in ten recombinant E. coli strains harboring different P450s ( Fig. 1 A) . Subsequently, the hydroxylation activity toward LCA was evaluated using these whole-cell biocatalysts.

### 3.2. Improving a high-throughput screening method for detecting UDCA
Despite advances in P450-mediated steroid hydroxylation via protein engineering [ 9 , 17 ], low steroid detection efficiency limits the screening of a large P450 mutation library. We previously established a high-throughput MDCA detection method using 75% H 2 SO 4 –EtOH, which outperforms the traditional NAD(P)H consumption assay in accuracy ( Fig. 2 A) [ 11 ]. For UDCA, a colorimetric assay based on the water-soluble tetrazolium salt, 2-(4-iodophenyl)-3-(4-nitrophenyl)-5-(2,4-disulfophenyl)-2H-tetrazolium (WST-1)/1-methoxy-5-methylphenazinium methyl sulfate (mPMS) system has been established, which couples NADPH production (from 7β-hydroxysteroid dehydrogenase catalyzed UDCA oxidation) [ 9 ]. Here, we improved the high-throughput detection method for UDCA by coupling two reactions ( Fig. 2 A): oxidation of UDCA to 7-keto-lithocholic acid (7-KLCA) via a NADP + -dependent 7β-hydroxysteroid dehydrogenase from Collinsella aerofaciens [ 19 ], followed by 2,4-dinitrophenylhydrazine (DNPH)-based ketone detection for 7-KLCA [ 36 ].

### 3.3. Structural characteristics of the OleP-LCA complex
To provide structural information for the subsequent rational design of P450 OleP, the crystal structure of OleP was resolved. The purified OleP enzyme was obtained using a Hi-trap chelating HP column, gel filtration chromatography, and ion-exchange chromatography with a Mono Q column (GE Healthcare) ( Fig. S2A–S2D ). The purified OleP enzyme was crystallized by sitting-drop vapor-diffusion, producing OleP (PDB: 21LA ) and OleP-LCA (PDB: 21GU ) complex structures at 1.98 Å and 2.79 Å resolution ( Fig. 3 A, Fig. S2E, Fig. S3 and Table S4 ). The asymmetric unit contained two molecules, each adopting the canonical P450 fold with 14 α-helices, 15 β-strands ( Fig. S4 ), and a central heme group coordinated by a thiolate ligand. Within each asymmetric unit, two OleP molecules (designated as chains A and B) adopt nearly identical conformations (RMSD = 0.31 Å), exhibiting high similarity to the previously reported crystal structure of OleP (PDB: 4XE3 ) with an RMSD of 1.1 Å ( Fig. S5A ) [ 37 ]. Structural alignment using the Dali Server confirmed that OleP shares high similarity with P450OleP (PDB: 4XE3 , 8QYI , Z-score 61.4, 52.1, Fig. S5 ), including conserved catalytic motifs in the I- and L-helices. The iron in the heme group in the active site is axially coordinated with the conserved cysteine residue at position 356. The two propionate groups of the heme molecule form electrostatic interactions with two highly conserved histidine residues (at positions 101 and 354), two arginine residues (at positions 105 and 298), a leucine residue (at position 94), and a phenylalanine residue (at position 321).

### 3.4. Semi-rational design of OleP to enhance the synthesis of MDCA and UDCA
To identify key residues affecting OleP-catalyzed LCA hydroxylation, a comprehensive mutant library with 60 amino acid sites in the substrate cavity was generated. This included ten heme-interacting residues, twenty-one residues within 5 Å of the heme, nine residues contacting the substrate LCA, and twenty residues within 5 Å of LCA ( Fig. 4 A–B, and S8A-B). Alanine residues at positions 95, 244, 293, 348, 353, and 359 were mutated to glycine, while the remaining 54 sites were substituted with alanine. The catalytic activity of the OleP variant was assessed by whole-cell catalysis, high-throughput screening, and HPLC analysis. Thirteen OleP mutants showed enhanced MDCA synthesis compared to the wild type, including P88A, A95G, H101A, R105A, L160A, S240A, S249A, S292A, A293G, V297A, H351A, H355A, and A359G. Notably, A95G, S240A, and S292A increased MDCA conversion rate from 32.5% to 89.4%, 94.5%, and 81.2%, respectively ( Fig. 4 C). Additionally, three mutants, including F84A, P88A, and S240A, enabled the 7β-hydroxylation of LCA to UDCA, with conversion rates of 1.5%, 1.8%, and 1.1%, respectively ( Fig. 4 D). ### 3.5. Optimization of OleP mutants based on rational design for enhanced UDCA synthesis
Through the application of semi-rational design, we successfully identified key residues involved in LCA hydroxylation and obtained the S240A mutant, along with its crystal structure, as well as the UDCA-enhanced triple mutant F84Q/S240A/V291G. This semi-rational design approach proved highly effective in the initial phase, enabling the construction of a focused mutant library for screening key residues without necessitating complex calculations. With these experimental advancements, our research objective evolved from merely identifying key residues to the precise optimization of UDCA synthesis efficiency and regioselectivity. Recognizing the limitations of semi-rational design in achieving multi-site precision, we employed rational design methodologies, specifically GEMS and Rosetta, to further enhance UDCA synthesis [ 32 ]. Building on the triple mutant F84Q/S240A/V291G, GEMS integrated structural, sequence, and evolutionary data to predict high-quality mutants, thereby guiding virtual saturation mutagenesis of 18 active site residues while ensuring diversity. The activity of the mutants was predicted with the GEME approach, and the top variants were reranked based on LCA binding affinity calculated by Rosetta Cartesian ΔΔG ( Table S5 for ΔΔG, Table S6 for reranking results). From the top 150 GEMS-predicted variants, 31 variants were selected, considering the sequence diversity and active pocket volume ( Fig. 5 A). Additionally, Foldseek was used to identify homologous structures and guide further mutant designs based on sequence insertions or deletions ( Fig. 5 A). Of the 35 rationally designed mutants, three mutants, OleP F84Q/S240A/V291G/E89D , OleP F84Q/S240A/V291G/E89N , and OleP F84Q/S240A/V291G/V239I , showed improved UDCA conversion ( Fig. 5 B). Among the variants, OleP F84Q/S240A/V291G/E89N was the most effective, raising UDCA yield from 6.5% to 12.1% (160.9 μM UDCA from 1.33 mM LCA at 12 h; UDCA selectively:79.5%) ( Fig. S1 and Fig. 5 B).

### 4. Conclusion
In this study, P450 OleP was systematically evaluated for its capacity to hydroxylate LCA into high-value bile acid derivatives, MDCA and UDCA. An improved high-throughput detection method was established to efficiently facilitate the screening of mutants with enhanced UDCA synthesis activity, overcoming the limitations of traditional screening approaches. Guided by the crystal structure of the OleP-LCA complex, semi-rational design identified the S240A mutant, which achieved a high MDCA conversion rate of 98.3% (1.30 mM MDCA from 1.33 mM LCA at 12h). The crystal structure of the S240A mutant was further resolved to elucidate the structural basis underlying its enhanced catalytic activity. Subsequently, the quadruple mutant F84Q/S240A/V291G/E89 N was yielded through rational design, achieving a UDCA conversion rate of 12.1% (160.9 μM UDCA from 1.33 mM LCA at 12 h). Molecular dynamics simulations were employed to elucidate the catalytic mechanisms of both S240A (the distance between C6–H and the oxygen atom of Cpd I is more favorable for hydrogen abstraction) and the quadruple mutant F84Q/S240A/V291G/E89 N (active pocket remodeling), which contribute to their superior catalytic performance.

### Abstract
Cytochrome P450 enzymes are promising for synthesizing therapeutically beneficial bile acids murideoxycholic acid (MDCA) and ursodeoxycholic acid (UDCA) from lithocholic acid (LCA). However, the catalytic efficiency of P450 enzymes remains inadequate for large-scale production. Here, we address this limitation by engineering P450 OleP, achieving high activity for the efficient synthesis of MDCA and UDCA. Firstly, an improved high-throughput detection method for UDCA was developed to accelerate the screening process for mutants with improved UDCA synthesis capabilities. Next, the mutant S240A was generated through semi-rational design guided by the crystal structure of the OleP-LCA complex, resulting in an improvement in the MDCA conversion rate from 32.5% to 98.3% using 0.5 g L −1 of LCA as the substrate. Furthermore, the crystal structure of the S240A mutant was resolved and analyzed. Subsequently, the mutant F84Q/S240A/V291G/E89 N was developed through rational design, improving the UDCA conversion rate from 6.5% to 12.1% with 0.5 g L −1 of LCA as the substrate. Finally, molecular dynamics simulations were employed to elucidate the mechanisms underlying the enhanced catalytic activity of the S240A and F84Q/S240A/V291G/E89 N mutants. This study provides optimized P450 enzyme mutants for the synthesis of MDCA and UDCA and offers significant insights into the catalytic mechanisms underlying the production of hydroxylated, LCA-derived high-value-added compounds.

### 1. Introduction
Bile acids are a class of steroid compounds characterized by multiple hydroxyl groups. Derived from cholesterol metabolism in the liver, they play integral roles in the digestion and absorption of lipids and fat-soluble vitamins [ 1 ]. Furthermore, they act as pivotal signaling molecules regulating lipid, glucose, steroid, and energy metabolism throughout the body [ 2 ]. They also possess notable pharmacological properties and are employed in the treatment of hepatobiliary disorders and as anti-inflammatory agents [ 3 , 4 ]. Among them, MDCA is crucial for maintaining bile acid homeostasis and preventing gallstone formation [ 5 , 6 ]. UDCA, another important bile acid and a natural active component found in bear bile, is approved for therapeutic use in various liver and gallbladder diseases [ 7 ].
Currently, production methods for MDCA and UDCA face significant limitations. The conventional approach of extracting these compounds from animal livers is constrained by ethical considerations [ 8 ]. Chemoenzymatic synthesis, while popular, often involves multi-step reactions with low yields (e.g., 30% for UDCA from cholic acid) [ 9 ] and suffers from high energy consumption and waste generation [ 10 ]. Similarly, MDCA synthesis from hyodeoxycholic acid requires harsh conditions and hazardous reagents (e.g., hypochlorous acid), posing potential environmental risks [ 11 ]. With advancements in biotechnology and the development of green biomanufacturing, enzyme-based biosynthesis technologies for MDCA and UDCA have emerged, offering advantages in terms of environmental sustainability, efficiency, and safety. Currently, UDCA is predominantly produced via a two-step enzymatic process using 7α-/7β-hydroxysteroid dehydrogenases with chenodeoxycholic acid as the substrate [ 12 , 13 ]. Notably, chenodeoxycholic acid is utilized in medical treatment due to its strong ability to dissolve cholesterol gallstones [ 14 ].

### 2.3. Production conditions for the P450 candidates and purification for OleP
The successfully engineered recombinant E. coli strains, each harboring a candidate P450 enzyme and redox partners, were initially activated by culturing on LB agar plates supplemented with 50 μg mL −1 of kanamycin at 37 °C for 12 h. A single colony was picked and inoculated into 2 mL of LB broth containing 50 μg mL −1 of kanamycin, followed by incubation at 37 °C with shaking at 220 rpm for 12 h to prepare the seed culture. Subsequently, 1 mL of the seed culture was transferred into 250 mL shaking flasks containing 50 mL of TB medium supplemented with 50 μg mL −1 of kanamycin, and the cultures were incubated at 37 °C with shaking at 220 rpm. When the optical density at 600 nm (OD 600 ) reached 0.6–0.8 (usually after 3–5 h of incubation), the culture was cooled to 16 °C and supplemented with 0.4 mM ALA and 0.2 mM FeCl 3 . Production of P450s proteins was induced by adding 0.2 mM IPTG, and the culture was further incubated for another 36 h. After cultivation, the cells were harvested by centrifugation at 18,400g for 10 min, washed twice with 100 mM potassium phosphate buffer (pH 7.4), and then prepared for subsequent purification and whole-cell catalysis experiments.

### 2.6. High-throughput screening method for UDCA and MDCA
The gene encoding 7β-HSDH ( A4ECA9 .1) from Collinsella aerofaciens was codon-optimized and synthesized by GenScript (Nanjing, China). The 7β-HSDH gene was subsequently subcloned into the C-terminal region of the OleP gene in plasmid pRSF-OleP-CamAB, with the incorporation of a ribosome binding site (RBS; GGATCCGAATTCGAGCTCATAAAAGGAGGAAAATAT), yielding the recombinant plasmid pRSF-OleP-CamAB-HSDH. After the whole-cell reaction, 50 μL of the reaction product was transferred to a 96-well microplate. Subsequently, 100 μL of a 2,4-dinitrophenylhydrazine solution (10 mM, dissolved in a 1:1 v/v mixture of dimethyl sulfoxide and ethanol) was added to the each well. The microplate was placed on a shaker and incubated at 25 °C with shaking at 800 rpm for 30 min. Following this, 100 μL of NaOH solution (4 M, dissolved in a 40% v/v ethanol aqueous solution) was added to the wells. Following the 30 min standing period at room temperature, the mixture was centrifuged at 18,400 g for 5 min to collect the supernatant. A 100 μL aliquot of the supernatant was transferred to a new 96-well microplate, and the absorbance at 440 nm was measured. Samples with high absorbance values were selected for further HPLC analysis.

### 2.7. Molecular docking and molecular dynamics (MD) simulations
The substrate LCA was docked into the active site of the OleP S240A crystal structure using Auto-Dock Vina in Chimera [ 23 ]. Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations of the mutant-substrate complex were performed to analyze the dynamic structural characteristics of the protein complexes. The protonation states of titratable residues (His, Glu, and Asp) were determined based on p K a values using PROPKA3.1 and through visual inspection of local H-bonding networks [ 24 , 25 ]. Histidine residues His 30, His 138, His 246, His 333, and His 354 were protonated at the δ position, while His 23, His 101, His 228, His 258, His 320, His 340, His 346, His 351, and His 355 were protonated at the ε position. Except for amino acids E89 and D91, which remain protonated, all other acidic residues are deprotonated. The substrate LCA was parameterized using the general AMBER Force Field (GAFF) [ 26 ], with atomic charge parameters fitted via the restrained electrostatic potential (RESP) method [ 27 ]. The RESP charges were calculated using Multiwfn software [ 28 , 29 ]. The force field parameters for Cpd I were sourced from a previous study [ 30 ]. The Amber ff14SB force field was employed for the protein residues [ 31 ]. The complex system was solvated in a 16 Å TIP3P water box, and Na + was added to the protein surface to neutralize the total charge of the system.

### 3.1. Screening of efficient P450 enzymes for converting LCA to MDCA and UDCA
According to previous reports, the three-component P450 OleP can catalyze the production of 6β-OH deoxycholic acid from deoxycholic acid, as well as the synthesis of MDCA from LCA. However, when whole-cell catalysts were prepared using E. coli C43(DE3) strain harboring the pET28a-oleP and pACYCDuet-camA-camB plasmids, the conversion rate of LCA to MDCA was merely 1.6% [ 33 ]. To achieve efficient conversion of LCA to MDCA and UDCA, several P450s were selected to investigate their hydroxylation activity towards LCA, including the self-sufficient P450 BM3, two-component P450 enzymes (CYP5150AP3, P450 cur , and P450 FE ), and other three-component P450 enzymes (CYP105A1, CYP105A3, CYP105AB3, CYP105D7, and CYP105P2).
An efficient redox partner Sm CPR I453V from Silybum marianum was employed to construct a whole-cell biocatalysis system for two-component P450s, while putidaredoxin reductase (CamA) and putidaredoxin (CamB) from Pseudomonas putida were used to establish the catalytic system for three-component P450s. The genes encoding theses redox partners were co-expressed with the corresponding P450 genes in the BL21(DE3) using a high-copy number plasmid pRSFDuet-1, resulting in ten recombinant E. coli strains harboring different P450s ( Fig. 1 A) . Subsequently, the hydroxylation activity toward LCA was evaluated using these whole-cell biocatalysts.

### 3.2. Improving a high-throughput screening method for detecting UDCA
Despite advances in P450-mediated steroid hydroxylation via protein engineering [ 9 , 17 ], low steroid detection efficiency limits the screening of a large P450 mutation library. We previously established a high-throughput MDCA detection method using 75% H 2 SO 4 –EtOH, which outperforms the traditional NAD(P)H consumption assay in accuracy ( Fig. 2 A) [ 11 ]. For UDCA, a colorimetric assay based on the water-soluble tetrazolium salt, 2-(4-iodophenyl)-3-(4-nitrophenyl)-5-(2,4-disulfophenyl)-2H-tetrazolium (WST-1)/1-methoxy-5-methylphenazinium methyl sulfate (mPMS) system has been established, which couples NADPH production (from 7β-hydroxysteroid dehydrogenase catalyzed UDCA oxidation) [ 9 ]. Here, we improved the high-throughput detection method for UDCA by coupling two reactions ( Fig. 2 A): oxidation of UDCA to 7-keto-lithocholic acid (7-KLCA) via a NADP + -dependent 7β-hydroxysteroid dehydrogenase from Collinsella aerofaciens [ 19 ], followed by 2,4-dinitrophenylhydrazine (DNPH)-based ketone detection for 7-KLCA [ 36 ].

### 3.3. Structural characteristics of the OleP-LCA complex
To provide structural information for the subsequent rational design of P450 OleP, the crystal structure of OleP was resolved. The purified OleP enzyme was obtained using a Hi-trap chelating HP column, gel filtration chromatography, and ion-exchange chromatography with a Mono Q column (GE Healthcare) ( Fig. S2A–S2D ). The purified OleP enzyme was crystallized by sitting-drop vapor-diffusion, producing OleP (PDB: 21LA ) and OleP-LCA (PDB: 21GU ) complex structures at 1.98 Å and 2.79 Å resolution ( Fig. 3 A, Fig. S2E, Fig. S3 and Table S4 ). The asymmetric unit contained two molecules, each adopting the canonical P450 fold with 14 α-helices, 15 β-strands ( Fig. S4 ), and a central heme group coordinated by a thiolate ligand. Within each asymmetric unit, two OleP molecules (designated as chains A and B) adopt nearly identical conformations (RMSD = 0.31 Å), exhibiting high similarity to the previously reported crystal structure of OleP (PDB: 4XE3 ) with an RMSD of 1.1 Å ( Fig. S5A ) [ 37 ]. Structural alignment using the Dali Server confirmed that OleP shares high similarity with P450OleP (PDB: 4XE3 , 8QYI , Z-score 61.4, 52.1, Fig. S5 ), including conserved catalytic motifs in the I- and L-helices. The iron in the heme group in the active site is axially coordinated with the conserved cysteine residue at position 356. The two propionate groups of the heme molecule form electrostatic interactions with two highly conserved histidine residues (at positions 101 and 354), two arginine residues (at positions 105 and 298), a leucine residue (at position 94), and a phenylalanine residue (at position 321).

### 3.4. Semi-rational design of OleP to enhance the synthesis of MDCA and UDCA
To identify key residues affecting OleP-catalyzed LCA hydroxylation, a comprehensive mutant library with 60 amino acid sites in the substrate cavity was generated. This included ten heme-interacting residues, twenty-one residues within 5 Å of the heme, nine residues contacting the substrate LCA, and twenty residues within 5 Å of LCA ( Fig. 4 A–B, and S8A-B). Alanine residues at positions 95, 244, 293, 348, 353, and 359 were mutated to glycine, while the remaining 54 sites were substituted with alanine. The catalytic activity of the OleP variant was assessed by whole-cell catalysis, high-throughput screening, and HPLC analysis. Thirteen OleP mutants showed enhanced MDCA synthesis compared to the wild type, including P88A, A95G, H101A, R105A, L160A, S240A, S249A, S292A, A293G, V297A, H351A, H355A, and A359G. Notably, A95G, S240A, and S292A increased MDCA conversion rate from 32.5% to 89.4%, 94.5%, and 81.2%, respectively ( Fig. 4 C). Additionally, three mutants, including F84A, P88A, and S240A, enabled the 7β-hydroxylation of LCA to UDCA, with conversion rates of 1.5%, 1.8%, and 1.1%, respectively ( Fig. 4 D). ### 3.5. Optimization of OleP mutants based on rational design for enhanced UDCA synthesis
Through the application of semi-rational design, we successfully identified key residues involved in LCA hydroxylation and obtained the S240A mutant, along with its crystal structure, as well as the UDCA-enhanced triple mutant F84Q/S240A/V291G. This semi-rational design approach proved highly effective in the initial phase, enabling the construction of a focused mutant library for screening key residues without necessitating complex calculations. With these experimental advancements, our research objective evolved from merely identifying key residues to the precise optimization of UDCA synthesis efficiency and regioselectivity. Recognizing the limitations of semi-rational design in achieving multi-site precision, we employed rational design methodologies, specifically GEMS and Rosetta, to further enhance UDCA synthesis [ 32 ]. Building on the triple mutant F84Q/S240A/V291G, GEMS integrated structural, sequence, and evolutionary data to predict high-quality mutants, thereby guiding virtual saturation mutagenesis of 18 active site residues while ensuring diversity. The activity of the mutants was predicted with the GEME approach, and the top variants were reranked based on LCA binding affinity calculated by Rosetta Cartesian ΔΔG ( Table S5 for ΔΔG, Table S6 for reranking results). From the top 150 GEMS-predicted variants, 31 variants were selected, considering the sequence diversity and active pocket volume ( Fig. 5 A). Additionally, Foldseek was used to identify homologous structures and guide further mutant designs based on sequence insertions or deletions ( Fig. 5 A). Of the 35 rationally designed mutants, three mutants, OleP F84Q/S240A/V291G/E89D , OleP F84Q/S240A/V291G/E89N , and OleP F84Q/S240A/V291G/V239I , showed improved UDCA conversion ( Fig. 5 B). ### 4. Conclusion
In this study, P450 OleP was systematically evaluated for its capacity to hydroxylate LCA into high-value bile acid derivatives, MDCA and UDCA. An improved high-throughput detection method was established to efficiently facilitate the screening of mutants with enhanced UDCA synthesis activity, overcoming the limitations of traditional screening approaches. Guided by the crystal structure of the OleP-LCA complex, semi-rational design identified the S240A mutant, which achieved a high MDCA conversion rate of 98.3% (1.30 mM MDCA from 1.33 mM LCA at 12h). The crystal structure of the S240A mutant was further resolved to elucidate the structural basis underlying its enhanced catalytic activity. Subsequently, the quadruple mutant F84Q/S240A/V291G/E89 N was yielded through rational design, achieving a UDCA conversion rate of 12.1% (160.9 μM UDCA from 1.33 mM LCA at 12 h). Molecular dynamics simulations were employed to elucidate the catalytic mechanisms of both S240A (the distance between C6–H and the oxygen atom of Cpd I is more favorable for hydrogen abstraction) and the quadruple mutant F84Q/S240A/V291G/E89 N (active pocket remodeling), which contribute to their superior catalytic performance.

### Abstract
Cytochrome P450 enzymes are promising for synthesizing therapeutically beneficial bile acids murideoxycholic acid (MDCA) and ursodeoxycholic acid (UDCA) from lithocholic acid (LCA). However, the catalytic efficiency of P450 enzymes remains inadequate for large-scale production. Here, we address this limitation by engineering P450 OleP, achieving high activity for the efficient synthesis of MDCA and UDCA. Firstly, an improved high-throughput detection method for UDCA was developed to accelerate the screening process for mutants with improved UDCA synthesis capabilities. Next, the mutant S240A was generated through semi-rational design guided by the crystal structure of the OleP-LCA complex, resulting in an improvement in the MDCA conversion rate from 32.5% to 98.3% using 0.5 g L −1 of LCA as the substrate. Furthermore, the crystal structure of the S240A mutant was resolved and analyzed. Subsequently, the mutant F84Q/S240A/V291G/E89 N was developed through rational design, improving the UDCA conversion rate from 6.5% to 12.1% with 0.5 g L −1 of LCA as the substrate. Finally, molecular dynamics simulations were employed to elucidate the mechanisms underlying the enhanced catalytic activity of the S240A and F84Q/S240A/V291G/E89 N mutants. This study provides optimized P450 enzyme mutants for the synthesis of MDCA and UDCA and offers significant insights into the catalytic mechanisms underlying the production of hydroxylated, LCA-derived high-value-added compounds.

### 1. Introduction
Bile acids are a class of steroid compounds characterized by multiple hydroxyl groups. Derived from cholesterol metabolism in the liver, they play integral roles in the digestion and absorption of lipids and fat-soluble vitamins [ 1 ]. Furthermore, they act as pivotal signaling molecules regulating lipid, glucose, steroid, and energy metabolism throughout the body [ 2 ]. They also possess notable pharmacological properties and are employed in the treatment of hepatobiliary disorders and as anti-inflammatory agents [ 3 , 4 ]. Among them, MDCA is crucial for maintaining bile acid homeostasis and preventing gallstone formation [ 5 , 6 ]. UDCA, another important bile acid and a natural active component found in bear bile, is approved for therapeutic use in various liver and gallbladder diseases [ 7 ].
Currently, production methods for MDCA and UDCA face significant limitations. The conventional approach of extracting these compounds from animal livers is constrained by ethical considerations [ 8 ]. Chemoenzymatic synthesis, while popular, often involves multi-step reactions with low yields (e.g., 30% for UDCA from cholic acid) [ 9 ] and suffers from high energy consumption and waste generation [ 10 ]. Similarly, MDCA synthesis from hyodeoxycholic acid requires harsh conditions and hazardous reagents (e.g., hypochlorous acid), posing potential environmental risks [ 11 ]. With advancements in biotechnology and the development of green biomanufacturing, enzyme-based biosynthesis technologies for MDCA and UDCA have emerged, offering advantages in terms of environmental sustainability, efficiency, and safety. Currently, UDCA is predominantly produced via a two-step enzymatic process using 7α-/7β-hydroxysteroid dehydrogenases with chenodeoxycholic acid as the substrate [ 12 , 13 ]. Notably, chenodeoxycholic acid is utilized in medical treatment due to its strong ability to dissolve cholesterol gallstones [ 14 ].

### 2.3. Production conditions for the P450 candidates and purification for OleP
The successfully engineered recombinant E. coli strains, each harboring a candidate P450 enzyme and redox partners, were initially activated by culturing on LB agar plates supplemented with 50 μg mL −1 of kanamycin at 37 °C for 12 h. A single colony was picked and inoculated into 2 mL of LB broth containing 50 μg mL −1 of kanamycin, followed by incubation at 37 °C with shaking at 220 rpm for 12 h to prepare the seed culture. Subsequently, 1 mL of the seed culture was transferred into 250 mL shaking flasks containing 50 mL of TB medium supplemented with 50 μg mL −1 of kanamycin, and the cultures were incubated at 37 °C with shaking at 220 rpm. When the optical density at 600 nm (OD 600 ) reached 0.6–0.8 (usually after 3–5 h of incubation), the culture was cooled to 16 °C and supplemented with 0.4 mM ALA and 0.2 mM FeCl 3 . Production of P450s proteins was induced by adding 0.2 mM IPTG, and the culture was further incubated for another 36 h. After cultivation, the cells were harvested by centrifugation at 18,400g for 10 min, washed twice with 100 mM potassium phosphate buffer (pH 7.4), and then prepared for subsequent purification and whole-cell catalysis experiments.

### 2.6. High-throughput screening method for UDCA and MDCA
The gene encoding 7β-HSDH ( A4ECA9 .1) from Collinsella aerofaciens was codon-optimized and synthesized by GenScript (Nanjing, China). The 7β-HSDH gene was subsequently subcloned into the C-terminal region of the OleP gene in plasmid pRSF-OleP-CamAB, with the incorporation of a ribosome binding site (RBS; GGATCCGAATTCGAGCTCATAAAAGGAGGAAAATAT), yielding the recombinant plasmid pRSF-OleP-CamAB-HSDH. After the whole-cell reaction, 50 μL of the reaction product was transferred to a 96-well microplate. Subsequently, 100 μL of a 2,4-dinitrophenylhydrazine solution (10 mM, dissolved in a 1:1 v/v mixture of dimethyl sulfoxide and ethanol) was added to the each well. The microplate was placed on a shaker and incubated at 25 °C with shaking at 800 rpm for 30 min. Following this, 100 μL of NaOH solution (4 M, dissolved in a 40% v/v ethanol aqueous solution) was added to the wells. Following the 30 min standing period at room temperature, the mixture was centrifuged at 18,400 g for 5 min to collect the supernatant. A 100 μL aliquot of the supernatant was transferred to a new 96-well microplate, and the absorbance at 440 nm was measured. Samples with high absorbance values were selected for further HPLC analysis.

### 2.7. Molecular docking and molecular dynamics (MD) simulations
The substrate LCA was docked into the active site of the OleP S240A crystal structure using Auto-Dock Vina in Chimera [ 23 ]. Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations of the mutant-substrate complex were performed to analyze the dynamic structural characteristics of the protein complexes. The protonation states of titratable residues (His, Glu, and Asp) were determined based on p K a values using PROPKA3.1 and through visual inspection of local H-bonding networks [ 24 , 25 ]. Histidine residues His 30, His 138, His 246, His 333, and His 354 were protonated at the δ position, while His 23, His 101, His 228, His 258, His 320, His 340, His 346, His 351, and His 355 were protonated at the ε position. Except for amino acids E89 and D91, which remain protonated, all other acidic residues are deprotonated. The substrate LCA was parameterized using the general AMBER Force Field (GAFF) [ 26 ], with atomic charge parameters fitted via the restrained electrostatic potential (RESP) method [ 27 ]. The RESP charges were calculated using Multiwfn software [ 28 , 29 ]. The force field parameters for Cpd I were sourced from a previous study [ 30 ]. The Amber ff14SB force field was employed for the protein residues [ 31 ]. The complex system was solvated in a 16 Å TIP3P water box, and Na + was added to the protein surface to neutralize the total charge of the system.

### 3.1. Screening of efficient P450 enzymes for converting LCA to MDCA and UDCA
According to previous reports, the three-component P450 OleP can catalyze the production of 6β-OH deoxycholic acid from deoxycholic acid, as well as the synthesis of MDCA from LCA. However, when whole-cell catalysts were prepared using E. coli C43(DE3) strain harboring the pET28a-oleP and pACYCDuet-camA-camB plasmids, the conversion rate of LCA to MDCA was merely 1.6% [ 33 ]. To achieve efficient conversion of LCA to MDCA and UDCA, several P450s were selected to investigate their hydroxylation activity towards LCA, including the self-sufficient P450 BM3, two-component P450 enzymes (CYP5150AP3, P450 cur , and P450 FE ), and other three-component P450 enzymes (CYP105A1, CYP105A3, CYP105AB3, CYP105D7, and CYP105P2).
An efficient redox partner Sm CPR I453V from Silybum marianum was employed to construct a whole-cell biocatalysis system for two-component P450s, while putidaredoxin reductase (CamA) and putidaredoxin (CamB) from Pseudomonas putida were used to establish the catalytic system for three-component P450s. The genes encoding theses redox partners were co-expressed with the corresponding P450 genes in the BL21(DE3) using a high-copy number plasmid pRSFDuet-1, resulting in ten recombinant E. coli strains harboring different P450s ( Fig. 1 A) . Subsequently, the hydroxylation activity toward LCA was evaluated using these whole-cell biocatalysts.

### 3.2. Improving a high-throughput screening method for detecting UDCA
Despite advances in P450-mediated steroid hydroxylation via protein engineering [ 9 , 17 ], low steroid detection efficiency limits the screening of a large P450 mutation library. We previously established a high-throughput MDCA detection method using 75% H 2 SO 4 –EtOH, which outperforms the traditional NAD(P)H consumption assay in accuracy ( Fig. 2 A) [ 11 ]. For UDCA, a colorimetric assay based on the water-soluble tetrazolium salt, 2-(4-iodophenyl)-3-(4-nitrophenyl)-5-(2,4-disulfophenyl)-2H-tetrazolium (WST-1)/1-methoxy-5-methylphenazinium methyl sulfate (mPMS) system has been established, which couples NADPH production (from 7β-hydroxysteroid dehydrogenase catalyzed UDCA oxidation) [ 9 ]. Here, we improved the high-throughput detection method for UDCA by coupling two reactions ( Fig. 2 A): oxidation of UDCA to 7-keto-lithocholic acid (7-KLCA) via a NADP + -dependent 7β-hydroxysteroid dehydrogenase from Collinsella aerofaciens [ 19 ], followed by 2,4-dinitrophenylhydrazine (DNPH)-based ketone detection for 7-KLCA [ 36 ].

### 3.3. Structural characteristics of the OleP-LCA complex
To provide structural information for the subsequent rational design of P450 OleP, the crystal structure of OleP was resolved. The purified OleP enzyme was obtained using a Hi-trap chelating HP column, gel filtration chromatography, and ion-exchange chromatography with a Mono Q column (GE Healthcare) ( Fig. S2A–S2D ). The purified OleP enzyme was crystallized by sitting-drop vapor-diffusion, producing OleP (PDB: 21LA ) and OleP-LCA (PDB: 21GU ) complex structures at 1.98 Å and 2.79 Å resolution ( Fig. 3 A, Fig. S2E, Fig. S3 and Table S4 ). The asymmetric unit contained two molecules, each adopting the canonical P450 fold with 14 α-helices, 15 β-strands ( Fig. S4 ), and a central heme group coordinated by a thiolate ligand. Within each asymmetric unit, two OleP molecules (designated as chains A and B) adopt nearly identical conformations (RMSD = 0.31 Å), exhibiting high similarity to the previously reported crystal structure of OleP (PDB: 4XE3 ) with an RMSD of 1.1 Å ( Fig. S5A ) [ 37 ]. Structural alignment using the Dali Server confirmed that OleP shares high similarity with P450OleP (PDB: 4XE3 , 8QYI , Z-score 61.4, 52.1, Fig. S5 ), including conserved catalytic motifs in the I- and L-helices. The iron in the heme group in the active site is axially coordinated with the conserved cysteine residue at position 356. The two propionate groups of the heme molecule form electrostatic interactions with two highly conserved histidine residues (at positions 101 and 354), two arginine residues (at positions 105 and 298), a leucine residue (at position 94), and a phenylalanine residue (at position 321).

### 3.4. Semi-rational design of OleP to enhance the synthesis of MDCA and UDCA
To identify key residues affecting OleP-catalyzed LCA hydroxylation, a comprehensive mutant library with 60 amino acid sites in the substrate cavity was generated. This included ten heme-interacting residues, twenty-one residues within 5 Å of the heme, nine residues contacting the substrate LCA, and twenty residues within 5 Å of LCA ( Fig. 4 A–B, and S8A-B). Alanine residues at positions 95, 244, 293, 348, 353, and 359 were mutated to glycine, while the remaining 54 sites were substituted with alanine. The catalytic activity of the OleP variant was assessed by whole-cell catalysis, high-throughput screening, and HPLC analysis. Thirteen OleP mutants showed enhanced MDCA synthesis compared to the wild type, including P88A, A95G, H101A, R105A, L160A, S240A, S249A, S292A, A293G, V297A, H351A, H355A, and A359G. Notably, A95G, S240A, and S292A increased MDCA conversion rate from 32.5% to 89.4%, 94.5%, and 81.2%, respectively ( Fig. 4 C). Additionally, three mutants, including F84A, P88A, and S240A, enabled the 7β-hydroxylation of LCA to UDCA, with conversion rates of 1.5%, 1.8%, and 1.1%, respectively ( Fig. 4 D). ### 3.5. Optimization of OleP mutants based on rational design for enhanced UDCA synthesis
Through the application of semi-rational design, we successfully identified key residues involved in LCA hydroxylation and obtained the S240A mutant, along with its crystal structure, as well as the UDCA-enhanced triple mutant F84Q/S240A/V291G. This semi-rational design approach proved highly effective in the initial phase, enabling the construction of a focused mutant library for screening key residues without necessitating complex calculations. With these experimental advancements, our research objective evolved from merely identifying key residues to the precise optimization of UDCA synthesis efficiency and regioselectivity. Recognizing the limitations of semi-rational design in achieving multi-site precision, we employed rational design methodologies, specifically GEMS and Rosetta, to further enhance UDCA synthesis [ 32 ]. Building on the triple mutant F84Q/S240A/V291G, GEMS integrated structural, sequence, and evolutionary data to predict high-quality mutants, thereby guiding virtual saturation mutagenesis of 18 active site residues while ensuring diversity. The activity of the mutants was predicted with the GEME approach, and the top variants were reranked based on LCA binding affinity calculated by Rosetta Cartesian ΔΔG ( Table S5 for ΔΔG, Table S6 for reranking results). From the top 150 GEMS-predicted variants, 31 variants were selected, considering the sequence diversity and active pocket volume ( Fig. 5 A). Additionally, Foldseek was used to identify homologous structures and guide further mutant designs based on sequence insertions or deletions ( Fig. 5 A). Of the 35 rationally designed mutants, three mutants, OleP F84Q/S240A/V291G/E89D , OleP F84Q/S240A/V291G/E89N , and OleP F84Q/S240A/V291G/V239I , showed improved UDCA conversion ( Fig. 5 B). Among the variants, OleP F84Q/S240A/V291G/E89N was the most effective, raising UDCA yield from 6.5% to 12.1% (160.9 μM UDCA from 1.33 mM LCA at 12 h; UDCA selectively:79.5%) ( Fig. S1 and Fig. 5 B).

### 4. Conclusion
In this study, P450 OleP was systematically evaluated for its capacity to hydroxylate LCA into high-value bile acid derivatives, MDCA and UDCA. An improved high-throughput detection method was established to efficiently facilitate the screening of mutants with enhanced UDCA synthesis activity, overcoming the limitations of traditional screening approaches. Guided by the crystal structure of the OleP-LCA complex, semi-rational design identified the S240A mutant, which achieved a high MDCA conversion rate of 98.3% (1.30 mM MDCA from 1.33 mM LCA at 12h). The crystal structure of the S240A mutant was further resolved to elucidate the structural basis underlying its enhanced catalytic activity. Subsequently, the quadruple mutant F84Q/S240A/V291G/E89 N was yielded through rational design, achieving a UDCA conversion rate of 12.1% (160.9 μM UDCA from 1.33 mM LCA at 12 h). Molecular dynamics simulations were employed to elucidate the catalytic mechanisms of both S240A (the distance between C6–H and the oxygen atom of Cpd I is more favorable for hydrogen abstraction) and the quadruple mutant F84Q/S240A/V291G/E89 N (active pocket remodeling), which contribute to their superior catalytic performance.

### Abstract
Cytochrome P450 enzymes are promising for synthesizing therapeutically beneficial bile acids murideoxycholic acid (MDCA) and ursodeoxycholic acid (UDCA) from lithocholic acid (LCA). However, the catalytic efficiency of P450 enzymes remains inadequate for large-scale production. Here, we address this limitation by engineering P450 OleP, achieving high activity for the efficient synthesis of MDCA and UDCA. Firstly, an improved high-throughput detection method for UDCA was developed to accelerate the screening process for mutants with improved UDCA synthesis capabilities. Next, the mutant S240A was generated through semi-rational design guided by the crystal structure of the OleP-LCA complex, resulting in an improvement in the MDCA conversion rate from 32.5% to 98.3% using 0.5 g L −1 of LCA as the substrate. Furthermore, the crystal structure of the S240A mutant was resolved and analyzed. Subsequently, the mutant F84Q/S240A/V291G/E89 N was developed through rational design, improving the UDCA conversion rate from 6.5% to 12.1% with 0.5 g L −1 of LCA as the substrate. Finally, molecular dynamics simulations were employed to elucidate the mechanisms underlying the enhanced catalytic activity of the S240A and F84Q/S240A/V291G/E89 N mutants. This study provides optimized P450 enzyme mutants for the synthesis of MDCA and UDCA and offers significant insights into the catalytic mechanisms underlying the production of hydroxylated, LCA-derived high-value-added compounds.

### 1. Introduction
Bile acids are a class of steroid compounds characterized by multiple hydroxyl groups. Derived from cholesterol metabolism in the liver, they play integral roles in the digestion and absorption of lipids and fat-soluble vitamins [ 1 ]. Furthermore, they act as pivotal signaling molecules regulating lipid, glucose, steroid, and energy metabolism throughout the body [ 2 ]. They also possess notable pharmacological properties and are employed in the treatment of hepatobiliary disorders and as anti-inflammatory agents [ 3 , 4 ]. Among them, MDCA is crucial for maintaining bile acid homeostasis and preventing gallstone formation [ 5 , 6 ]. UDCA, another important bile acid and a natural active component found in bear bile, is approved for therapeutic use in various liver and gallbladder diseases [ 7 ].
Currently, production methods for MDCA and UDCA face significant limitations. The conventional approach of extracting these compounds from animal livers is constrained by ethical considerations [ 8 ]. Chemoenzymatic synthesis, while popular, often involves multi-step reactions with low yields (e.g., 30% for UDCA from cholic acid) [ 9 ] and suffers from high energy consumption and waste generation [ 10 ]. Similarly, MDCA synthesis from hyodeoxycholic acid requires harsh conditions and hazardous reagents (e.g., hypochlorous acid), posing potential environmental risks [ 11 ]. With advancements in biotechnology and the development of green biomanufacturing, enzyme-based biosynthesis technologies for MDCA and UDCA have emerged, offering advantages in terms of environmental sustainability, efficiency, and safety. Currently, UDCA is predominantly produced via a two-step enzymatic process using 7α-/7β-hydroxysteroid dehydrogenases with chenodeoxycholic acid as the substrate [ 12 , 13 ]. Notably, chenodeoxycholic acid is utilized in medical treatment due to its strong ability to dissolve cholesterol gallstones [ 14 ].

### 2.3. Production conditions for the P450 candidates and purification for OleP
The successfully engineered recombinant E. coli strains, each harboring a candidate P450 enzyme and redox partners, were initially activated by culturing on LB agar plates supplemented with 50 μg mL −1 of kanamycin at 37 °C for 12 h. A single colony was picked and inoculated into 2 mL of LB broth containing 50 μg mL −1 of kanamycin, followed by incubation at 37 °C with shaking at 220 rpm for 12 h to prepare the seed culture. Subsequently, 1 mL of the seed culture was transferred into 250 mL shaking flasks containing 50 mL of TB medium supplemented with 50 μg mL −1 of kanamycin, and the cultures were incubated at 37 °C with shaking at 220 rpm. When the optical density at 600 nm (OD 600 ) reached 0.6–0.8 (usually after 3–5 h of incubation), the culture was cooled to 16 °C and supplemented with 0.4 mM ALA and 0.2 mM FeCl 3 . Production of P450s proteins was induced by adding 0.2 mM IPTG, and the culture was further incubated for another 36 h. After cultivation, the cells were harvested by centrifugation at 18,400g for 10 min, washed twice with 100 mM potassium phosphate buffer (pH 7.4), and then prepared for subsequent purification and whole-cell catalysis experiments.

### 2.6. High-throughput screening method for UDCA and MDCA
The gene encoding 7β-HSDH ( A4ECA9 .1) from Collinsella aerofaciens was codon-optimized and synthesized by GenScript (Nanjing, China). The 7β-HSDH gene was subsequently subcloned into the C-terminal region of the OleP gene in plasmid pRSF-OleP-CamAB, with the incorporation of a ribosome binding site (RBS; GGATCCGAATTCGAGCTCATAAAAGGAGGAAAATAT), yielding the recombinant plasmid pRSF-OleP-CamAB-HSDH. After the whole-cell reaction, 50 μL of the reaction product was transferred to a 96-well microplate. Subsequently, 100 μL of a 2,4-dinitrophenylhydrazine solution (10 mM, dissolved in a 1:1 v/v mixture of dimethyl sulfoxide and ethanol) was added to the each well. The microplate was placed on a shaker and incubated at 25 °C with shaking at 800 rpm for 30 min. Following this, 100 μL of NaOH solution (4 M, dissolved in a 40% v/v ethanol aqueous solution) was added to the wells. Following the 30 min standing period at room temperature, the mixture was centrifuged at 18,400 g for 5 min to collect the supernatant. A 100 μL aliquot of the supernatant was transferred to a new 96-well microplate, and the absorbance at 440 nm was measured. Samples with high absorbance values were selected for further HPLC analysis.

### 2.7. Molecular docking and molecular dynamics (MD) simulations
The substrate LCA was docked into the active site of the OleP S240A crystal structure using Auto-Dock Vina in Chimera [ 23 ]. Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations of the mutant-substrate complex were performed to analyze the dynamic structural characteristics of the protein complexes. The protonation states of titratable residues (His, Glu, and Asp) were determined based on p K a values using PROPKA3.1 and through visual inspection of local H-bonding networks [ 24 , 25 ]. Histidine residues His 30, His 138, His 246, His 333, and His 354 were protonated at the δ position, while His 23, His 101, His 228, His 258, His 320, His 340, His 346, His 351, and His 355 were protonated at the ε position. Except for amino acids E89 and D91, which remain protonated, all other acidic residues are deprotonated. The substrate LCA was parameterized using the general AMBER Force Field (GAFF) [ 26 ], with atomic charge parameters fitted via the restrained electrostatic potential (RESP) method [ 27 ]. The RESP charges were calculated using Multiwfn software [ 28 , 29 ]. The force field parameters for Cpd I were sourced from a previous study [ 30 ]. The Amber ff14SB force field was employed for the protein residues [ 31 ]. The complex system was solvated in a 16 Å TIP3P water box, and Na + was added to the protein surface to neutralize the total charge of the system.

### 3.1. Screening of efficient P450 enzymes for converting LCA to MDCA and UDCA
According to previous reports, the three-component P450 OleP can catalyze the production of 6β-OH deoxycholic acid from deoxycholic acid, as well as the synthesis of MDCA from LCA. However, when whole-cell catalysts were prepared using E. coli C43(DE3) strain harboring the pET28a-oleP and pACYCDuet-camA-camB plasmids, the conversion rate of LCA to MDCA was merely 1.6% [ 33 ]. To achieve efficient conversion of LCA to MDCA and UDCA, several P450s were selected to investigate their hydroxylation activity towards LCA, including the self-sufficient P450 BM3, two-component P450 enzymes (CYP5150AP3, P450 cur , and P450 FE ), and other three-component P450 enzymes (CYP105A1, CYP105A3, CYP105AB3, CYP105D7, and CYP105P2).
An efficient redox partner Sm CPR I453V from Silybum marianum was employed to construct a whole-cell biocatalysis system for two-component P450s, while putidaredoxin reductase (CamA) and putidaredoxin (CamB) from Pseudomonas putida were used to establish the catalytic system for three-component P450s. The genes encoding theses redox partners were co-expressed with the corresponding P450 genes in the BL21(DE3) using a high-copy number plasmid pRSFDuet-1, resulting in ten recombinant E. coli strains harboring different P450s ( Fig. 1 A) . Subsequently, the hydroxylation activity toward LCA was evaluated using these whole-cell biocatalysts.

### 3.2. Improving a high-throughput screening method for detecting UDCA
Despite advances in P450-mediated steroid hydroxylation via protein engineering [ 9 , 17 ], low steroid detection efficiency limits the screening of a large P450 mutation library. We previously established a high-throughput MDCA detection method using 75% H 2 SO 4 –EtOH, which outperforms the traditional NAD(P)H consumption assay in accuracy ( Fig. 2 A) [ 11 ]. For UDCA, a colorimetric assay based on the water-soluble tetrazolium salt, 2-(4-iodophenyl)-3-(4-nitrophenyl)-5-(2,4-disulfophenyl)-2H-tetrazolium (WST-1)/1-methoxy-5-methylphenazinium methyl sulfate (mPMS) system has been established, which couples NADPH production (from 7β-hydroxysteroid dehydrogenase catalyzed UDCA oxidation) [ 9 ]. Here, we improved the high-throughput detection method for UDCA by coupling two reactions ( Fig. 2 A): oxidation of UDCA to 7-keto-lithocholic acid (7-KLCA) via a NADP + -dependent 7β-hydroxysteroid dehydrogenase from Collinsella aerofaciens [ 19 ], followed by 2,4-dinitrophenylhydrazine (DNPH)-based ketone detection for 7-KLCA [ 36 ].

### 3.3. Structural characteristics of the OleP-LCA complex
To provide structural information for the subsequent rational design of P450 OleP, the crystal structure of OleP was resolved. The purified OleP enzyme was obtained using a Hi-trap chelating HP column, gel filtration chromatography, and ion-exchange chromatography with a Mono Q column (GE Healthcare) ( Fig. S2A–S2D ). The purified OleP enzyme was crystallized by sitting-drop vapor-diffusion, producing OleP (PDB: 21LA ) and OleP-LCA (PDB: 21GU ) complex structures at 1.98 Å and 2.79 Å resolution ( Fig. 3 A, Fig. S2E, Fig. S3 and Table S4 ). The asymmetric unit contained two molecules, each adopting the canonical P450 fold with 14 α-helices, 15 β-strands ( Fig. S4 ), and a central heme group coordinated by a thiolate ligand. Within each asymmetric unit, two OleP molecules (designated as chains A and B) adopt nearly identical conformations (RMSD = 0.31 Å), exhibiting high similarity to the previously reported crystal structure of OleP (PDB: 4XE3 ) with an RMSD of 1.1 Å ( Fig. S5A ) [ 37 ]. Structural alignment using the Dali Server confirmed that OleP shares high similarity with P450OleP (PDB: 4XE3 , 8QYI , Z-score 61.4, 52.1, Fig. S5 ), including conserved catalytic motifs in the I- and L-helices. The iron in the heme group in the active site is axially coordinated with the conserved cysteine residue at position 356. The two propionate groups of the heme molecule form electrostatic interactions with two highly conserved histidine residues (at positions 101 and 354), two arginine residues (at positions 105 and 298), a leucine residue (at position 94), and a phenylalanine residue (at position 321).

### 3.4. Semi-rational design of OleP to enhance the synthesis of MDCA and UDCA
To identify key residues affecting OleP-catalyzed LCA hydroxylation, a comprehensive mutant library with 60 amino acid sites in the substrate cavity was generated. This included ten heme-interacting residues, twenty-one residues within 5 Å of the heme, nine residues contacting the substrate LCA, and twenty residues within 5 Å of LCA ( Fig. 4 A–B, and S8A-B). Alanine residues at positions 95, 244, 293, 348, 353, and 359 were mutated to glycine, while the remaining 54 sites were substituted with alanine. The catalytic activity of the OleP variant was assessed by whole-cell catalysis, high-throughput screening, and HPLC analysis. Thirteen OleP mutants showed enhanced MDCA synthesis compared to the wild type, including P88A, A95G, H101A, R105A, L160A, S240A, S249A, S292A, A293G, V297A, H351A, H355A, and A359G. Notably, A95G, S240A, and S292A increased MDCA conversion rate from 32.5% to 89.4%, 94.5%, and 81.2%, respectively ( Fig. 4 C). Additionally, three mutants, including F84A, P88A, and S240A, enabled the 7β-hydroxylation of LCA to UDCA, with conversion rates of 1.5%, 1.8%, and 1.1%, respectively ( Fig. 4 D). ### 3.5. Optimization of OleP mutants based on rational design for enhanced UDCA synthesis
Through the application of semi-rational design, we successfully identified key residues involved in LCA hydroxylation and obtained the S240A mutant, along with its crystal structure, as well as the UDCA-enhanced triple mutant F84Q/S240A/V291G. This semi-rational design approach proved highly effective in the initial phase, enabling the construction of a focused mutant library for screening key residues without necessitating complex calculations. With these experimental advancements, our research objective evolved from merely identifying key residues to the precise optimization of UDCA synthesis efficiency and regioselectivity. Recognizing the limitations of semi-rational design in achieving multi-site precision, we employed rational design methodologies, specifically GEMS and Rosetta, to further enhance UDCA synthesis [ 32 ]. Building on the triple mutant F84Q/S240A/V291G, GEMS integrated structural, sequence, and evolutionary data to predict high-quality mutants, thereby guiding virtual saturation mutagenesis of 18 active site residues while ensuring diversity. The activity of the mutants was predicted with the GEME approach, and the top variants were reranked based on LCA binding affinity calculated by Rosetta Cartesian ΔΔG ( Table S5 for ΔΔG, Table S6 for reranking results). From the top 150 GEMS-predicted variants, 31 variants were selected, considering the sequence diversity and active pocket volume ( Fig. 5 A). Additionally, Foldseek was used to identify homologous structures and guide further mutant designs based on sequence insertions or deletions ( Fig. 5 A). Of the 35 rationally designed mutants, three mutants, OleP F84Q/S240A/V291G/E89D , OleP F84Q/S240A/V291G/E89N , and OleP F84Q/S240A/V291G/V239I , showed improved UDCA conversion ( Fig. 5 B). Among the variants, OleP F84Q/S240A/V291G/E89N was the most effective, raising UDCA yield from 6.5% to 12.1% (160.9 μM UDCA from 1.33 mM LCA at 12 h; UDCA selectively:79.5%) ( Fig. S1 and Fig. 5 B).

### 4. Conclusion
In this study, P450 OleP was systematically evaluated for its capacity to hydroxylate LCA into high-value bile acid derivatives, MDCA and UDCA. An improved high-throughput detection method was established to efficiently facilitate the screening of mutants with enhanced UDCA synthesis activity, overcoming the limitations of traditional screening approaches. Guided by the crystal structure of the OleP-LCA complex, semi-rational design identified the S240A mutant, which achieved a high MDCA conversion rate of 98.3% (1.30 mM MDCA from 1.33 mM LCA at 12h). The crystal structure of the S240A mutant was further resolved to elucidate the structural basis underlying its enhanced catalytic activity. Subsequently, the quadruple mutant F84Q/S240A/V291G/E89 N was yielded through rational design, achieving a UDCA conversion rate of 12.1% (160.9 μM UDCA from 1.33 mM LCA at 12 h). Molecular dynamics simulations were employed to elucidate the catalytic mechanisms of both S240A (the distance between C6–H and the oxygen atom of Cpd I is more favorable for hydrogen abstraction) and the quadruple mutant F84Q/S240A/V291G/E89 N (active pocket remodeling), which contribute to their superior catalytic performance.

### Abstract
Cytochrome P450 enzymes are promising for synthesizing therapeutically beneficial bile acids murideoxycholic acid (MDCA) and ursodeoxycholic acid (UDCA) from lithocholic acid (LCA). However, the catalytic efficiency of P450 enzymes remains inadequate for large-scale production. Here, we address this limitation by engineering P450 OleP, achieving high activity for the efficient synthesis of MDCA and UDCA. Firstly, an improved high-throughput detection method for UDCA was developed to accelerate the screening process for mutants with improved UDCA synthesis capabilities. Next, the mutant S240A was generated through semi-rational design guided by the crystal structure of the OleP-LCA complex, resulting in an improvement in the MDCA conversion rate from 32.5% to 98.3% using 0.5 g L −1 of LCA as the substrate. Furthermore, the crystal structure of the S240A mutant was resolved and analyzed. Subsequently, the mutant F84Q/S240A/V291G/E89 N was developed through rational design, improving the UDCA conversion rate from 6.5% to 12.1% with 0.5 g L −1 of LCA as the substrate. Finally, molecular dynamics simulations were employed to elucidate the mechanisms underlying the enhanced catalytic activity of the S240A and F84Q/S240A/V291G/E89 N mutants. This study provides optimized P450 enzyme mutants for the synthesis of MDCA and UDCA and offers significant insights into the catalytic mechanisms underlying the production of hydroxylated, LCA-derived high-value-added compounds.

### 1. Introduction
Bile acids are a class of steroid compounds characterized by multiple hydroxyl groups. Derived from cholesterol metabolism in the liver, they play integral roles in the digestion and absorption of lipids and fat-soluble vitamins [ 1 ]. Furthermore, they act as pivotal signaling molecules regulating lipid, glucose, steroid, and energy metabolism throughout the body [ 2 ]. They also possess notable pharmacological properties and are employed in the treatment of hepatobiliary disorders and as anti-inflammatory agents [ 3 , 4 ]. Among them, MDCA is crucial for maintaining bile acid homeostasis and preventing gallstone formation [ 5 , 6 ]. UDCA, another important bile acid and a natural active component found in bear bile, is approved for therapeutic use in various liver and gallbladder diseases [ 7 ].
Currently, production methods for MDCA and UDCA face significant limitations. The conventional approach of extracting these compounds from animal livers is constrained by ethical considerations [ 8 ]. Chemoenzymatic synthesis, while popular, often involves multi-step reactions with low yields (e.g., 30% for UDCA from cholic acid) [ 9 ] and suffers from high energy consumption and waste generation [ 10 ]. Similarly, MDCA synthesis from hyodeoxycholic acid requires harsh conditions and hazardous reagents (e.g., hypochlorous acid), posing potential environmental risks [ 11 ]. With advancements in biotechnology and the development of green biomanufacturing, enzyme-based biosynthesis technologies for MDCA and UDCA have emerged, offering advantages in terms of environmental sustainability, efficiency, and safety. Currently, UDCA is predominantly produced via a two-step enzymatic process using 7α-/7β-hydroxysteroid dehydrogenases with chenodeoxycholic acid as the substrate [ 12 , 13 ]. Notably, chenodeoxycholic acid is utilized in medical treatment due to its strong ability to dissolve cholesterol gallstones [ 14 ].

### 2.3. Production conditions for the P450 candidates and purification for OleP
The successfully engineered recombinant E. coli strains, each harboring a candidate P450 enzyme and redox partners, were initially activated by culturing on LB agar plates supplemented with 50 μg mL −1 of kanamycin at 37 °C for 12 h. A single colony was picked and inoculated into 2 mL of LB broth containing 50 μg mL −1 of kanamycin, followed by incubation at 37 °C with shaking at 220 rpm for 12 h to prepare the seed culture. Subsequently, 1 mL of the seed culture was transferred into 250 mL shaking flasks containing 50 mL of TB medium supplemented with 50 μg mL −1 of kanamycin, and the cultures were incubated at 37 °C with shaking at 220 rpm. When the optical density at 600 nm (OD 600 ) reached 0.6–0.8 (usually after 3–5 h of incubation), the culture was cooled to 16 °C and supplemented with 0.4 mM ALA and 0.2 mM FeCl 3 . Production of P450s proteins was induced by adding 0.2 mM IPTG, and the culture was further incubated for another 36 h. After cultivation, the cells were harvested by centrifugation at 18,400g for 10 min, washed twice with 100 mM potassium phosphate buffer (pH 7.4), and then prepared for subsequent purification and whole-cell catalysis experiments.

### 2.6. High-throughput screening method for UDCA and MDCA
The gene encoding 7β-HSDH ( A4ECA9 .1) from Collinsella aerofaciens was codon-optimized and synthesized by GenScript (Nanjing, China). The 7β-HSDH gene was subsequently subcloned into the C-terminal region of the OleP gene in plasmid pRSF-OleP-CamAB, with the incorporation of a ribosome binding site (RBS; GGATCCGAATTCGAGCTCATAAAAGGAGGAAAATAT), yielding the recombinant plasmid pRSF-OleP-CamAB-HSDH. After the whole-cell reaction, 50 μL of the reaction product was transferred to a 96-well microplate. Subsequently, 100 μL of a 2,4-dinitrophenylhydrazine solution (10 mM, dissolved in a 1:1 v/v mixture of dimethyl sulfoxide and ethanol) was added to the each well. The microplate was placed on a shaker and incubated at 25 °C with shaking at 800 rpm for 30 min. Following this, 100 μL of NaOH solution (4 M, dissolved in a 40% v/v ethanol aqueous solution) was added to the wells. Following the 30 min standing period at room temperature, the mixture was centrifuged at 18,400 g for 5 min to collect the supernatant. A 100 μL aliquot of the supernatant was transferred to a new 96-well microplate, and the absorbance at 440 nm was measured. Samples with high absorbance values were selected for further HPLC analysis.

### 2.7. Molecular docking and molecular dynamics (MD) simulations
The substrate LCA was docked into the active site of the OleP S240A crystal structure using Auto-Dock Vina in Chimera [ 23 ]. Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations of the mutant-substrate complex were performed to analyze the dynamic structural characteristics of the protein complexes. The protonation states of titratable residues (His, Glu, and Asp) were determined based on p K a values using PROPKA3.1 and through visual inspection of local H-bonding networks [ 24 , 25 ]. Histidine residues His 30, His 138, His 246, His 333, and His 354 were protonated at the δ position, while His 23, His 101, His 228, His 258, His 320, His 340, His 346, His 351, and His 355 were protonated at the ε position. Except for amino acids E89 and D91, which remain protonated, all other acidic residues are deprotonated. The substrate LCA was parameterized using the general AMBER Force Field (GAFF) [ 26 ], with atomic charge parameters fitted via the restrained electrostatic potential (RESP) method [ 27 ]. The RESP charges were calculated using Multiwfn software [ 28 , 29 ]. The force field parameters for Cpd I were sourced from a previous study [ 30 ]. The Amber ff14SB force field was employed for the protein residues [ 31 ]. The complex system was solvated in a 16 Å TIP3P water box, and Na + was added to the protein surface to neutralize the total charge of the system.

### 3.1. Screening of efficient P450 enzymes for converting LCA to MDCA and UDCA
According to previous reports, the three-component P450 OleP can catalyze the production of 6β-OH deoxycholic acid from deoxycholic acid, as well as the synthesis of MDCA from LCA. However, when whole-cell catalysts were prepared using E. coli C43(DE3) strain harboring the pET28a-oleP and pACYCDuet-camA-camB plasmids, the conversion rate of LCA to MDCA was merely 1.6% [ 33 ]. To achieve efficient conversion of LCA to MDCA and UDCA, several P450s were selected to investigate their hydroxylation activity towards LCA, including the self-sufficient P450 BM3, two-component P450 enzymes (CYP5150AP3, P450 cur , and P450 FE ), and other three-component P450 enzymes (CYP105A1, CYP105A3, CYP105AB3, CYP105D7, and CYP105P2).
An efficient redox partner Sm CPR I453V from Silybum marianum was employed to construct a whole-cell biocatalysis system for two-component P450s, while putidaredoxin reductase (CamA) and putidaredoxin (CamB) from Pseudomonas putida were used to establish the catalytic system for three-component P450s. The genes encoding theses redox partners were co-expressed with the corresponding P450 genes in the BL21(DE3) using a high-copy number plasmid pRSFDuet-1, resulting in ten recombinant E. coli strains harboring different P450s ( Fig. 1 A) . Subsequently, the hydroxylation activity toward LCA was evaluated using these whole-cell biocatalysts.

### 3.2. Improving a high-throughput screening method for detecting UDCA
Despite advances in P450-mediated steroid hydroxylation via protein engineering [ 9 , 17 ], low steroid detection efficiency limits the screening of a large P450 mutation library. We previously established a high-throughput MDCA detection method using 75% H 2 SO 4 –EtOH, which outperforms the traditional NAD(P)H consumption assay in accuracy ( Fig. 2 A) [ 11 ]. For UDCA, a colorimetric assay based on the water-soluble tetrazolium salt, 2-(4-iodophenyl)-3-(4-nitrophenyl)-5-(2,4-disulfophenyl)-2H-tetrazolium (WST-1)/1-methoxy-5-methylphenazinium methyl sulfate (mPMS) system has been established, which couples NADPH production (from 7β-hydroxysteroid dehydrogenase catalyzed UDCA oxidation) [ 9 ]. Here, we improved the high-throughput detection method for UDCA by coupling two reactions ( Fig. 2 A): oxidation of UDCA to 7-keto-lithocholic acid (7-KLCA) via a NADP + -dependent 7β-hydroxysteroid dehydrogenase from Collinsella aerofaciens [ 19 ], followed by 2,4-dinitrophenylhydrazine (DNPH)-based ketone detection for 7-KLCA [ 36 ].

### 3.3. Structural characteristics of the OleP-LCA complex
To provide structural information for the subsequent rational design of P450 OleP, the crystal structure of OleP was resolved. The purified OleP enzyme was obtained using a Hi-trap chelating HP column, gel filtration chromatography, and ion-exchange chromatography with a Mono Q column (GE Healthcare) ( Fig. S2A–S2D ). The purified OleP enzyme was crystallized by sitting-drop vapor-diffusion, producing OleP (PDB: 21LA ) and OleP-LCA (PDB: 21GU ) complex structures at 1.98 Å and 2.79 Å resolution ( Fig. 3 A, Fig. S2E, Fig. S3 and Table S4 ). The asymmetric unit contained two molecules, each adopting the canonical P450 fold with 14 α-helices, 15 β-strands ( Fig. S4 ), and a central heme group coordinated by a thiolate ligand. Within each asymmetric unit, two OleP molecules (designated as chains A and B) adopt nearly identical conformations (RMSD = 0.31 Å), exhibiting high similarity to the previously reported crystal structure of OleP (PDB: 4XE3 ) with an RMSD of 1.1 Å ( Fig. S5A ) [ 37 ]. Structural alignment using the Dali Server confirmed that OleP shares high similarity with P450OleP (PDB: 4XE3 , 8QYI , Z-score 61.4, 52.1, Fig. S5 ), including conserved catalytic motifs in the I- and L-helices. The iron in the heme group in the active site is axially coordinated with the conserved cysteine residue at position 356. The two propionate groups of the heme molecule form electrostatic interactions with two highly conserved histidine residues (at positions 101 and 354), two arginine residues (at positions 105 and 298), a leucine residue (at position 94), and a phenylalanine residue (at position 321).

### 3.4. Semi-rational design of OleP to enhance the synthesis of MDCA and UDCA
To identify key residues affecting OleP-catalyzed LCA hydroxylation, a comprehensive mutant library with 60 amino acid sites in the substrate cavity was generated. This included ten heme-interacting residues, twenty-one residues within 5 Å of the heme, nine residues contacting the substrate LCA, and twenty residues within 5 Å of LCA ( Fig. 4 A–B, and S8A-B). Alanine residues at positions 95, 244, 293, 348, 353, and 359 were mutated to glycine, while the remaining 54 sites were substituted with alanine. The catalytic activity of the OleP variant was assessed by whole-cell catalysis, high-throughput screening, and HPLC analysis. Thirteen OleP mutants showed enhanced MDCA synthesis compared to the wild type, including P88A, A95G, H101A, R105A, L160A, S240A, S249A, S292A, A293G, V297A, H351A, H355A, and A359G. Notably, A95G, S240A, and S292A increased MDCA conversion rate from 32.5% to 89.4%, 94.5%, and 81.2%, respectively ( Fig. 4 C). Additionally, three mutants, including F84A, P88A, and S240A, enabled the 7β-hydroxylation of LCA to UDCA, with conversion rates of 1.5%, 1.8%, and 1.1%, respectively ( Fig. 4 D). ### 3.5. Optimization of OleP mutants based on rational design for enhanced UDCA synthesis
Through the application of semi-rational design, we successfully identified key residues involved in LCA hydroxylation and obtained the S240A mutant, along with its crystal structure, as well as the UDCA-enhanced triple mutant F84Q/S240A/V291G. This semi-rational design approach proved highly effective in the initial phase, enabling the construction of a focused mutant library for screening key residues without necessitating complex calculations. With these experimental advancements, our research objective evolved from merely identifying key residues to the precise optimization of UDCA synthesis efficiency and regioselectivity. Recognizing the limitations of semi-rational design in achieving multi-site precision, we employed rational design methodologies, specifically GEMS and Rosetta, to further enhance UDCA synthesis [ 32 ]. Building on the triple mutant F84Q/S240A/V291G, GEMS integrated structural, sequence, and evolutionary data to predict high-quality mutants, thereby guiding virtual saturation mutagenesis of 18 active site residues while ensuring diversity. The activity of the mutants was predicted with the GEME approach, and the top variants were reranked based on LCA binding affinity calculated by Rosetta Cartesian ΔΔG ( Table S5 for ΔΔG, Table S6 for reranking results). From the top 150 GEMS-predicted variants, 31 variants were selected, considering the sequence diversity and active pocket volume ( Fig. 5 A). Additionally, Foldseek was used to identify homologous structures and guide further mutant designs based on sequence insertions or deletions ( Fig. 5 A). Of the 35 rationally designed mutants, three mutants, OleP F84Q/S240A/V291G/E89D , OleP F84Q/S240A/V291G/E89N , and OleP F84Q/S240A/V291G/V239I , showed improved UDCA conversion ( Fig. 5 B). Among the variants, OleP F84Q/S240A/V291G/E89N was the most effective, raising UDCA yield from 6.5% to 12.1% (160.9 μM UDCA from 1.33 mM LCA at 12 h; UDCA selectively:79.5%) ( Fig. S1 and Fig. 5 B).

### 4. Conclusion
In this study, P450 OleP was systematically evaluated for its capacity to hydroxylate LCA into high-value bile acid derivatives, MDCA and UDCA. An improved high-throughput detection method was established to efficiently facilitate the screening of mutants with enhanced UDCA synthesis activity, overcoming the limitations of traditional screening approaches. Guided by the crystal structure of the OleP-LCA complex, semi-rational design identified the S240A mutant, which achieved a high MDCA conversion rate of 98.3% (1.30 mM MDCA from 1.33 mM LCA at 12h). The crystal structure of the S240A mutant was further resolved to elucidate the structural basis underlying its enhanced catalytic activity. Subsequently, the quadruple mutant F84Q/S240A/V291G/E89 N was yielded through rational design, achieving a UDCA conversion rate of 12.1% (160.9 μM UDCA from 1.33 mM LCA at 12 h). Molecular dynamics simulations were employed to elucidate the catalytic mechanisms of both S240A (the distance between C6–H and the oxygen atom of Cpd I is more favorable for hydrogen abstraction) and the quadruple mutant F84Q/S240A/V291G/E89 N (active pocket remodeling), which contribute to their superior catalytic performance.

### Abstract
Cytochrome P450 enzymes are promising for synthesizing therapeutically beneficial bile acids murideoxycholic acid (MDCA) and ursodeoxycholic acid (UDCA) from lithocholic acid (LCA). However, the catalytic efficiency of P450 enzymes remains inadequate for large-scale production. Here, we address this limitation by engineering P450 OleP, achieving high activity for the efficient synthesis of MDCA and UDCA. Firstly, an improved high-throughput detection method for UDCA was developed to accelerate the screening process for mutants with improved UDCA synthesis capabilities. Next, the mutant S240A was generated through semi-rational design guided by the crystal structure of the OleP-LCA complex, resulting in an improvement in the MDCA conversion rate from 32.5% to 98.3% using 0.5 g L −1 of LCA as the substrate. Furthermore, the crystal structure of the S240A mutant was resolved and analyzed. Subsequently, the mutant F84Q/S240A/V291G/E89 N was developed through rational design, improving the UDCA conversion rate from 6.5% to 12.1% with 0.5 g L −1 of LCA as the substrate. Finally, molecular dynamics simulations were employed to elucidate the mechanisms underlying the enhanced catalytic activity of the S240A and F84Q/S240A/V291G/E89 N mutants. This study provides optimized P450 enzyme mutants for the synthesis of MDCA and UDCA and offers significant insights into the catalytic mechanisms underlying the production of hydroxylated, LCA-derived high-value-added compounds.

### 1. Introduction
Bile acids are a class of steroid compounds characterized by multiple hydroxyl groups. Derived from cholesterol metabolism in the liver, they play integral roles in the digestion and absorption of lipids and fat-soluble vitamins [ 1 ]. Furthermore, they act as pivotal signaling molecules regulating lipid, glucose, steroid, and energy metabolism throughout the body [ 2 ]. They also possess notable pharmacological properties and are employed in the treatment of hepatobiliary disorders and as anti-inflammatory agents [ 3 , 4 ]. Among them, MDCA is crucial for maintaining bile acid homeostasis and preventing gallstone formation [ 5 , 6 ]. UDCA, another important bile acid and a natural active component found in bear bile, is approved for therapeutic use in various liver and gallbladder diseases [ 7 ].
Currently, production methods for MDCA and UDCA face significant limitations. The conventional approach of extracting these compounds from animal livers is constrained by ethical considerations [ 8 ]. Chemoenzymatic synthesis, while popular, often involves multi-step reactions with low yields (e.g., 30% for UDCA from cholic acid) [ 9 ] and suffers from high energy consumption and waste generation [ 10 ]. Similarly, MDCA synthesis from hyodeoxycholic acid requires harsh conditions and hazardous reagents (e.g., hypochlorous acid), posing potential environmental risks [ 11 ]. With advancements in biotechnology and the development of green biomanufacturing, enzyme-based biosynthesis technologies for MDCA and UDCA have emerged, offering advantages in terms of environmental sustainability, efficiency, and safety. Currently, UDCA is predominantly produced via a two-step enzymatic process using 7α-/7β-hydroxysteroid dehydrogenases with chenodeoxycholic acid as the substrate [ 12 , 13 ]. Notably, chenodeoxycholic acid is utilized in medical treatment due to its strong ability to dissolve cholesterol gallstones [ 14 ].

### 2.3. Production conditions for the P450 candidates and purification for OleP
The successfully engineered recombinant E. coli strains, each harboring a candidate P450 enzyme and redox partners, were initially activated by culturing on LB agar plates supplemented with 50 μg mL −1 of kanamycin at 37 °C for 12 h. A single colony was picked and inoculated into 2 mL of LB broth containing 50 μg mL −1 of kanamycin, followed by incubation at 37 °C with shaking at 220 rpm for 12 h to prepare the seed culture. Subsequently, 1 mL of the seed culture was transferred into 250 mL shaking flasks containing 50 mL of TB medium supplemented with 50 μg mL −1 of kanamycin, and the cultures were incubated at 37 °C with shaking at 220 rpm. When the optical density at 600 nm (OD 600 ) reached 0.6–0.8 (usually after 3–5 h of incubation), the culture was cooled to 16 °C and supplemented with 0.4 mM ALA and 0.2 mM FeCl 3 . Production of P450s proteins was induced by adding 0.2 mM IPTG, and the culture was further incubated for another 36 h. After cultivation, the cells were harvested by centrifugation at 18,400g for 10 min, washed twice with 100 mM potassium phosphate buffer (pH 7.4), and then prepared for subsequent purification and whole-cell catalysis experiments.

### 2.6. High-throughput screening method for UDCA and MDCA
The gene encoding 7β-HSDH ( A4ECA9 .1) from Collinsella aerofaciens was codon-optimized and synthesized by GenScript (Nanjing, China). The 7β-HSDH gene was subsequently subcloned into the C-terminal region of the OleP gene in plasmid pRSF-OleP-CamAB, with the incorporation of a ribosome binding site (RBS; GGATCCGAATTCGAGCTCATAAAAGGAGGAAAATAT), yielding the recombinant plasmid pRSF-OleP-CamAB-HSDH. After the whole-cell reaction, 50 μL of the reaction product was transferred to a 96-well microplate. Subsequently, 100 μL of a 2,4-dinitrophenylhydrazine solution (10 mM, dissolved in a 1:1 v/v mixture of dimethyl sulfoxide and ethanol) was added to the each well. The microplate was placed on a shaker and incubated at 25 °C with shaking at 800 rpm for 30 min. Following this, 100 μL of NaOH solution (4 M, dissolved in a 40% v/v ethanol aqueous solution) was added to the wells. Following the 30 min standing period at room temperature, the mixture was centrifuged at 18,400 g for 5 min to collect the supernatant. A 100 μL aliquot of the supernatant was transferred to a new 96-well microplate, and the absorbance at 440 nm was measured. Samples with high absorbance values were selected for further HPLC analysis.

### 2.7. Molecular docking and molecular dynamics (MD) simulations
The substrate LCA was docked into the active site of the OleP S240A crystal structure using Auto-Dock Vina in Chimera [ 23 ]. Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations of the mutant-substrate complex were performed to analyze the dynamic structural characteristics of the protein complexes. The protonation states of titratable residues (His, Glu, and Asp) were determined based on p K a values using PROPKA3.1 and through visual inspection of local H-bonding networks [ 24 , 25 ]. Histidine residues His 30, His 138, His 246, His 333, and His 354 were protonated at the δ position, while His 23, His 101, His 228, His 258, His 320, His 340, His 346, His 351, and His 355 were protonated at the ε position. Except for amino acids E89 and D91, which remain protonated, all other acidic residues are deprotonated. The substrate LCA was parameterized using the general AMBER Force Field (GAFF) [ 26 ], with atomic charge parameters fitted via the restrained electrostatic potential (RESP) method [ 27 ]. The RESP charges were calculated using Multiwfn software [ 28 , 29 ]. The force field parameters for Cpd I were sourced from a previous study [ 30 ]. The Amber ff14SB force field was employed for the protein residues [ 31 ]. The complex system was solvated in a 16 Å TIP3P water box, and Na + was added to the protein surface to neutralize the total charge of the system.

### 3.1. Screening of efficient P450 enzymes for converting LCA to MDCA and UDCA
According to previous reports, the three-component P450 OleP can catalyze the production of 6β-OH deoxycholic acid from deoxycholic acid, as well as the synthesis of MDCA from LCA. However, when whole-cell catalysts were prepared using E. coli C43(DE3) strain harboring the pET28a-oleP and pACYCDuet-camA-camB plasmids, the conversion rate of LCA to MDCA was merely 1.6% [ 33 ]. To achieve efficient conversion of LCA to MDCA and UDCA, several P450s were selected to investigate their hydroxylation activity towards LCA, including the self-sufficient P450 BM3, two-component P450 enzymes (CYP5150AP3, P450 cur , and P450 FE ), and other three-component P450 enzymes (CYP105A1, CYP105A3, CYP105AB3, CYP105D7, and CYP105P2).
An efficient redox partner Sm CPR I453V from Silybum marianum was employed to construct a whole-cell biocatalysis system for two-component P450s, while putidaredoxin reductase (CamA) and putidaredoxin (CamB) from Pseudomonas putida were used to establish the catalytic system for three-component P450s. The genes encoding theses redox partners were co-expressed with the corresponding P450 genes in the BL21(DE3) using a high-copy number plasmid pRSFDuet-1, resulting in ten recombinant E. coli strains harboring different P450s ( Fig. 1 A) . Subsequently, the hydroxylation activity toward LCA was evaluated using these whole-cell biocatalysts.

### 3.2. Improving a high-throughput screening method for detecting UDCA
Despite advances in P450-mediated steroid hydroxylation via protein engineering [ 9 , 17 ], low steroid detection efficiency limits the screening of a large P450 mutation library. We previously established a high-throughput MDCA detection method using 75% H 2 SO 4 –EtOH, which outperforms the traditional NAD(P)H consumption assay in accuracy ( Fig. 2 A) [ 11 ]. For UDCA, a colorimetric assay based on the water-soluble tetrazolium salt, 2-(4-iodophenyl)-3-(4-nitrophenyl)-5-(2,4-disulfophenyl)-2H-tetrazolium (WST-1)/1-methoxy-5-methylphenazinium methyl sulfate (mPMS) system has been established, which couples NADPH production (from 7β-hydroxysteroid dehydrogenase catalyzed UDCA oxidation) [ 9 ]. Here, we improved the high-throughput detection method for UDCA by coupling two reactions ( Fig. 2 A): oxidation of UDCA to 7-keto-lithocholic acid (7-KLCA) via a NADP + -dependent 7β-hydroxysteroid dehydrogenase from Collinsella aerofaciens [ 19 ], followed by 2,4-dinitrophenylhydrazine (DNPH)-based ketone detection for 7-KLCA [ 36 ].

### 3.3. Structural characteristics of the OleP-LCA complex
To provide structural information for the subsequent rational design of P450 OleP, the crystal structure of OleP was resolved. The purified OleP enzyme was obtained using a Hi-trap chelating HP column, gel filtration chromatography, and ion-exchange chromatography with a Mono Q column (GE Healthcare) ( Fig. S2A–S2D ). The purified OleP enzyme was crystallized by sitting-drop vapor-diffusion, producing OleP (PDB: 21LA ) and OleP-LCA (PDB: 21GU ) complex structures at 1.98 Å and 2.79 Å resolution ( Fig. 3 A, Fig. S2E, Fig. S3 and Table S4 ). The asymmetric unit contained two molecules, each adopting the canonical P450 fold with 14 α-helices, 15 β-strands ( Fig. S4 ), and a central heme group coordinated by a thiolate ligand. Within each asymmetric unit, two OleP molecules (designated as chains A and B) adopt nearly identical conformations (RMSD = 0.31 Å), exhibiting high similarity to the previously reported crystal structure of OleP (PDB: 4XE3 ) with an RMSD of 1.1 Å ( Fig. S5A ) [ 37 ]. Structural alignment using the Dali Server confirmed that OleP shares high similarity with P450OleP (PDB: 4XE3 , 8QYI , Z-score 61.4, 52.1, Fig. S5 ), including conserved catalytic motifs in the I- and L-helices. The iron in the heme group in the active site is axially coordinated with the conserved cysteine residue at position 356. The two propionate groups of the heme molecule form electrostatic interactions with two highly conserved histidine residues (at positions 101 and 354), two arginine residues (at positions 105 and 298), a leucine residue (at position 94), and a phenylalanine residue (at position 321).

### 3.4. Semi-rational design of OleP to enhance the synthesis of MDCA and UDCA
To identify key residues affecting OleP-catalyzed LCA hydroxylation, a comprehensive mutant library with 60 amino acid sites in the substrate cavity was generated. This included ten heme-interacting residues, twenty-one residues within 5 Å of the heme, nine residues contacting the substrate LCA, and twenty residues within 5 Å of LCA ( Fig. 4 A–B, and S8A-B). Alanine residues at positions 95, 244, 293, 348, 353, and 359 were mutated to glycine, while the remaining 54 sites were substituted with alanine. The catalytic activity of the OleP variant was assessed by whole-cell catalysis, high-throughput screening, and HPLC analysis. Thirteen OleP mutants showed enhanced MDCA synthesis compared to the wild type, including P88A, A95G, H101A, R105A, L160A, S240A, S249A, S292A, A293G, V297A, H351A, H355A, and A359G. Notably, A95G, S240A, and S292A increased MDCA conversion rate from 32.5% to 89.4%, 94.5%, and 81.2%, respectively ( Fig. 4 C). ### 3.5. Optimization of OleP mutants based on rational design for enhanced UDCA synthesis
Through the application of semi-rational design, we successfully identified key residues involved in LCA hydroxylation and obtained the S240A mutant, along with its crystal structure, as well as the UDCA-enhanced triple mutant F84Q/S240A/V291G. This semi-rational design approach proved highly effective in the initial phase, enabling the construction of a focused mutant library for screening key residues without necessitating complex calculations. With these experimental advancements, our research objective evolved from merely identifying key residues to the precise optimization of UDCA synthesis efficiency and regioselectivity. Recognizing the limitations of semi-rational design in achieving multi-site precision, we employed rational design methodologies, specifically GEMS and Rosetta, to further enhance UDCA synthesis [ 32 ]. Building on the triple mutant F84Q/S240A/V291G, GEMS integrated structural, sequence, and evolutionary data to predict high-quality mutants, thereby guiding virtual saturation mutagenesis of 18 active site residues while ensuring diversity. The activity of the mutants was predicted with the GEME approach, and the top variants were reranked based on LCA binding affinity calculated by Rosetta Cartesian ΔΔG ( Table S5 for ΔΔG, Table S6 for reranking results). From the top 150 GEMS-predicted variants, 31 variants were selected, considering the sequence diversity and active pocket volume ( Fig. 5 A). Additionally, Foldseek was used to identify homologous structures and guide further mutant designs based on sequence insertions or deletions ( Fig. 5 A). Of the 35 rationally designed mutants, three mutants, OleP F84Q/S240A/V291G/E89D , OleP F84Q/S240A/V291G/E89N , and OleP F84Q/S240A/V291G/V239I , showed improved UDCA conversion ( Fig. 5 B). Among the variants, OleP F84Q/S240A/V291G/E89N was the most effective, raising UDCA yield from 6.5% to 12.1% (160.9 μM UDCA from 1.33 mM LCA at 12 h; UDCA selectively:79.5%) ( Fig. S1 and Fig. 5 B).

### 4. Conclusion
In this study, P450 OleP was systematically evaluated for its capacity to hydroxylate LCA into high-value bile acid derivatives, MDCA and UDCA. An improved high-throughput detection method was established to efficiently facilitate the screening of mutants with enhanced UDCA synthesis activity, overcoming the limitations of traditional screening approaches. Guided by the crystal structure of the OleP-LCA complex, semi-rational design identified the S240A mutant, which achieved a high MDCA conversion rate of 98.3% (1.30 mM MDCA from 1.33 mM LCA at 12h). The crystal structure of the S240A mutant was further resolved to elucidate the structural basis underlying its enhanced catalytic activity. Subsequently, the quadruple mutant F84Q/S240A/V291G/E89 N was yielded through rational design, achieving a UDCA conversion rate of 12.1% (160.9 μM UDCA from 1.33 mM LCA at 12 h). Molecular dynamics simulations were employed to elucidate the catalytic mechanisms of both S240A (the distance between C6–H and the oxygen atom of Cpd I is more favorable for hydrogen abstraction) and the quadruple mutant F84Q/S240A/V291G/E89 N (active pocket remodeling), which contribute to their superior catalytic performance.

### Abstract
Cytochrome P450 enzymes are promising for synthesizing therapeutically beneficial bile acids murideoxycholic acid (MDCA) and ursodeoxycholic acid (UDCA) from lithocholic acid (LCA). However, the catalytic efficiency of P450 enzymes remains inadequate for large-scale production. Here, we address this limitation by engineering P450 OleP, achieving high activity for the efficient synthesis of MDCA and UDCA. Firstly, an improved high-throughput detection method for UDCA was developed to accelerate the screening process for mutants with improved UDCA synthesis capabilities. Next, the mutant S240A was generated through semi-rational design guided by the crystal structure of the OleP-LCA complex, resulting in an improvement in the MDCA conversion rate from 32.5% to 98.3% using 0.5 g L −1 of LCA as the substrate. Furthermore, the crystal structure of the S240A mutant was resolved and analyzed. Subsequently, the mutant F84Q/S240A/V291G/E89 N was developed through rational design, improving the UDCA conversion rate from 6.5% to 12.1% with 0.5 g L −1 of LCA as the substrate. Finally, molecular dynamics simulations were employed to elucidate the mechanisms underlying the enhanced catalytic activity of the S240A and F84Q/S240A/V291G/E89 N mutants. This study provides optimized P450 enzyme mutants for the synthesis of MDCA and UDCA and offers significant insights into the catalytic mechanisms underlying the production of hydroxylated, LCA-derived high-value-added compounds.

### 1. Introduction
Bile acids are a class of steroid compounds characterized by multiple hydroxyl groups. Derived from cholesterol metabolism in the liver, they play integral roles in the digestion and absorption of lipids and fat-soluble vitamins [ 1 ]. Furthermore, they act as pivotal signaling molecules regulating lipid, glucose, steroid, and energy metabolism throughout the body [ 2 ]. They also possess notable pharmacological properties and are employed in the treatment of hepatobiliary disorders and as anti-inflammatory agents [ 3 , 4 ]. Among them, MDCA is crucial for maintaining bile acid homeostasis and preventing gallstone formation [ 5 , 6 ]. UDCA, another important bile acid and a natural active component found in bear bile, is approved for therapeutic use in various liver and gallbladder diseases [ 7 ].
Currently, production methods for MDCA and UDCA face significant limitations. The conventional approach of extracting these compounds from animal livers is constrained by ethical considerations [ 8 ]. Chemoenzymatic synthesis, while popular, often involves multi-step reactions with low yields (e.g., 30% for UDCA from cholic acid) [ 9 ] and suffers from high energy consumption and waste generation [ 10 ]. Similarly, MDCA synthesis from hyodeoxycholic acid requires harsh conditions and hazardous reagents (e.g., hypochlorous acid), posing potential environmental risks [ 11 ]. With advancements in biotechnology and the development of green biomanufacturing, enzyme-based biosynthesis technologies for MDCA and UDCA have emerged, offering advantages in terms of environmental sustainability, efficiency, and safety. Currently, UDCA is predominantly produced via a two-step enzymatic process using 7α-/7β-hydroxysteroid dehydrogenases with chenodeoxycholic acid as the substrate [ 12 , 13 ]. Notably, chenodeoxycholic acid is utilized in medical treatment due to its strong ability to dissolve cholesterol gallstones [ 14 ].

### 2.3. Production conditions for the P450 candidates and purification for OleP
The successfully engineered recombinant E. coli strains, each harboring a candidate P450 enzyme and redox partners, were initially activated by culturing on LB agar plates supplemented with 50 μg mL −1 of kanamycin at 37 °C for 12 h. A single colony was picked and inoculated into 2 mL of LB broth containing 50 μg mL −1 of kanamycin, followed by incubation at 37 °C with shaking at 220 rpm for 12 h to prepare the seed culture. Subsequently, 1 mL of the seed culture was transferred into 250 mL shaking flasks containing 50 mL of TB medium supplemented with 50 μg mL −1 of kanamycin, and the cultures were incubated at 37 °C with shaking at 220 rpm. When the optical density at 600 nm (OD 600 ) reached 0.6–0.8 (usually after 3–5 h of incubation), the culture was cooled to 16 °C and supplemented with 0.4 mM ALA and 0.2 mM FeCl 3 . Production of P450s proteins was induced by adding 0.2 mM IPTG, and the culture was further incubated for another 36 h. After cultivation, the cells were harvested by centrifugation at 18,400g for 10 min, washed twice with 100 mM potassium phosphate buffer (pH 7.4), and then prepared for subsequent purification and whole-cell catalysis experiments.

### 2.6. High-throughput screening method for UDCA and MDCA
The gene encoding 7β-HSDH ( A4ECA9 .1) from Collinsella aerofaciens was codon-optimized and synthesized by GenScript (Nanjing, China). The 7β-HSDH gene was subsequently subcloned into the C-terminal region of the OleP gene in plasmid pRSF-OleP-CamAB, with the incorporation of a ribosome binding site (RBS; GGATCCGAATTCGAGCTCATAAAAGGAGGAAAATAT), yielding the recombinant plasmid pRSF-OleP-CamAB-HSDH. After the whole-cell reaction, 50 μL of the reaction product was transferred to a 96-well microplate. Subsequently, 100 μL of a 2,4-dinitrophenylhydrazine solution (10 mM, dissolved in a 1:1 v/v mixture of dimethyl sulfoxide and ethanol) was added to the each well. The microplate was placed on a shaker and incubated at 25 °C with shaking at 800 rpm for 30 min. Following this, 100 μL of NaOH solution (4 M, dissolved in a 40% v/v ethanol aqueous solution) was added to the wells. Following the 30 min standing period at room temperature, the mixture was centrifuged at 18,400 g for 5 min to collect the supernatant. A 100 μL aliquot of the supernatant was transferred to a new 96-well microplate, and the absorbance at 440 nm was measured. Samples with high absorbance values were selected for further HPLC analysis.

### 2.7. Molecular docking and molecular dynamics (MD) simulations
The substrate LCA was docked into the active site of the OleP S240A crystal structure using Auto-Dock Vina in Chimera [ 23 ]. Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations of the mutant-substrate complex were performed to analyze the dynamic structural characteristics of the protein complexes. The protonation states of titratable residues (His, Glu, and Asp) were determined based on p K a values using PROPKA3.1 and through visual inspection of local H-bonding networks [ 24 , 25 ]. Histidine residues His 30, His 138, His 246, His 333, and His 354 were protonated at the δ position, while His 23, His 101, His 228, His 258, His 320, His 340, His 346, His 351, and His 355 were protonated at the ε position. Except for amino acids E89 and D91, which remain protonated, all other acidic residues are deprotonated. The substrate LCA was parameterized using the general AMBER Force Field (GAFF) [ 26 ], with atomic charge parameters fitted via the restrained electrostatic potential (RESP) method [ 27 ]. The RESP charges were calculated using Multiwfn software [ 28 , 29 ]. The force field parameters for Cpd I were sourced from a previous study [ 30 ]. The Amber ff14SB force field was employed for the protein residues [ 31 ]. The complex system was solvated in a 16 Å TIP3P water box, and Na + was added to the protein surface to neutralize the total charge of the system.

### 3.1. Screening of efficient P450 enzymes for converting LCA to MDCA and UDCA
According to previous reports, the three-component P450 OleP can catalyze the production of 6β-OH deoxycholic acid from deoxycholic acid, as well as the synthesis of MDCA from LCA. However, when whole-cell catalysts were prepared using E. coli C43(DE3) strain harboring the pET28a-oleP and pACYCDuet-camA-camB plasmids, the conversion rate of LCA to MDCA was merely 1.6% [ 33 ]. To achieve efficient conversion of LCA to MDCA and UDCA, several P450s were selected to investigate their hydroxylation activity towards LCA, including the self-sufficient P450 BM3, two-component P450 enzymes (CYP5150AP3, P450 cur , and P450 FE ), and other three-component P450 enzymes (CYP105A1, CYP105A3, CYP105AB3, CYP105D7, and CYP105P2).
An efficient redox partner Sm CPR I453V from Silybum marianum was employed to construct a whole-cell biocatalysis system for two-component P450s, while putidaredoxin reductase (CamA) and putidaredoxin (CamB) from Pseudomonas putida were used to establish the catalytic system for three-component P450s. The genes encoding theses redox partners were co-expressed with the corresponding P450 genes in the BL21(DE3) using a high-copy number plasmid pRSFDuet-1, resulting in ten recombinant E. coli strains harboring different P450s ( Fig. 1 A) . Subsequently, the hydroxylation activity toward LCA was evaluated using these whole-cell biocatalysts.

### 3.2. Improving a high-throughput screening method for detecting UDCA
Despite advances in P450-mediated steroid hydroxylation via protein engineering [ 9 , 17 ], low steroid detection efficiency limits the screening of a large P450 mutation library. We previously established a high-throughput MDCA detection method using 75% H 2 SO 4 –EtOH, which outperforms the traditional NAD(P)H consumption assay in accuracy ( Fig. 2 A) [ 11 ]. For UDCA, a colorimetric assay based on the water-soluble tetrazolium salt, 2-(4-iodophenyl)-3-(4-nitrophenyl)-5-(2,4-disulfophenyl)-2H-tetrazolium (WST-1)/1-methoxy-5-methylphenazinium methyl sulfate (mPMS) system has been established, which couples NADPH production (from 7β-hydroxysteroid dehydrogenase catalyzed UDCA oxidation) [ 9 ]. Here, we improved the high-throughput detection method for UDCA by coupling two reactions ( Fig. 2 A): oxidation of UDCA to 7-keto-lithocholic acid (7-KLCA) via a NADP + -dependent 7β-hydroxysteroid dehydrogenase from Collinsella aerofaciens [ 19 ], followed by 2,4-dinitrophenylhydrazine (DNPH)-based ketone detection for 7-KLCA [ 36 ].

### 3.3. Structural characteristics of the OleP-LCA complex
To provide structural information for the subsequent rational design of P450 OleP, the crystal structure of OleP was resolved. The purified OleP enzyme was obtained using a Hi-trap chelating HP column, gel filtration chromatography, and ion-exchange chromatography with a Mono Q column (GE Healthcare) ( Fig. S2A–S2D ). The purified OleP enzyme was crystallized by sitting-drop vapor-diffusion, producing OleP (PDB: 21LA ) and OleP-LCA (PDB: 21GU ) complex structures at 1.98 Å and 2.79 Å resolution ( Fig. 3 A, Fig. S2E, Fig. S3 and Table S4 ). The asymmetric unit contained two molecules, each adopting the canonical P450 fold with 14 α-helices, 15 β-strands ( Fig. S4 ), and a central heme group coordinated by a thiolate ligand. Within each asymmetric unit, two OleP molecules (designated as chains A and B) adopt nearly identical conformations (RMSD = 0.31 Å), exhibiting high similarity to the previously reported crystal structure of OleP (PDB: 4XE3 ) with an RMSD of 1.1 Å ( Fig. S5A ) [ 37 ]. Structural alignment using the Dali Server confirmed that OleP shares high similarity with P450OleP (PDB: 4XE3 , 8QYI , Z-score 61.4, 52.1, Fig. S5 ), including conserved catalytic motifs in the I- and L-helices. The iron in the heme group in the active site is axially coordinated with the conserved cysteine residue at position 356. The two propionate groups of the heme molecule form electrostatic interactions with two highly conserved histidine residues (at positions 101 and 354), two arginine residues (at positions 105 and 298), a leucine residue (at position 94), and a phenylalanine residue (at position 321).

### 3.4. Semi-rational design of OleP to enhance the synthesis of MDCA and UDCA
To identify key residues affecting OleP-catalyzed LCA hydroxylation, a comprehensive mutant library with 60 amino acid sites in the substrate cavity was generated. This included ten heme-interacting residues, twenty-one residues within 5 Å of the heme, nine residues contacting the substrate LCA, and twenty residues within 5 Å of LCA ( Fig. 4 A–B, and S8A-B). Alanine residues at positions 95, 244, 293, 348, 353, and 359 were mutated to glycine, while the remaining 54 sites were substituted with alanine. The catalytic activity of the OleP variant was assessed by whole-cell catalysis, high-throughput screening, and HPLC analysis. Thirteen OleP mutants showed enhanced MDCA synthesis compared to the wild type, including P88A, A95G, H101A, R105A, L160A, S240A, S249A, S292A, A293G, V297A, H351A, H355A, and A359G. Notably, A95G, S240A, and S292A increased MDCA conversion rate from 32.5% to 89.4%, 94.5%, and 81.2%, respectively ( Fig. 4 C). Additionally, three mutants, including F84A, P88A, and S240A, enabled the 7β-hydroxylation of LCA to UDCA, with conversion rates of 1.5%, 1.8%, and 1.1%, respectively ( Fig. 4 D). ### 3.5. Optimization of OleP mutants based on rational design for enhanced UDCA synthesis
Through the application of semi-rational design, we successfully identified key residues involved in LCA hydroxylation and obtained the S240A mutant, along with its crystal structure, as well as the UDCA-enhanced triple mutant F84Q/S240A/V291G. This semi-rational design approach proved highly effective in the initial phase, enabling the construction of a focused mutant library for screening key residues without necessitating complex calculations. With these experimental advancements, our research objective evolved from merely identifying key residues to the precise optimization of UDCA synthesis efficiency and regioselectivity. Recognizing the limitations of semi-rational design in achieving multi-site precision, we employed rational design methodologies, specifically GEMS and Rosetta, to further enhance UDCA synthesis [ 32 ]. Building on the triple mutant F84Q/S240A/V291G, GEMS integrated structural, sequence, and evolutionary data to predict high-quality mutants, thereby guiding virtual saturation mutagenesis of 18 active site residues while ensuring diversity. The activity of the mutants was predicted with the GEME approach, and the top variants were reranked based on LCA binding affinity calculated by Rosetta Cartesian ΔΔG ( Table S5 for ΔΔG, Table S6 for reranking results). From the top 150 GEMS-predicted variants, 31 variants were selected, considering the sequence diversity and active pocket volume ( Fig. 5 A). Additionally, Foldseek was used to identify homologous structures and guide further mutant designs based on sequence insertions or deletions ( Fig. 5 A). Of the 35 rationally designed mutants, three mutants, OleP F84Q/S240A/V291G/E89D , OleP F84Q/S240A/V291G/E89N , and OleP F84Q/S240A/V291G/V239I , showed improved UDCA conversion ( Fig. 5 B). Among the variants, OleP F84Q/S240A/V291G/E89N was the most effective, raising UDCA yield from 6.5% to 12.1% (160.9 μM UDCA from 1.33 mM LCA at 12 h; UDCA selectively:79.5%) ( Fig. S1 and Fig. 5 B).

### 4. Conclusion
In this study, P450 OleP was systematically evaluated for its capacity to hydroxylate LCA into high-value bile acid derivatives, MDCA and UDCA. An improved high-throughput detection method was established to efficiently facilitate the screening of mutants with enhanced UDCA synthesis activity, overcoming the limitations of traditional screening approaches. Guided by the crystal structure of the OleP-LCA complex, semi-rational design identified the S240A mutant, which achieved a high MDCA conversion rate of 98.3% (1.30 mM MDCA from 1.33 mM LCA at 12h). The crystal structure of the S240A mutant was further resolved to elucidate the structural basis underlying its enhanced catalytic activity. Subsequently, the quadruple mutant F84Q/S240A/V291G/E89 N was yielded through rational design, achieving a UDCA conversion rate of 12.1% (160.9 μM UDCA from 1.33 mM LCA at 12 h). Molecular dynamics simulations were employed to elucidate the catalytic mechanisms of both S240A (the distance between C6–H and the oxygen atom of Cpd I is more favorable for hydrogen abstraction) and the quadruple mutant F84Q/S240A/V291G/E89 N (active pocket remodeling), which contribute to their superior catalytic performance.

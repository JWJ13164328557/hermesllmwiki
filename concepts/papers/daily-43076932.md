# Rewiring versus optimization: how to connect the two philosophies of improving photorespiration?

## 论文信息
- **期刊**: Planta (2026)
- **PMID**: [43076932](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/43076932/)
- **DOI**: [10.1007/s00425-026-05228-z](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00425-026-05228-z)
- **第一作者**: Stefan Timm
- **作者**: Stefan Timm, Martin Hagemann, Amanda P Cavanagh, Wei Huang

## 摘要
Synthetic bypasses as well as optimized expression of native photorespiratory enzymes improve plant performance through overlapping mechanistic principles, yet their full benefits emerge from similar and distinct features such as relief of metabolic constraints and energetic savings. The long-standing view on photorespiration as a wasteful and dispensable evolutionary relic has shifted substantially in recent years. Photorespiration is now recognized as an essential metabolic process that not only recycles 2-phosphoglycolate (2PG) to sustain oxygenic photosynthesis, but also impacts on metabolic homeostasis, redox balance, environmental acclimation, and stomatal regulation. Nevertheless, its associated CO2 loss, energetic costs, and metabolic constraints have motivated extensive efforts to improve plant productivity through genetic engineering of photorespiration. Two principal strategies have emerged: (i) introducing synthetic bypasses that reroute photorespiratory flux, particularly inside the chloroplast, and (ii) optimizing the native pathway by engineering expression of key enzymes such as 2PG phosphatase (PGLP1) and glycine decarboxylase (GDC). Although these approaches differ fundamentally in design, they produce overlapping physiological and metabolic outcomes, suggesting a partially shared mechanistic basis. One possibility is that both alleviate negative feedback from photorespiratory intermediates on carbon fixation and downstream carbon utilization. However, important differences between the strategies point to potential trade-offs in energy balance and additional, unresolved effects. Here, we propose an integrative framework in which synthetic rerouting and optimization of the native pathway are viewed not as competing strategies, but as complementary approaches. We evaluate their respective advantages and limitations and discuss how combining their strengths could contribute to the development of crops with improved performance under future climates.

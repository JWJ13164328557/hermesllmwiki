# Sublethal insecticide exposure and inbreeding impose a developmental bottleneck but enhance crop-damaging traits in surviving Colorado potato beetles.

## 论文信息

- **PMID**: [42979627](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42979627/)
- **DOI**: [10.1098/rsos.261014](https://doi.org/10.1098/rsos.261014)
- **第一作者**: Nijat Nariman
- **作者**: Nijat Nariman, Himrekha Agarwal, Shuqing Xu

## 摘要
Intensive agricultural insecticide use often fails to eradicate pest populations completely, leaving small remnant populations exposed to persistent sublethal residues and potentially elevated inbreeding. How such remnant populations persist remains poorly understood. Here, we examined how inbreeding and sublethal imidacloprid exposure shape survival and post-emergence performance in the Colorado potato beetle (Leptinotarsa decemlineata), a major agricultural pest. We compared four groups differing in breeding status and insecticide exposure history, and quantified emergence rate, locomotor activity, feeding behaviour and reproductive output. We found that despite the insecticide dosage being sublethal, both chemical exposure and inbreeding imposed severe developmental costs, reducing the adult emergence rate with no significant interaction effects. However, among the adults, we observed trait-specific phenotypic plasticity. Male locomotor activity was reduced by both stressors, while female activity remained stable. Notably, herbivory significantly increased under both inbreeding and sublethal insecticide exposure. Furthermore, while inbreeding severely reduced both egg production and hatching success, sublethal exposure to the insecticide surprisingly increased hatching success in surviving pairs. These results demonstrate that sublethal insecticide exposure and inbreeding can create a strong developmental bottleneck. Yet, surviving individuals can exhibit compensatory plasticity that maintains or even enhances their capacity for crop damage.

### 核心发现
1. Taken together, our results suggest that outbreeding is likely to be especially important for persistence after insecticide-driven bottlenecks because it reduces the fitness costs imposed by low genetic quality during development and reproduction.
2. We found that despite the insecticide dosage being sublethal, both chemical exposure and inbreeding imposed severe developmental costs, reducing the adult emergence rate with no significant interaction effects.
3. Because all experimental groups in this study were derived from the same laboratory population and reared under identical conditions for 12 generations prior to the experiment, any population-of-origin effects are held constant across treatments and cannot explain the inbreeding- or insecticide-driven differences we report.
4. We found no evidence that parental activity or feeding significantly predicted either reproductive trait ( p = 0.4–0.9).
5. In that sense, our results suggest that remnant beetles may persist not only because some individuals survive the initial bottleneck, but also because those survivors can adjust feeding and retain substantial reproductive capacity under continued stress.
6. From an agricultural perspective, our results suggest that insecticide application may achieve short-term demographic suppression without delivering equally strong longer-term control.
7. More broadly, our results suggest that the consequences of insecticide exposure may depend not only on how many individuals are removed, but also on which individuals survive and what they are able to do afterwards.
8. Although our experiment was not designed to identify mechanism, one possibility is that inbreeding and sublethal insecticide exposure affect overlapping physiological pathways involved in neural signalling, detoxification or general stress tolerance rather than acting through a single shared target site [,, ].

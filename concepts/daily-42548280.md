Rice (Oryza sativaL.) cultivation presents a significant opportunity to reduce methane and nitrous oxide fluxes by adopting optimal irrigation strategies and smart N fertilization. A 3‐year field study was conducted in Northeast Arkansas to evaluate the impacts of flooded and furrow irrigation with either urea or sulfur‐enriched urea (Urea‐S) at 168 kg N ha−1on rice yield and soil trace gas fluxes. Gas samples were collected using the flux‐vented chamber method and analyzed via gas chromatography. Furrow irrigation reduced CH4fluxes by 90%–100%, but increased N2O fluxes by up to 86% relative to flooded irrigation. Urea‐S reduced N2O emissions under certain moisture conditions by 67%. Grain yields were >10 Mg ha−1across all treatments and were unaffected by irrigation and N fertilizer types. Results highlight the relevance of aligning irrigation strategy with N fertilizer type to successfully reduce N2O fluxes. Flooded rice fields use large amounts of water and are a major source of methane, a soil trace gas that contributes to climate change. This study evaluated whether furrow irrigation, a water‐saving production system, can reduce trace gas fluxes while maintaining rice yields. Across three growing seasons, furrow irrigation greatly reduced methane fluxes compared with flooded fields and produced similar grain yields. However, nitrous oxide fluxes increased when soils dried and were later rewetted, especially after fertilizer applications. Using sulfur‐enriched nitrogen fertilizer reduced nitrous oxide fluxes under stable soil moisture conditions, but results varied depending on seasonal rainfall. These findings show that furrow irrigation can help farmers reduce water use and climate impacts of rice production, but successful adoption requires careful fertilizer timing and soil moisture management to avoid increased nitrous oxide fluxes. Rice (Oryza sativaL.) is a major staple crop, supplying more than 20% of global caloric intake and supporting over half of the world's population. Despite its importance, rice cultivation has a disproportionate environmental footprint because it emits methane (CH4) and nitrous oxide (N2O), two potent trace gases  (IPCC,2021; Linquist et al.,2012). Traditional continuously flooded rice systems create anaerobic soil conditions that favor methanogenesis, making rice production responsible for approximately 11% of global anthropogenic CH4fluxes (Saunois et al.,2020; Yan et al.,2009).

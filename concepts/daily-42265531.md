Keywords:evolutionary trade‐offs, omega‐3 fatty acids, oxylipins, plant defense, plant–insect interactions Plants confronted with insect herbivory face a problem of timing. While signaling pathways can be activated within minutes, the accumulation of many classical defense metabolites takes considerably longer. How, then, do plants gain precious time during the earliest moments of attack? In an article published in the issue ofNew Phytologist, Jimenez‐Alemanet al. (2026; pp. 1823–1840) provide a striking answer. By dissecting omega‐3 polyunsaturated fatty acid (n3‐PUFA) metabolism in the liverwortMarchantia polymorpha, they reveal that plants deploy a dual defense strategy: they convert nutritionally essential fatty acids into oxylipins, simultaneously depriving herbivores of key dietary components and activating immune signaling (Fig.1). …larvae feeding on the fully JA‐deficient fad3 fad7 Marchantia plants do not show improved performance. Instead, their growth mirrors that observed on wild‐type (WT) plants… This work challenges a long‐standing assumption in plant biology that impaired jasmonate (JA) biosynthesis should inevitably benefit chewing herbivores. Instead, it offers a compelling evolutionary rationale for why JAs are synthesized almost exclusively from n3‐PUFAs rather than their omega‐6 counterparts, with JA biosynthesis and other n3‐derived oxylipins likely evolving later as mechanisms to recycle and amplify these initial metabolic perturbations. Plants produce omega‐3 polyunsaturated fatty acids (n3‐PUFAs), which are of high nutritional value to insect caterpillars and sustain their growth. Upon herbivory attack, plants quickly catabolize n3‐PUFAs and convert them into potent jasmonate defense hormones, thus depleting essential nutrients and activating chemical defense pathways with a single metabolic move. Their biosynthesis is remarkably conserved, including that JAs derive almost exclusively from n3‐PUFAs, such as α‐linolenic acid (18:3n3), despite the structural similarity and availability of n6‐PUFAs (Wasternack & Song,2017; Chiniet al.,2023). The evolutionary logic of this substrate specificity has remained puzzling.

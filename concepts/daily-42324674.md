Nitrogen level significantly impacts rice resistance to the brown planthopper (BPH,Nilaparvata lugens). Yet, the mechanisms underlying nitrogen level‐mediated rice BPH resistance remain largely unclear. Here, we found that, relative to medium nitrogen (MN) and high nitrogen (HN) conditions, low nitrogen (LN) suppressed rice growth but enhanced plant resistance to BPH, as evidenced by reduced nymph biomass, decreased phloem ingestion and lower fecundity. Biochemical and transcriptome analyses revealed that compared to MN and HN plants, LN plants exhibited significantly higher basal and/or BPH‐induced levels of abscisic acid (ABA) and salicylic acid (SA), cell wall components (lignin, cellulose and hemicellulose), and certain flavonoids but lower levels of phenolamides. Moreover, the relative water content (RWC) and total amino acid concentrations were significantly lower in LN plants compared with those in MN and HN plants. Impairing the signalling pathway mediated by ABA but not SA in rice abolished the basal and/or BPH‐induced accumulation of defence compounds (cell wall components and certain flavonoids) in plants and plant resistance to BPH. The results demonstrate that the ABA signalling pathway, together with reduced RWC and total amino acid concentrations in plants caused by LN conditions, plays an important role in regulating the LN‐mediated rice resistance to BPH. Keywords:abscisic acid,Nilaparvata lugens, nitrogen, plant defence, rice Nitrogen limitation enhances rice resistance to the brown planthopper by activating the ABA signalling pathway, thereby promoting the accumulation of cell wall constituents as well as flavonoid compounds, while concurrently reducing water content and free amino acid concentrations in leaf sheaths. To counteract herbivory, plants have evolved both constitutive and induced defence strategies (Wu and Baldwin2010; Santamaria et al.2013). Constitutive defences depend on preformed physical barriers (e.g., cuticle, waxes and silica deposits) and/or defence compounds that affect insect feeding, digestion and behaviour (Balakrishnan et al.2024; Dixon and Gschwend2024). In contrast, herbivore‐induced defences are activated upon plant perception of herbivore‐associated signals. This process involves early signalling events, such as calcium influx, reactive oxygen species bursts and activation of mitogen‐activated protein kinase cascades, which trigger a signalling network mediated by phytohormones including jasmonic acid (JA), salicylic acid (SA), ethylene and abscisic acid (ABA) (Ye et al.2021; Yamasaki et al.2021; Bungala et al.2024). This network reshapes the transcriptomic, proteomic and metabolic profiles of plants, thereby promoting the direct and indirect resistance of plants to herbivores (Yin et al.2021; Krishnan et al.2022; Zhang et al.2024a).

A new modification of the whole‐blood chemiluminescence assay has been developed and used to assess the impact of the tobacco‐smoking status on the phagocyte‐derived generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS). The strategy behind experiments consisted of maximizing the preservation of the whole‐blood composition in the experimental samples while removing erythrocytes that distort the results of chemiluminescence analysis. The removal of erythrocytes was achieved through their spontaneous sedimentation within 2–3 h. In this approach, only a nominal loss of leukocytes (1%–2% of the total amount), as a result of their co‐precipitation with red blood cells, takes place, which does not significantly affect the outcome of the chemiluminescence assay. Luminol was utilized as the appropriate chemiluminophore to monitor the ROS generation in the experimental blood samples. The developed experimental approach enables establishing the differences in the reactivity (priming) of circulating leukocytes in the blood of former smokers, individuals who never smoked, and active smokers, while using traditional and well‐studied (zymosan) phagocytosis inducers and new agonists such as particles of tobacco dust from regular cigarettes. Using tobacco smoking products as well as alternative nicotine delivery tools bears a variety of risks for a human organism, among which the impact on the development of oxidative stress in vivo is one of the major factors in the emergence of diverse pathologies [1,2,3]. Smoking refers to those types of stress influences that modify the functioning of the cells responsible for innate and acquired immunity, including monocytes and neutrophils circulating in the blood, tissue macrophages [4], and the production of proinflammatory cytokines [5]. The harm and risks to human health derived from smoking have been under intensive study for decades; nevertheless, there exists data exhibiting a decrease in the risk of some neurodegenerative and inflammatory diseases in smokers. This paradoxical effect of smoking may be accounted for by the ability of cigarette smoke to suppress the immune system, which in some cases may be beneficial [6]. Nicotine, being one of the most important components of tobacco smoke, can reduce immunity and inflammation through α‐7 nicotinic acetylcholine receptor (α7nAChR) activation [7,8].

## 摘要
A new modification of the whole-blood chemiluminescence assay has been developed and used to assess the impact of the tobacco-smoking status on the phagocyte-derived generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS). The strategy behind experiments consisted of maximizing the preservation of the whole-blood composition in the experimental samples while removing erythrocytes that distort the results of chemiluminescence analysis. The removal of erythrocytes was achieved through their spontaneous sedimentation within 2-3 h. In this approach, only a nominal loss of leukocytes (1%-2% of the total amount), as a result of their co-precipitation with red blood cells, takes place, which does not significantly affect the outcome of the chemiluminescence assay. Luminol was utilized as the appropriate chemiluminophore to monitor the ROS generation in the experimental blood samples. The developed experimental approach enables establishing the differences in the reactivity (priming) of circulating leukocytes in the blood of former smokers, individuals who never smoked, and active smokers, while using traditional and well-studied (zymosan) phagocytosis inducers and new agonists such as particles of tobacco dust from regular cigarettes.

# Biochemical mechanisms of layered double hydroxide mediated azole fungicide delivery for crop protection.

Azole fungicides inhibit the ergosterol biosynthetic enzyme CYP51, but their efficacy is limited by poor bioavailability, efflux pump mediated resistance, and short residual activity. This review looks at how layered double hydroxide (LDH) nanoparticles change the biochemical fate of azoles in plant pathogenic fungi. LDH encapsulated azoles enter fungal cells by endocytosis, bypassing plasma membrane ABC transporters. The mechanism is more nuanced than simple physical exclusion. At the molecular level, LDH encapsulation prevents efflux pump recognition through three complementary effects: (i) the intact LDH nanoparticle (80-120 nm diameter) cannot enter the narrow binding pocket of ABC transporters such as Cdr1 and BcatrB, which are designed to accommodate small lipophilic molecules; (ii) the positively charged LDH surface is electrostatically repelled by the negatively charged plasma membrane microdomains where efflux pumps cluster, reducing local nanoparticle concentration near transporter entry sites; and (iii) following endocytosis, azoles are released directly into the cytoplasm from acidifying endosomes, never traversing the plasma membrane as free drug where ABC transporters would encounter them. However, it should be noted that if free azole dissociates from LDH particles in the apoplast prior to endocytosis (e.g., due to ion exchange displacement by phosphate), that free fraction would remain susceptible to efflux pump recognition. Thus, LDH encapsulation substantially reduces but does not absolutely eliminate drug recognition by efflux pumps; the degree of bypass depends on formulation stability and the kinetics of ion exchange versus endocytosis. Instead, endosomal acidification (pH 5.0-5.5) triggers LDH dissolution and releases a concentrated azole bolus directly into the cytoplasm. This gives 2.0 to 2.6-fold lower EC₅₀ values against Fusarium graminearum, Botrytis cinerea and Colletotrichum gloeosporioides compared to free fungicide. Beyond CYP51 inhibition, LDH azole treatment causes secondary fungal stress responses including oxidative burst (more H₂O₂ and lipid peroxidation) and cell wall integrity pathway activation (upregulation of chs and fks genes). Sustained-release keeps CYP51 inhibited for 10-14 days (compared to 3-5 days for standard formulations), and stronger soil binding reduces leaching. On the plant side, LDH nanoparticles enter leaf cuticles through stomata and trichomes, with limited xylem movement but long retention in the apoplast, giving extended protection. Plant processing of LDH azoles involves cuticular wax dependent adhesion, detoxification by P450s (CYP71 and CYP81 families), and transient organellar responses in chloroplasts and mitochondria without lasting damage, plus weak induction of systemic acquired resistance (SAR). LDH delivery offers a biochemical way to overcome azole resistance and could reduce environmental load, but ecotoxicological and scale up gaps remain.

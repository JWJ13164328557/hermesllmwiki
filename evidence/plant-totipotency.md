# Plant Cells Retain Totipotency Throughout the Lifespan

## Claim

Unlike most animal somatic cells, plant somatic cells retain totipotency — the capacity to dedifferentiate and regenerate complete organs or entire organisms — throughout the plant's lifespan, enabling continuous growth and regeneration.

## Methodological Context

Totipotency is the ability of a single cell to give rise to all cell types of an organism. In animals, this is restricted to the zygote and early embryonic cells. In plants, somatic cells can be induced to form callus tissue (dedifferentiated cell mass), which can then regenerate shoots and roots, demonstrating that differentiated plant cells maintain developmental plasticity.

## Evidence Quality

**Tier 3** — Well-established biological principle with extensive experimental support

## Contradictory Evidence

None. Plant totipotency is a foundational principle of plant biology confirmed over decades of research. However, practical regeneration efficiency varies widely across species and cell types.

## Consensus Assessment

**Established** — Plant somatic cell totipotency is a core concept in plant developmental biology.

## Alternative Models

Some plant stem cells (e.g., root quiescent center) may maintain a less-differentiated state akin to animal stem cells, but the capacity for somatic cells to regain totipotency is unique to plants.

## Open Questions

- What are the molecular barriers that protect differentiated cell identity, and how are they overcome during regeneration?
- Why does regeneration efficiency vary so dramatically across plant species and genotypes?

## Next Critical Experiment

Single-cell multi-omics during callus induction to identify the epigenetic and transcriptional sequence of dedifferentiation at cell-type resolution.
